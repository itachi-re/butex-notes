## Section A: Fundamentals, Classification & Nomenclature

### Q1. Define the terms "monomer," "repeating unit," and "degree of polymerization." *(2 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

- **Monomer:** A small, low molecular weight molecule capable of undergoing polymerization to form a long-chain macromolecule (e.g., ethylene, CH₂=CH₂).
- **Repeating unit:** The smallest structural unit whose repetition constitutes the polymer chain. For polyethylene: $(-CH_2-CH_2-)_n$.
- **Degree of polymerization (DP):** The number of repeating units (n) present in a polymer chain. $DP = \dfrac{M}{M_0}$, where $M$ is the molecular weight of the polymer and $M_0$ is the molecular weight of the repeating unit.

**Exam Tip:** DP relates chain length to molecular weight — always specify whether it refers to the monomer or repeat unit when structural units differ (e.g., nylon-6,6).
</details>

### Q2. Differentiate between a homopolymer and a copolymer with examples. *(2 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Homopolymer | Copolymer |
|---|---|
| Formed from a single type of monomer | Formed from two or more different monomers |
| Repeat unit is uniform throughout | Repeat units vary along the chain |
| Example: Polyethylene $(-CH_2-CH_2-)_n$ | Example: SBR (styrene-butadiene rubber) |

**Exam Tip:** Copolymer properties are often intermediate between the homopolymers of the constituent monomers, and can be tuned by composition.
</details>

### Q3. Classify polymers on the basis of thermal behaviour, giving one example of each. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

1. **Thermoplastics:** Soften on heating and harden on cooling reversibly; linear/branched chains held by weak secondary forces. *Example: Polyethylene, PVC.*
2. **Thermosetting polymers (Thermosets):** Undergo irreversible cross-linking on heating/curing to form a rigid 3-D network; cannot be remelted. *Example: Bakelite, epoxy resin.*
3. **Elastomers:** Lightly cross-linked polymers that can be stretched to several times their length and recover their original shape. *Example: Natural rubber, SBR.*

**Exam Tip:** The key distinguishing feature is the degree and reversibility of cross-linking.
</details>

### Q4. Classify polymers based on the source of origin. *(2 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

- **Natural polymers:** Occur in nature. *Examples: Cellulose, natural rubber, proteins, starch, silk.*
- **Semi-synthetic polymers:** Chemically modified natural polymers. *Examples: Cellulose acetate, vulcanized rubber.*
- **Synthetic polymers:** Man-made from simple monomers. *Examples: Nylon, polyester, PVC, polystyrene.*
</details>

### Q5. Classify polymers based on the structure of the polymer chain. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

1. **Linear polymers:** Monomer units joined in a continuous, unbranched chain. *Example: HDPE.*
2. **Branched polymers:** Main chain with side chain branches attached. *Example: LDPE.*
3. **Cross-linked (network) polymers:** Chains joined by covalent bonds forming a 3-D network. *Example: Bakelite, vulcanized rubber.*

**Exam Tip:** Chain structure directly affects density, crystallinity, and mechanical strength — branching lowers crystallinity and density (as in LDPE vs HDPE).
</details>

### Q6. Define tacticity in polymers and describe its three types. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Tacticity** refers to the relative stereochemical arrangement of substituent groups (R) along the polymer backbone, applicable to vinyl-type polymers with a chiral backbone carbon.

1. **Isotactic:** All substituent groups lie on the same side of the chain (same configuration at every stereocenter). Highly crystalline, high melting point.
2. **Syndiotactic:** Substituent groups alternate regularly on either side of the chain. Moderately crystalline.
3. **Atactic:** Substituent groups are randomly arranged with no regular order. Amorphous, low softening point.

**Exam Tip:** Ziegler–Natta and metallocene catalysts allow stereo-controlled synthesis of isotactic/syndiotactic polymers (e.g., isotactic polypropylene), while free radical polymerization typically gives atactic products.
</details>

### Q7. Write a short note on functionality of a monomer and its role in polymerization. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Functionality** is the number of reactive/bonding sites (functional groups or double bonds) present in a monomer molecule.

- **Bifunctional monomers** (functionality = 2, e.g., ethylene glycol, adipic acid) give **linear polymers**.
- **Polyfunctional monomers** (functionality ≥ 3, e.g., glycerol, phthalic anhydride) introduce **branching/cross-linking**, leading to network (thermoset) polymers.

Functionality determines whether step-growth polymerization yields a linear chain or a 3-D cross-linked network, and controls the gel point in such reactions.
</details>

### Q8. What is meant by "degree of polymerization" and how does it influence polymer properties? *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

Degree of polymerization (DP) is the number of repeat units in a polymer chain, $DP = M/M_0$.

**Influence on properties:**
- Higher DP → higher molecular weight → increased chain entanglement → higher tensile strength, viscosity, and melting/softening point.
- Very low DP (oligomers) gives waxy, low-strength materials unsuitable for structural use.
- Mechanical strength typically plateaus beyond a threshold DP once sufficient entanglement is achieved.
</details>

## Section B: Addition (Chain-Growth) Polymerization

### Q9. Explain the mechanism of free radical addition polymerization with the four elementary steps. *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

Free radical polymerization proceeds through four stages:

**1. Initiation:**
An initiator (e.g., benzoyl peroxide) decomposes on heating/UV to give free radicals:
$$In-In \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2In^{\bullet}$$
The radical adds to a monomer:
$$In^{\bullet} + CH_2=CHX \rightarrow In-CH_2-\overset{\bullet}{C}HX$$

**2. Propagation:**
The chain radical adds successively to monomer units, extending the chain:
$$In-CH_2-\overset{\bullet}{C}HX + n\,CH_2=CHX \rightarrow In-(CH_2-CHX)_n-CH_2-\overset{\bullet}{C}HX$$

**3. Chain transfer (optional):**
The growing radical abstracts an atom from solvent/monomer/polymer, terminating one chain while generating a new radical elsewhere, lowering average molecular weight.

**4. Termination:**
Two radical chains combine or disproportionate:
- **Combination:** $R_1^{\bullet} + R_2^{\bullet} \rightarrow R_1-R_2$
- **Disproportionation:** one radical abstracts an H atom from another, giving one saturated and one unsaturated chain end.

**Exam Tip:** Rate of polymerization $R_p \propto [M][I]^{1/2}$ — always mention this kinetic expression for full marks.
</details>

### Q10. Differentiate between cationic and anionic addition polymerization. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Property | Cationic Polymerization | Anionic Polymerization |
|---|---|---|
| Active species | Carbocation | Carbanion |
| Suitable monomers | Electron-rich alkenes (isobutylene, vinyl ethers) | Electron-poor alkenes (acrylonitrile, styrene, MMA) |
| Catalyst/initiator | Lewis acids (BF₃, AlCl₃) with co-catalyst (H₂O) | Alkali metals, organolithiums (n-BuLi) |
| Termination | Chain transfer to monomer/counter-ion, common | Often absent — "living polymerization" possible |
| Example product | Butyl rubber | Living polystyrene, SBS block copolymers |

**Exam Tip:** Anionic polymerization's absence of natural termination is exploited industrially to make well-defined block copolymers.
</details>

### Q11. What is "living polymerization"? Explain its significance in polymer synthesis. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Living polymerization** is a chain-growth process in which chain termination and chain transfer are absent, so the propagating chain end remains active (a "living" carbanion, cation, or radical) as long as monomer is available.

**Significance:**
- Allows precise control of molecular weight (proportional to monomer/initiator ratio).
- Gives very narrow molecular weight distribution (PDI close to 1).
- Enables synthesis of well-defined **block copolymers** by sequential monomer addition, and end-functionalized polymers.

*Example: Anionic polymerization of styrene with n-BuLi in the absence of moisture/CO₂.*
</details>

### Q12. Explain the mechanism of Ziegler–Natta (coordination) polymerization and its significance. *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Ziegler–Natta catalysts** are combinations of a transition metal halide (e.g., TiCl₄) and an organometallic co-catalyst (e.g., Al(C₂H₅)₃), used for stereoregular polymerization of alkenes.

**Mechanism (Cossee–Arlman mechanism, simplified):**

1. **Alkylation:** The Al-alkyl co-catalyst alkylates the Ti center, generating a Ti–alkyl bond with a vacant coordination site.
2. **Coordination:** The alkene monomer coordinates to the vacant site on the Ti center via its π-electrons.
3. **Insertion:** The coordinated monomer inserts into the Ti–alkyl bond (migratory insertion), extending the chain by one unit and regenerating the vacant site.
4. Steps 2–3 repeat, growing the polymer chain in a stereo-controlled manner at the catalyst surface.

**Significance:**
- Produces **stereoregular** (isotactic/syndiotactic) polymers, e.g., isotactic polypropylene, HDPE.
- Operates at low pressure/temperature (unlike free-radical high-pressure LDPE process).
- Gives polymers with high crystallinity, density, and mechanical strength.

**Exam Tip:** Draw the Ti coordination site with the vacant orbital and monomer insertion arrow for full diagram marks.
</details>

### Q13. Compare free radical, cationic, anionic, and coordination polymerization mechanisms. *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Feature | Free Radical | Cationic | Anionic | Coordination |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Active center | Free radical | Carbocation | Carbanion | Metal–carbon bond |
| Typical monomers | Most vinyl monomers | Electron-rich alkenes | Electron-poor alkenes | Alkenes/dienes |
| Stereo control | Poor (atactic) | Poor | Moderate | Excellent (iso/syndiotactic) |
| Termination | Combination/disproportionation | Chain transfer | Often none (living) | Chain transfer/H₂ |
| Industrial example | LDPE, PVC, PS | Butyl rubber | SBS block rubber | HDPE, isotactic PP |

**Exam Tip:** Emphasize that coordination polymerization is unique in giving precise stereochemical control, which free radical mechanisms cannot achieve due to the planar, non-stereospecific radical center.
</details>

### Q14. Define chain transfer reaction. How does it affect the molecular weight of the polymer? *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Chain transfer** occurs when a growing radical (or ion) abstracts an atom from another molecule (solvent, monomer, initiator, or another polymer chain), terminating the original chain and generating a new active species that initiates a new chain.

**Effect on molecular weight:**
- Since one long-growing chain is prematurely terminated and a new chain starts, the **average degree of polymerization decreases**.
- Chain transfer to polymer (intramolecular or intermolecular) leads to **branching** in the product.
- The Mayo equation relates chain transfer constant to the reduction in DP: $\dfrac{1}{DP} = \dfrac{1}{DP_0} + C_s\dfrac{[S]}{[M]}$
</details>

### Q15. Explain inhibitors and retarders used in addition polymerization. *(2 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

- **Inhibitors:** Substances that completely stop polymerization by reacting with initiating/propagating radicals to form stable, non-reactive species (e.g., hydroquinone). Used to prevent premature polymerization during monomer storage/transport.
- **Retarders:** Substances that slow down (but do not completely stop) the rate of polymerization by competing with monomer for radicals, reducing the overall reaction rate.
</details>

## Section C: Condensation (Step-Growth) Polymerization

### Q16. Explain the mechanism of step-growth (condensation) polymerization with a suitable example. *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

In **step-growth polymerization**, bifunctional (or polyfunctional) monomers react stepwise, with each step usually accompanied by elimination of a small molecule (H₂O, HCl, etc.), and the polymer chain grows through monomer–monomer, monomer–oligomer, and oligomer–oligomer reactions.

**Example: Nylon-6,6 synthesis** from hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid:
$$nH_2N-(CH_2)_6-NH_2 + nHOOC-(CH_2)_4-COOH \rightarrow$$
$$[-NH-(CH_2)_6-NH-CO-(CH_2)_4-CO-]_n + 2nH_2O$$

**Key features:**
- Molecular weight builds up slowly and reaches high values only at high conversion (>98%).
- Unlike chain-growth, monomer disappears early but molecular weight rises gradually throughout the reaction.
- Requires exact stoichiometric balance of functional groups for high molecular weight.

**Exam Tip:** Emphasize the Carothers equation: $DP = \dfrac{1}{1-p}$, where $p$ is the extent of reaction.
</details>

### Q17. Differentiate between addition (chain-growth) and condensation (step-growth) polymerization. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Property | Addition (Chain-Growth) | Condensation (Step-Growth) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Monomers add to a growing active chain end | Any two molecules (monomer/oligomer) can react |
| By-product | None (no small molecule eliminated) | Small molecule (H₂O, HCl, etc.) usually eliminated |
| Molecular weight buildup | High MW achieved rapidly, even at low conversion | High MW achieved only at very high conversion |
| Monomer type | Usually contains a double bond | Usually bifunctional with reactive end groups |
| Example | Polyethylene, PVC | Nylon-6,6, polyester (PET) |
</details>

### Q18. State and explain the Carothers equation. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

The **Carothers equation** relates the average degree of polymerization (DP) to the extent of reaction (p) in step-growth polymerization:
$$\overline{DP} = \frac{1}{1-p}$$
where $p$ = fraction of functional groups reacted (extent of reaction).

**Significance:**
- Shows that high molecular weight polymer is obtained only when $p$ is very close to 1 (e.g., at $p = 0.99$, $DP = 100$).
- Explains why step-growth reactions require near-quantitative conversion and precise stoichiometry to achieve useful molecular weights.
</details>

### Q19. Explain the preparation, properties, and applications of polyesters (PET). *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Preparation:**
PET (polyethylene terephthalate) is prepared by transesterification/condensation of dimethyl terephthalate (or terephthalic acid) with ethylene glycol:
$$nHOOC-C_6H_4-COOH + nHOCH_2CH_2OH \rightarrow [-OC-C_6H_4-CO-O-CH_2CH_2-O-]_n + 2nH_2O$$

**Properties:**
- High tensile strength and stiffness
- Good chemical and moisture resistance
- High melting point (~260 °C), thermoplastic
- Transparent and can be highly crystalline (oriented) or amorphous depending on processing

**Applications:**
- Fibers (Terylene/Dacron), textile industry
- Bottles for beverages (blow-molded PET bottles)
- Packaging films, photographic film base
- Engineering plastic for automotive parts
</details>

### Q20. Explain the preparation, properties, and applications of Nylon-6 and Nylon-6,6. *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Nylon-6,6:**
- Prepared by condensation of hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid (see Q16).

**Nylon-6:**
- Prepared by ring-opening polymerization of caprolactam:
$$n(CH_2)_5CO(NH) \xrightarrow{\Delta} [-NH-(CH_2)_5-CO-]_n$$

**Properties (both):**
- High tensile strength, abrasion resistance, and elasticity
- Good resistance to chemicals and moisture (moderate water absorption)
- High melting point, semi-crystalline

**Applications:**
- Textile fibers, carpets, ropes
- Engineering plastic components — gears, bearings
- Tyre cord reinforcement

**Exam Tip:** Note that nylon-6 is made from a single monomer (ring-opening) while nylon-6,6 is made from two monomers (condensation) — a common comparison question.
</details>

### Q21. Explain the preparation and applications of polycarbonates. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Preparation:** Polycarbonate is prepared by the reaction of bisphenol-A with phosgene (COCl₂) via interfacial polycondensation:
$$nHO-C_6H_4-C(CH_3)_2-C_6H_4-OH + nCOCl_2 \rightarrow [-O-C_6H_4-C(CH_3)_2-C_6H_4-O-CO-]_n + 2nHCl$$

**Properties:** High impact strength and optical clarity, good heat resistance, dimensional stability.

**Applications:** Safety helmets, bullet-resistant windows, CDs/DVDs, eyewear lenses, automotive headlamp lenses.
</details>

### Q22. Explain the synthesis and cross-linking of phenol-formaldehyde resin (Bakelite). *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Synthesis:** Phenol reacts with formaldehyde under acidic or basic catalysis.

- **Under basic catalysis (excess formaldehyde):** forms **resole**, a low molecular weight pre-polymer with methylol (-CH₂OH) groups, which can self-cross-link on heating (single-stage resin).
- **Under acidic catalysis (excess phenol):** forms **novolac**, a linear, fusible pre-polymer requiring an external cross-linker (hexamethylenetetramine) for curing (two-stage resin).

**Cross-linking (curing):** On heating, methylol groups condense with phenol rings (or via formaldehyde released from hexamine) to form methylene (-CH₂-) bridges between phenol rings, building a rigid, infusible 3-D network:
$$\text{Ar-OH} + HOCH_2\text{-Ar} \rightarrow \text{Ar-CH}_2\text{-Ar} + H_2O$$

**Properties:** Hard, rigid, heat and chemical resistant, good electrical insulator, but brittle.

**Applications:** Electrical switches/insulators, laminates, adhesives, moulded articles.
</details>

### Q23. Write a short note on epoxy resins — preparation and curing. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Preparation:** Epoxy resins (e.g., DGEBA) are formed by the reaction of bisphenol-A with epichlorohydrin in the presence of NaOH, giving a pre-polymer chain terminated with reactive epoxide (oxirane) rings.

**Curing:** The pre-polymer is cross-linked ("cured") using hardeners such as polyamines or anhydrides, which react with the epoxide/hydroxyl groups to form a rigid thermoset network at room temperature or with mild heating.

**Properties & Applications:** Excellent adhesion, chemical resistance, and mechanical strength; used in adhesives, protective coatings, laminates, and composite materials (fiber-reinforced composites).
</details>

### Q24. Differentiate between resole and novolac types of phenolic resins. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Property | Resole | Novolac |
|---|---|---|
| Formaldehyde:Phenol ratio | Excess formaldehyde (>1:1) | Excess phenol (<1:1) |
| Catalyst | Basic (NaOH, NH₃) | Acidic (HCl, oxalic acid) |
| Curing | Single-stage — self-curing on heating | Two-stage — needs hexamine as curing agent |
| Methylol groups | Present, enable self-condensation | Absent |
</details>

## Section D: Copolymers & Copolymerization

### Q25. Classify copolymers based on the arrangement of monomer units, with examples. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

1. **Random copolymer:** Monomer units A and B arranged randomly: -A-B-B-A-A-B-A-. *Example: SBR.*
2. **Alternating copolymer:** A and B units alternate regularly: -A-B-A-B-A-B-. *Example: Styrene-maleic anhydride copolymer.*
3. **Block copolymer:** Long sequences ("blocks") of each monomer joined end to end: -AAAA-BBBB-. *Example: SBS (styrene-butadiene-styrene) thermoplastic elastomer.*
4. **Graft copolymer:** A main chain of one monomer with branches of a different monomer grafted onto it. *Example: High-impact polystyrene (HIPS, polystyrene backbone with polybutadiene grafts).*
</details>

### Q26. Explain the concept of reactivity ratios in copolymerization. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Reactivity ratio** ($r_1$, $r_2$) quantifies the relative preference of a growing radical for adding its own monomer versus the other monomer, defined for monomers M₁ and M₂ as:
$$r_1 = \frac{k_{11}}{k_{12}}, \quad r_2 = \frac{k_{22}}{k_{21}}$$

**Interpretation:**
- $r_1 > 1$: radical M₁• prefers adding M₁ (homopolymerization tendency).
- $r_1 < 1$: radical M₁• prefers adding M₂ (cross-propagation favored).
- $r_1 = r_2 = 1$: ideal random copolymerization.
- $r_1 = r_2 = 0$: strictly alternating copolymer.

Reactivity ratios are used with the copolymer composition equation to predict copolymer composition from monomer feed ratio.
</details>

### Q27. Discuss the significance of block and graft copolymers in industrial applications. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

- **Block copolymers** (e.g., SBS, SIS) combine properties of both blocks — hard glassy end-blocks (polystyrene) provide physical cross-links, while the rubbery mid-block (polybutadiene/polyisoprene) provides elasticity, giving **thermoplastic elastomers** that are processable like plastics yet behave like rubber, without vulcanization.
- **Graft copolymers** (e.g., HIPS, ABS) toughen brittle matrices — rubbery grafts (polybutadiene) dispersed in a rigid backbone (polystyrene/SAN) absorb impact energy, dramatically improving impact resistance while retaining processability of the rigid phase.

**Applications:** Footwear, adhesives, impact-modified plastics for automotive and appliance housings.
</details>

### Q28. Write a short note on the copolymer composition equation. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

The copolymer composition equation relates the instantaneous ratio of monomers incorporated into the copolymer $d[M_1]/d[M_2]$ to the monomer feed ratio $[M_1]/[M_2]$ and reactivity ratios $r_1, r_2$:
$$\frac{d[M_1]}{d[M_2]} = \frac{[M_1]}{[M_2]} \cdot \frac{r_1[M_1] + [M_2]}{[M_2]\,r_2 + [M_1]}$$

It allows prediction of copolymer composition at any instant during polymerization and is essential for controlling composition drift in batch copolymerization.
</details>

## Section E: Molecular Weight & Characterization

### Q29. Differentiate between number-average molecular weight (Mn) and weight-average molecular weight (Mw). *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Property | Number-Average (Mn) | Weight-Average (Mw) |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | $M_n = \dfrac{\sum N_iM_i}{\sum N_i}$ (based on number of molecules) | $M_w = \dfrac{\sum N_iM_i^2}{\sum N_iM_i}$ (based on weight fraction) |
| Sensitivity | More sensitive to smaller molecules | More sensitive to larger molecules |
| Determination method | Osmometry, end-group analysis, ebullioscopy | Light scattering, ultracentrifugation |
| Relative value | Always $M_n \leq M_w$ for a polydisperse sample | Always $\geq M_n$ |

**Exam Tip:** For a perfectly monodisperse polymer, $M_n = M_w$ (PDI = 1).
</details>

### Q30. Define polydispersity index (PDI). What does a high PDI indicate about a polymer sample? *(2 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**PDI (Polydispersity Index)** $= \dfrac{M_w}{M_n}$, a dimensionless measure of the breadth of the molecular weight distribution.

A **high PDI** (significantly greater than 1) indicates a broad distribution of chain lengths — i.e., a wide range of molecular weights present in the sample, typically resulting from uncontrolled (non-living) polymerization. A PDI close to 1 indicates a narrow, nearly uniform chain length distribution, as achieved in living polymerization.
</details>

### Q31. Explain Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC) for molecular weight determination. *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Principle:** GPC (also called Size Exclusion Chromatography, SEC) separates polymer molecules according to their **hydrodynamic size** in solution as they pass through a column packed with porous gel beads.

**Working:**
1. A dilute polymer solution is injected into the column.
2. Larger molecules cannot penetrate the small pores of the gel and elute first (shorter retention time).
3. Smaller molecules enter the pores, travel a longer path, and elute later.
4. A detector (refractive index or UV) measures polymer concentration as a function of elution volume/time.
5. The elution profile is converted to a molecular weight distribution using calibration against standards (usually polystyrene) of known molecular weight.

**Output:** Provides $M_n$, $M_w$, and PDI directly from a single chromatogram.

**Exam Tip:** Mention that GPC gives the *entire* molecular weight distribution curve, unlike osmometry or light scattering which give only a single average value.
</details>

### Q32. Explain the principle of membrane osmometry for determining Mn. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Principle:** A semi-permeable membrane (permeable to solvent, not to polymer) separates a polymer solution from pure solvent. Solvent flows into the solution side by osmosis until the osmotic pressure balances the flow, and the resulting pressure difference is measured.

**Relation:** For dilute solutions, osmotic pressure $\pi$ is related to concentration $c$ and $M_n$ by:
$$\frac{\pi}{c} = RT\left(\frac{1}{M_n} + A_2c + ...\right)$$
Plotting $\pi/c$ vs $c$ and extrapolating to $c \to 0$ gives $1/M_n$ from the intercept (via $RT/M_n$).

**Note:** Osmometry gives **number-average** molecular weight since osmotic pressure depends on the number of solute particles, and is suitable for the range 20,000–500,000 g/mol.
</details>

### Q33. Explain the viscometry method for determination of molecular weight. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Principle:** The intrinsic viscosity $[\eta]$ of a dilute polymer solution is related to its (viscosity-average) molecular weight through the **Mark–Houwink equation**:
$$[\eta] = KM_v^{a}$$
where $K$ and $a$ are constants specific to the polymer-solvent-temperature system (typically $0.5 \leq a \leq 0.8$).

**Procedure:**
1. Measure flow times of solvent and polymer solutions of various dilute concentrations using an Ubbelohde viscometer.
2. Calculate relative, specific, and reduced viscosities at each concentration.
3. Extrapolate reduced viscosity ($\eta_{sp}/c$) to zero concentration to obtain intrinsic viscosity $[\eta]$ (Huggins plot).
4. Use $[\eta]$ in the Mark–Houwink equation with known $K$, $a$ to calculate $M_v$.

**Exam Tip:** Viscometry is simple and rapid but gives only an *average* molecular weight ($M_v$), not the full distribution.
</details>

### Q34. Write short notes on end-group analysis for molecular weight determination. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**End-group analysis** determines $M_n$ by chemically quantifying the number of reactive/known end groups per polymer chain (e.g., -COOH or -NH₂ end groups in condensation polymers via titration).

$$M_n = \frac{\text{weight of polymer} \times \text{no. of end groups per chain}}{\text{moles of end groups determined}}$$

**Limitation:** Only applicable to polymers with a known, quantifiable functional end group (mainly step-growth/condensation polymers); becomes inaccurate at very high molecular weights since end-group concentration becomes too low to detect accurately.
</details>

### Q35. Compare the various methods available for molecular weight determination of polymers. *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Method | Average Obtained | MW Range | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| End-group analysis | $M_n$ | Low (< 25,000) | Chemical titration of end groups |
| Membrane osmometry | $M_n$ | 20,000–500,000 | Osmotic pressure |
| Ebullioscopy/Cryoscopy | $M_n$ | Low | Boiling/freezing point elevation/depression |
| Light scattering | $M_w$ | Wide (up to 10⁷) | Intensity of scattered light |
| Viscometry | $M_v$ | Wide | Intrinsic viscosity (Mark–Houwink) |
| GPC/SEC | $M_n$, $M_w$, full distribution | Wide | Size-based column separation |
| Ultracentrifugation | $M_w$ or $M_z$ | Wide | Sedimentation rate |

**Exam Tip:** Highlight that GPC is the only technique giving the complete molecular weight distribution (and hence PDI) in one experiment.
</details>

## Section F: Structure–Property Relationships & Thermal Behaviour

### Q36. Define glass transition temperature (Tg) and melting temperature (Tm). Differentiate between them. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

- **Glass transition temperature (Tg):** The temperature at which an amorphous polymer (or the amorphous regions of a semi-crystalline polymer) changes from a hard, glassy state to a soft, rubbery state, due to onset of long-range segmental chain mobility. It is not a true phase transition (second-order transition).
- **Melting temperature (Tm):** The temperature at which the crystalline regions of a polymer melt into a disordered, amorphous liquid state. It is a true first-order phase transition, applicable only to crystalline/semi-crystalline polymers.

| Property | Tg | Tm |
|---|---|---|
| Applies to | Amorphous region | Crystalline region |
| Transition order | Second-order (no latent heat) | First-order (latent heat absorbed) |
| Polymers exhibiting | All polymers | Only crystalline/semi-crystalline polymers |
</details>

### Q37. Discuss the factors affecting the glass transition temperature (Tg) of a polymer. *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

1. **Chain flexibility:** Flexible backbones (e.g., -Si-O- in silicones) lower Tg; rigid backbones (aromatic rings) raise Tg.
2. **Intermolecular forces:** Strong forces (H-bonding, polar groups) restrict segmental motion, raising Tg (e.g., nylon has higher Tg than polyethylene).
3. **Molecular weight:** Tg increases with molecular weight up to a limiting value, beyond which it becomes essentially independent of further MW increase (fewer chain ends acting as free volume).
4. **Branching:** Branching generally increases free volume, tending to lower Tg; but bulky, rigid branches can raise it.
5. **Cross-linking:** Increased cross-link density restricts chain mobility and raises Tg.
6. **Plasticizers:** Small molecules added between chains increase free volume and chain mobility, lowering Tg (e.g., plasticized PVC).
7. **Side group bulkiness/stiffness:** Bulky, rigid side groups (e.g., phenyl in polystyrene) hinder rotation, raising Tg.

**Exam Tip:** A comparison example — polystyrene (bulky phenyl group, Tg ≈ 100°C) vs polyethylene (flexible chain, Tg ≈ -120°C) illustrates several of these factors at once.
</details>

### Q38. Explain crystallinity in polymers and the factors that favor it. *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Crystallinity** refers to the degree of ordered, regularly packed chain segments (crystallites) within a polymer, coexisting with disordered amorphous regions — polymers are typically **semi-crystalline** rather than 100% crystalline.

**Factors favoring crystallinity:**
1. **Structural regularity:** Linear chains with regular repeat units favor close, ordered packing (e.g., HDPE vs branched LDPE).
2. **Stereoregularity (tacticity):** Isotactic/syndiotactic polymers crystallize readily; atactic polymers are generally amorphous.
3. **Absence of bulky side groups:** Small, uniform side groups allow closer chain packing.
4. **Strong intermolecular forces:** H-bonding (as in nylon) promotes ordered packing.
5. **Slow cooling/annealing:** Allows chains sufficient time to organize into ordered lamellae.

**Effect on properties:** Higher crystallinity increases density, tensile strength, rigidity, and chemical resistance, but reduces transparency (due to light scattering at crystallite boundaries) and impact toughness.
</details>

### Q39. Explain the technique of Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) and its applications in polymer characterization. *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Principle:** DSC measures the difference in heat flow required to maintain a sample and an inert reference at the same temperature, as both are subjected to a controlled temperature program.

**Working:** As the polymer sample is heated, transitions are recorded as changes in heat flow:
- **Glass transition (Tg):** appears as a step change in baseline (no latent heat, only change in heat capacity).
- **Crystallization:** appears as an exothermic peak (heat released as chains order).
- **Melting (Tm):** appears as an endothermic peak (heat absorbed to disrupt crystallites).

**Applications:**
- Determination of Tg, Tm, and crystallization temperature.
- Calculation of percent crystallinity from heat of fusion.
- Studying curing behavior of thermosets and oxidative stability.
- Quality control/comparison of polymer batches.
</details>

### Q40. Explain Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) and its significance in polymer science. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Principle:** TGA measures the change in weight (mass) of a polymer sample as a function of temperature (or time) under a controlled atmosphere (inert or oxidative).

**Working:** As temperature increases, the sample loses weight due to evaporation of volatiles/moisture, then due to thermal decomposition of the polymer backbone; the resulting weight-loss curve (thermogram) shows characteristic decomposition steps and onset/completion temperatures.

**Significance/Applications:**
- Determination of thermal stability and decomposition temperature.
- Estimation of filler/additive content (residual weight after polymer decomposes).
- Comparing thermal degradation behavior of different polymer formulations.
- Kinetic studies of decomposition mechanisms.
</details>

### Q41. Discuss the effect of crystallinity on the mechanical and optical properties of polymers. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Mechanical properties:**
- Increased crystallinity raises tensile strength, stiffness (modulus), hardness, and density due to tighter chain packing and stronger interchain forces within crystallites.
- However, it typically reduces elongation at break and impact toughness, making the polymer more brittle.

**Optical properties:**
- Highly crystalline polymers are generally **translucent/opaque** because crystallites (of size comparable to visible light wavelength) scatter light at crystalline-amorphous boundaries.
- Amorphous polymers (e.g., atactic polystyrene, PMMA) are typically **transparent** since there are no crystallite boundaries to scatter light.

*Example: LDPE (lower crystallinity) is more transparent and flexible than HDPE (higher crystallinity), which is stiffer and more opaque.*
</details>

## Section G: Mechanical Properties, Elastomers & Rubber

### Q42. Explain the stress–strain behaviour of a typical thermoplastic polymer with a neat diagram. *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

A typical stress–strain curve for a ductile thermoplastic shows:

1. **Elastic region:** Initial linear portion — stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law); the polymer returns to its original shape if unloaded here. The slope gives **Young's modulus**.
2. **Yield point:** The point at which the material begins to deform plastically (permanent deformation begins); marks the **yield stress**.
3. **Plastic (drawing) region:** Beyond yield, chains slide/align under stress with relatively little increase in stress for large increase in strain (necking may occur).
4. **Strain hardening:** As chains become aligned along the draw direction, stress rises again before failure.
5. **Fracture point:** The point at which the sample breaks, corresponding to the **ultimate tensile strength** and elongation at break.

```
Stress
  |            ___strain hardening
  |           /    \
  |          /      \___ fracture
  |    yield/
  |    point/
  |    ____/
  |   /
  |  / elastic region
  |_/________________________ Strain
```

**Exam Tip:** Brittle polymers (e.g., polystyrene) fracture soon after the elastic region without a distinct yield/plastic region; ductile polymers (e.g., LDPE) show pronounced yielding and drawing.
</details>

### Q43. What is vulcanization of rubber? Explain its mechanism and effect on properties. *(6 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Vulcanization** is the process of cross-linking natural/synthetic rubber chains, typically using sulfur (with accelerators and activators), by heating the rubber-sulfur mixture.

**Mechanism:**
1. Sulfur, in the presence of accelerators (e.g., zinc oxide, stearic acid, mercaptobenzothiazole), forms reactive sulfurating species on heating.
2. These species abstract allylic hydrogen atoms from the polyisoprene chain at the double bonds.
3. Sulfur atoms/chains (mono-, di-, or polysulfide bridges) form cross-links between adjacent rubber chains, converting the linear, tacky raw rubber into a 3-D cross-linked elastic network.

$$\text{Raw rubber (linear chains)} + S_8 \xrightarrow{\text{heat, accelerator}} \text{cross-linked (vulcanized) rubber}$$

**Effect on properties:**
- Raw rubber: soft, tacky, low elasticity, poor temperature stability, low tensile strength.
- Vulcanized rubber: significantly improved tensile strength, elasticity (rapid recovery), resilience, and resistance to heat, oxidation, and solvents.
- Degree of cross-linking (sulfur content) controls hardness: low sulfur → soft rubber (tyres); high sulfur (~30%) → hard rubber (ebonite).

**Exam Tip:** Emphasize that vulcanization converts weak Van der Waals interactions between chains into permanent covalent cross-links, which is why vulcanized rubber cannot be remolded (behaves as thermoset).
</details>

### Q44. Explain the concept of elasticity in rubber based on its molecular structure. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

Natural rubber consists of long, flexible, coiled polyisoprene chains held together (after vulcanization) by occasional sulfur cross-links.

**Origin of elasticity:**
- In the unstretched state, chains exist in a highly disordered, coiled (high entropy) conformation.
- On stretching, chains straighten and align along the stretch direction, decreasing configurational entropy (a thermodynamically unfavorable, low-entropy state).
- On releasing the applied stress, thermal (Brownian) motion drives the chains to spontaneously recoil back to their original high-entropy, disordered coiled state, restoring the original shape — this is termed **entropic elasticity**.
- Cross-links (from vulcanization) prevent permanent slippage of chains past one another, ensuring full recovery rather than permanent (plastic) deformation.
</details>

### Q45. Compare thermoplastic elastomers (TPEs) with conventional vulcanized rubber. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Property | Vulcanized Rubber | Thermoplastic Elastomer (TPE) |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-linking | Permanent covalent (chemical) cross-links | Physical cross-links (glassy domains) — reversible |
| Processability | Cannot be remelted/reprocessed once cured | Can be melted, remolded, and recycled |
| Example | Vulcanized natural rubber | SBS (styrene-butadiene-styrene) block copolymer |
| Structure | Randomly cross-linked network | Block copolymer with hard glassy and soft rubbery domains |

**Exam Tip:** TPEs achieve rubber-like elasticity without chemical vulcanization because the hard polystyrene end-blocks physically aggregate into glassy domains that act as reversible (thermally reversible) cross-links.
</details>

### Q46. Discuss the important mechanical properties considered in the selection of polymers for engineering applications. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

1. **Tensile strength:** Maximum stress a polymer can withstand before breaking under tension.
2. **Modulus of elasticity (stiffness):** Resistance to elastic deformation under applied stress.
3. **Elongation at break:** Measure of ductility — how much the material can stretch before fracture.
4. **Impact strength:** Resistance to fracture under sudden, high-rate loading (measured by Izod/Charpy tests).
5. **Hardness:** Resistance to surface indentation/scratching.
6. **Fatigue resistance:** Ability to withstand repeated cyclic loading without failure.
7. **Creep resistance:** Resistance to slow, time-dependent deformation under sustained load.
</details>

## Section H: Major Industrial Polymers

### Q47. Explain the preparation, properties, and applications of polyvinyl chloride (PVC). *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Preparation:** PVC is prepared by free-radical addition polymerization of vinyl chloride monomer (CH₂=CHCl), typically via suspension or emulsion polymerization using a peroxide initiator:
$$n\,CH_2=CHCl \rightarrow (-CH_2-CHCl-)_n$$

**Properties:** Rigid, tough, and chemically resistant in its unplasticized form; can be made flexible by adding plasticizers (e.g., DOP); good flame resistance (due to chlorine content); relatively low cost.

**Applications:** Pipes and fittings, electrical cable insulation, flooring, flexible films/sheets (with plasticizers), window frames.
</details>

### Q48. Explain the preparation, properties, and applications of polystyrene (PS). *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Preparation:** Prepared by free-radical addition polymerization of styrene (C₆H₅-CH=CH₂), commonly by bulk or suspension polymerization:
$$n\,C_6H_5CH=CH_2 \rightarrow (-CH_2-CHC_6H_5-)_n$$

**Properties:** Rigid, transparent (amorphous, atactic), brittle, low impact strength, good electrical insulation, low cost.

**Applications:** Disposable cutlery/packaging, foamed polystyrene (thermal insulation, packaging), toys, CD cases, laboratory ware.
</details>

### Q49. Explain the preparation, properties, and applications of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE/Teflon). *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Preparation:** Prepared by free-radical polymerization of tetrafluoroethylene (CF₂=CF₂) under high pressure:
$$n\,CF_2=CF_2 \rightarrow (-CF_2-CF_2-)_n$$

**Properties:** Extremely chemically inert (resistant to nearly all chemicals), very low coefficient of friction (non-stick), excellent thermal stability and electrical insulation, high melting point.

**Applications:** Non-stick cookware coatings, chemical-resistant gaskets/seals, electrical insulation, bearings, laboratory equipment.
</details>

### Q50. Differentiate between LDPE and HDPE with respect to structure, properties, and preparation. *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Property | LDPE | HDPE |
|---|---|---|
| Method of preparation | Free-radical, high-pressure/temperature process | Ziegler-Natta/coordination catalysis, low pressure |
| Chain structure | Highly branched (short & long branches) | Predominantly linear, minimal branching |
| Crystallinity | Lower (~50–60%) | Higher (~80–95%) |
| Density | Lower (0.91–0.94 g/cm³) | Higher (0.94–0.97 g/cm³) |
| Mechanical strength | Lower, more flexible | Higher tensile strength, stiffer |
| Applications | Plastic bags, films, squeeze bottles | Pipes, bottles/crates, containers |

**Exam Tip:** The key structural distinction (branching vs linearity) explains all the downstream differences in crystallinity, density, and mechanical properties — always lead with this in your answer.
</details>

### Q51. Write a short note on polyurethanes — preparation and applications. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Preparation:** Polyurethanes are prepared by the step-growth reaction of a diisocyanate (e.g., toluene diisocyanate, TDI) with a polyol (diol/triol), forming characteristic urethane (-NH-CO-O-) linkages:
$$n\,O=C=N-R-N=C=O + n\,HO-R'-OH \rightarrow [-NH-CO-O-R'-O-CO-NH-R-]_n$$

**Properties:** Wide property range from rigid to highly flexible foams, tunable via choice of polyol chain length and degree of cross-linking; good abrasion resistance and toughness.

**Applications:** Flexible/rigid foams (mattresses, cushioning, insulation), elastomers (soles, wheels), coatings and adhesives, spandex/elastane fibers.
</details>

## Section I: Specialty & Functional Polymers

### Q52. Write a short note on biodegradable polymers, giving examples. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Biodegradable polymers** are polymers that can be broken down by microbial/enzymatic action into simpler, environmentally benign products (CO₂, water, biomass) under natural conditions, addressing the persistence problem of conventional plastics.

**Examples:**
1. **Polylactic acid (PLA):** Derived from renewable resources (corn starch); used in packaging, disposable cutlery, 3D printing filament.
2. **Polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB):** Produced by bacterial fermentation; used in medical sutures, packaging.
3. **Polycaprolactone (PCL):** Used in drug delivery and tissue engineering scaffolds.
4. **Starch-based polymers:** Blended with synthetic polymers for compostable packaging.

**Significance:** Reduce plastic waste accumulation and environmental pollution; important for sustainable packaging and biomedical applications.
</details>

### Q53. What are conducting polymers? Explain the mechanism of electrical conductivity in polyacetylene. *(5 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Conducting polymers** are organic polymers possessing a conjugated backbone (alternating single and double bonds) that, upon doping, can conduct electricity comparable to metals/semiconductors, while retaining polymer processability.

**Mechanism (polyacetylene example):**
- Polyacetylene $(-CH=CH-)_n$ has a fully conjugated backbone providing a continuous, delocalized π-electron system along the chain.
- In its pristine (undoped) form, it behaves as a semiconductor due to the alternating bond-length pattern (Peierls distortion) opening a band gap.
- **Doping** (oxidation with I₂/AsF₅, or reduction with Na/K) removes or adds electrons from/to the π-system, creating charge carriers (**polarons/solitons**) that can migrate along and between chains.
- This delocalized charge transport gives rise to high electrical conductivity, approaching metallic values upon heavy doping.

**Applications:** Organic LEDs (OLEDs), antistatic coatings, batteries, sensors, flexible electronics.
</details>

### Q54. Write a short note on liquid crystalline polymers (LCPs). *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Liquid crystalline polymers (LCPs)** are polymers whose rigid, rod-like segments (mesogens) align in an ordered manner in the melt or solution state, exhibiting properties intermediate between conventional crystalline solids and isotropic liquids.

**Key features:**
- High degree of molecular orientation gives exceptionally high tensile strength and modulus along the orientation direction.
- Low thermal expansion coefficient, excellent chemical and heat resistance.

**Examples & applications:** Kevlar (aramid fiber) — bulletproof vests, tire reinforcement; used in high-performance engineering plastics and electronic connectors requiring dimensional stability.
</details>

### Q55. Write a short note on polymer composites and their reinforcement mechanism. *(4 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Polymer composites** consist of a polymer matrix reinforced with a filler/fiber phase (glass fiber, carbon fiber, natural fiber) to enhance mechanical properties beyond what either component achieves alone.

**Reinforcement mechanism:**
- The reinforcing fiber (high strength/stiffness) bears the majority of the applied load.
- The polymer matrix transfers stress to the fibers via interfacial adhesion, protects fibers from environmental damage, and maintains the composite's shape.
- Effective stress transfer requires good fiber-matrix interfacial bonding (often improved via coupling agents/sizing on fiber surfaces).

**Applications:** Fiber-reinforced plastics (FRP) in aerospace, automotive body panels, wind turbine blades, sporting goods (e.g., carbon-fiber composites).
</details>

### Q56. Differentiate between thermosetting and thermoplastic composites. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

| Property | Thermoplastic Composite | Thermoset Composite |
|---|---|---|
| Matrix behavior on heating | Softens/melts reversibly | Cures irreversibly (cross-links), cannot remelt |
| Reprocessing/recycling | Possible by remelting | Difficult — generally not remeltable |
| Typical matrix examples | Polypropylene, nylon | Epoxy, unsaturated polyester |
| Processing | Faster cycle times (injection/compression molding) | Requires curing time (autoclave/oven cure) |
</details>

### Q57. Explain the concept of polymer blends and their significance. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

**Polymer blends** are physical mixtures of two or more different polymers, combined (usually by melt mixing) without chemical bonding between them, to achieve a balance of properties not available in either component alone.

**Significance:**
- Cost-effective alternative to synthesizing a new polymer with tailored properties.
- Can combine desirable features — e.g., PC/ABS blends combine the toughness of ABS with the heat resistance and impact strength of polycarbonate.
- Compatibilizers are often added to improve miscibility/interfacial adhesion between immiscible blend components.
</details>

### Q58. Write a short note on the applications of polymers in drug delivery systems. *(3 marks)*
<details>
<summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

Biocompatible and biodegradable polymers are widely used to control the rate, timing, and site of drug release in the body:

- **Matrix/reservoir systems:** Drug is dispersed in or encapsulated by a polymer (e.g., PLGA, PCL) that degrades slowly, releasing drug over an extended period.
- **Hydrogels:** Cross-linked hydrophilic polymer networks (e.g., polyacrylamide, PEG-based) that swell in water and release drugs in response to pH/temperature stimuli.
- **Advantages:** Sustained/controlled release reduces dosing frequency, improves patient compliance, and can target specific sites (targeted drug delivery), minimizing side effects.
</details>
