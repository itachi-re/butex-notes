# Determination of the Electrochemical Equivalent (E.C.E.) of Copper

## Aim
To determine the electrochemical equivalent (E.C.E.) of copper using a copper voltameter.

## Apparatus / Equipment
- Copper voltameter (a glass/plastic cell with two copper plate electrodes) containing acidified copper sulphate solution (CuSO₄ + a little dilute H₂SO₄)
- Battery eliminator or battery (E)
- Rheostat (Rh)
- Ammeter (A)
- One-way key (K), stopwatch
- Analytical/physical balance, sandpaper, distilled water, alcohol/acetone, drying cloth or blotting paper

## Theory / Principle
When a steady current is passed through an electrolyte, chemical action occurs at the electrodes (electrolysis). **Faraday's first law of electrolysis** states that the mass of a substance liberated (or deposited) at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electric charge passed through the electrolyte:
$$
m \propto Q = It
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
m = ZIt
$$
where $Z$, the constant of proportionality, is called the **electrochemical equivalent (E.C.E.)** of the substance — the mass deposited per unit charge (or per ampere per second) passed.

In a copper voltameter, two copper plates are dipped in acidified copper sulphate solution and connected as the cathode (–) and anode (+). On passing current, copper ions ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$) from the solution are deposited on the cathode, while an equal amount of copper dissolves from the anode into the solution, keeping the electrolyte concentration essentially unchanged. The cathode is weighed before and after passing a known, steady current for a measured time, and the mass gained gives $m$.

## Formula / Working Equation
$$
Z = \frac{m}{It}
$$
where
- $m$ = mass of copper deposited on the cathode (kg)
- $I$ = steady current passed through the voltameter (A)
- $t$ = time for which the current is passed (s)
- $Z$ = electrochemical equivalent of copper (kg/C, i.e. kg per ampere-second)

## Experimental Setup
The copper voltameter is connected in series with the battery, rheostat, ammeter, and key, so that the same current flows through the voltameter and the ammeter. The cathode plate (on which deposition is to be measured) is carefully weighed before insertion and after the experiment.

## Procedure
1. Clean both copper plates with fine sandpaper, wash with distilled water, rinse with alcohol/acetone, dry thoroughly, and weigh the plate to be used as the cathode accurately on a physical/analytical balance. Record this as $m_1$.
2. Fix the cleaned, weighed plate as the cathode and the other plate as the anode in the voltameter containing acidified copper sulphate solution.
3. Connect the voltameter in series with the battery, rheostat, ammeter, and key, as shown in the circuit diagram.
4. Close the key and adjust the rheostat to pass a small, steady current (note the ammeter reading $I$); keep the current constant throughout the run by monitoring the ammeter.
5. Simultaneously start the stopwatch. Allow the current to flow for a measured, sufficiently long interval $t$ (typically 20–30 minutes) so that an appreciable, accurately weighable mass of copper is deposited.
6. At the end of the time interval, open the key and stop the watch simultaneously.
7. Carefully remove the cathode, rinse it gently in distilled water (without rubbing off the deposited copper), then rinse with alcohol/acetone, dry, and weigh it again. Record this as $m_2$.
8. Compute the mass deposited $m = m_2 - m_1$.
9. Repeat the experiment for two or three different values of current and/or time to obtain independent estimates of $Z$.

**Precautions**
- Clean and dry the cathode thoroughly before the first weighing; any grease, oxide layer, or moisture affects the mass measurement.
- Keep the current strictly constant during the run; note the ammeter reading frequently and adjust the rheostat if it drifts.
- Handle the cathode by its edges (or with clean forceps) after cleaning, to avoid depositing skin oils and altering its mass.
- While removing and rinsing the cathode after the experiment, do not wipe or rub its surface, as the freshly deposited copper is loosely adherent and can flake off.
- Dry the electrode gently (e.g., waving in air or with a hair dryer on low heat) rather than by rubbing.
- Ensure good, clean electrical contact between the electrodes and the connecting wires.
- Start and stop the stopwatch and the current simultaneously for an accurate value of $t$.

## Calculations
For each trial (with $m$ converted to kg and $t$ in seconds, if SI units are required):
$$
Z_i = \frac{m_i}{I_i t_i}
$$
Mean electrochemical equivalent:
$$
\bar{Z} = \frac{Z_1+Z_2+Z_3}{3}
$$

## Graph
For a series of trials at constant current $I$ but increasing deposition time $t$, a plot of $m$ (on the *y*-axis) against $t$ (on the *x*-axis) should give a straight line through the origin, with slope equal to $ZI$; from the measured slope and known $I$, $Z$ can also be obtained as $Z = \text{slope}/I$. This graph provides an additional check that $m \propto t$ at constant current, consistent with Faraday's first law.

## Result
The electrochemical equivalent of copper, as determined experimentally:
$$
Z = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\ \text{g/C} \;=\; \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\ \text{kg/C}
$$

## Precautions
- Weigh the cathode carefully before and after, avoiding contamination.
- Maintain a constant current throughout the experiment.
- Do not disturb the deposited copper while rinsing and drying the cathode after the run.
- Ensure simultaneous starting/stopping of the stopwatch and current.
- Keep all electrical connections clean and secure.
