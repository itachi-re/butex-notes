## 1. Introduction to Colour, Dyes and Pigments

**Colour** is the visual sensation produced when the human eye and brain interpret light of a particular composition of wavelengths reaching the retina. Physically, colour arises because an object selectively **absorbs** certain wavelengths of visible light and **transmits, reflects, or scatters** the remainder; the eye perceives the wavelengths that are *not* absorbed (strictly, the complementary combination of the reflected/transmitted light).

A **colourant** is a general term for any substance — dye, pigment, or other coloured material — that imparts colour to a substrate. "Colourant" is the umbrella term; **dye** and **pigment** are the two principal sub-classes, distinguished chiefly by solubility and mode of application.

- **Dye**: A coloured, typically ionic or polar organic compound that is **soluble** (or can be brought into solution/dispersion) in the application medium, has **substantivity** (affinity) for a particular substrate (textile fibre, paper, leather, hair, food, biological tissue, etc.), and becomes fixed within or onto that substrate, usually by absorption, ionic attraction, hydrogen bonding, or covalent bond formation.
- **Pigment**: A coloured, **insoluble** particulate solid that is mechanically dispersed (not dissolved) in a vehicle or binding medium (paint, ink, plastic, resin) and imparts colour by remaining as discrete particles embedded in that medium, contributing colour largely through light scattering and absorption at the particle surface.

**Why substances appear coloured:** A substance appears coloured when it absorbs electromagnetic radiation from the visible region (approximately 400–700 nm) and this absorption is due to promotion of electrons between molecular electronic energy levels whose energy gap corresponds to visible-light photon energies. If a compound absorbs no visible light, it is colourless (white/transparent); if it absorbs all visible wavelengths roughly equally, it appears black or grey.

**Relationship between molecular structure and observed colour:** Colour is fundamentally an *electronic* property. It depends on how easily electrons in a molecule can be excited from a lower-energy (ground) state to a higher-energy (excited) state by absorbing a visible-light photon. Extended conjugation, the presence of specific unsaturated groups (chromophores), and electron-donating/withdrawing substituents (auxochromes) all modify the electronic energy-level spacing and hence the wavelength of light absorbed. This structure–colour relationship is the central theme of Part I and Part II of this guide.

# Part I — Colour

## 2. Fundamentals of Colour

### 2.1 Electromagnetic Radiation and the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Light is a form of **electromagnetic radiation** — a transverse wave of oscillating electric and magnetic fields that also has particle character (the **photon**). The electromagnetic spectrum extends from very short-wavelength, high-energy radiation (gamma rays, X-rays) through ultraviolet, the **visible region**, infrared, microwaves, and radio waves.

The **visible region** is the narrow band of the spectrum, roughly **400–700 nm**, that the human eye can detect. Radiation of shorter wavelength (200–400 nm) is **ultraviolet (UV)**; radiation of longer wavelength (>700 nm) is **infrared (IR)**.

### 2.2 Wavelength, Frequency, and Energy

- **Wavelength (λ)**: distance between successive crests of the wave, usually expressed in nanometres (nm) for visible light.
- **Frequency (ν)**: number of wave cycles passing a point per second, expressed in hertz (Hz, s⁻¹).
- **Energy (E)**: energy carried by a photon of the radiation.

These are related by:

$$c = \lambda \nu$$

$$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

where *c* is the speed of light (2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹) and *h* is Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s).

**Key consequence:** Energy is *inversely proportional* to wavelength. Short-wavelength (violet, blue) light is more energetic per photon than long-wavelength (red) light. This inverse relationship is the reason that increasing conjugation (which *lowers* the electronic energy gap) shifts absorption to *longer* wavelengths — a bathochromic shift (Section 4.2, Section 5).

### 2.3 Why Absorption of Particular Wavelengths Produces Colour

When white light (containing all visible wavelengths) strikes a coloured substance, the substance absorbs photons whose energy matches the gap between its ground-state and an accessible excited electronic state. The wavelengths *not* absorbed are transmitted or reflected and reach the eye; the brain interprets this remaining mixture of wavelengths as a **colour** that is complementary to the colour absorbed. For example, a substance that absorbs strongly around 600–620 nm (orange-red) appears blue-green to the eye, because blue-green is the complementary colour to what was removed from white light.

### 2.4 Approximate Visible Wavelengths and Perceived Colours

| Wavelength Absorbed (nm) | Colour Absorbed | Colour Observed (Complementary) |
|---|---|---|
| 400–430 | Violet | Yellow-green |
| 430–490 | Blue | Orange |
| 490–510 | Blue-green | Red |

It is a simplification — the observed colour depends on the entire absorption curve, not a single wavelength — but it is the standard first-approximation tool taught at undergraduate level.

## 3. Why Molecules Have Colour

### 3.1 Electronic Transitions and Absorption

Colour in organic molecules originates from **electronic transitions**: the promotion of an electron from an occupied molecular orbital (the **ground state**) to an unoccupied, higher-energy molecular orbital (an **excited state**) upon absorption of a photon of matching energy.

- **HOMO** (Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital): the highest-energy orbital that contains electrons in the ground state.
- **LUMO** (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital): the lowest-energy orbital that is empty in the ground state.
- The **energy gap (ΔE)** between HOMO and LUMO (or, more generally, between the ground and relevant excited electronic states) determines the wavelength of light that can be absorbed, via $E = hc/\lambda$.

A **small** HOMO–LUMO gap corresponds to absorption of **low-energy, long-wavelength** photons; a **large** gap corresponds to absorption of **high-energy, short-wavelength** photons.

### 3.2 UV-Visible Absorption Spectroscopy

The technique used to measure these transitions experimentally is **UV-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectroscopy**, which records absorbance versus wavelength and reports the **absorption maximum, λmax** — the wavelength at which absorption is strongest. A compound is visibly coloured only if it has significant absorption with λmax falling within, or extending into, the 400–700 nm visible window (and even then, sufficient molar absorptivity, ε, is needed for the colour to be perceptible).

### 3.3 Why Most Simple Organic Molecules Are Colourless

Simple saturated compounds (alkanes, alcohols) have only σ and non-bonding electrons with very large HOMO–LUMO gaps; their σ→σ* transitions require energies corresponding to **vacuum-UV** wavelengths (well below 200 nm), far outside the visible range. Simple isolated alkenes and carbonyl compounds undergo π→π* and n→π* transitions, but these typically absorb in the **UV** (below ~350 nm for an isolated chromophore) — again outside the visible range. Hence, most simple organic molecules are colourless to the human eye even though they do absorb electromagnetic radiation (in the UV).

### 3.4 Why Extended Conjugation Shifts Absorption into the Visible Region

When double bonds are **conjugated** (alternating with single bonds so that p-orbitals overlap continuously), the π electrons become **delocalised** over the whole conjugated system rather than confined to a single bond. Delocalisation causes the discrete π and π* molecular orbitals of individual double bonds to combine and split into a larger set of closely spaced molecular orbitals. As the number of conjugated double bonds increases:

- The energy gap between the resulting HOMO and LUMO **decreases** (the orbitals become more closely spaced).
- The wavelength required to bridge that gap **increases** (red-shifts), following $E = hc/\lambda$.

This is summarised by the core relationship used throughout this guide:

> **increasing conjugation → decreasing HOMO–LUMO energy gap → longer wavelength of absorption → possible shift of λmax into the visible region → observable colour**

This is not a loose analogy but a direct consequence of molecular-orbital theory (or, equivalently, of the particle-in-a-box model, in which a longer conjugated "box" gives more closely spaced energy levels). It is chemically accurate and forms the theoretical basis for both the classical chromophore/auxochrome theory and the modern electronic theory of colour discussed next.

## 4. Theories of Colour

Several frameworks have been used historically and are still taught to rationalise the colour of organic compounds. They range from the classical, empirical "chromophore–auxochrome" scheme (developed before modern quantum theory) to the modern molecular-orbital treatment. Undergraduate courses typically present all of them because the classical vocabulary (chromophore, auxochrome, bathochromic shift) remains the standard working language of dye chemistry, even though the *explanation* for why these groups behave as they do is now understood in electronic/MO terms.

### 4.1 Chromophore Theory

**Chromophore** (from Greek *chroma* = colour, *phoros* = bearer): an unsaturated functional group capable of absorbing radiation, thereby giving rise to (or contributing to) colour in an organic molecule. The chromophore theory, proposed originally by Otto Witt (1876), held that colour in organic compounds arises from the presence of one or more such unsaturated groups.

**Common chromophoric groups:**

| Chromophore | Group | Example Compound |
|---|---|---|
| Azo | –N=N– | Azobenzene |
| Nitro | –NO₂ (as –N(=O)=O, conjugated with ring) | Nitrobenzene (pale yellow), picric acid |
| Nitroso | –N=O | Nitrosobenzene |
| Carbonyl | C=O | Quinones, anthraquinone |
| Ethylenic | –C=C– | Polyenes such as carotenoids |
| Quinonoid | Six-membered ring with two exocyclic double bonds (cross-conjugated diene system) | *p*-benzoquinone |
| Aromatic conjugated ring systems | Extended π systems | Polycyclic aromatics, phthalocyanines |

> **Important qualification:** The presence of a chromophore is **necessary but not always sufficient** for intense visible colour. A single isolated chromophore (e.g., one –N=N– group in a very small, non-conjugated environment, or an isolated C=O in acetone) may absorb only in the UV. Whether a molecule is *actually* visibly coloured depends on the **overall extent of conjugation, ring fusion, and auxochromic substitution**, not merely on the presence of a named chromophore in isolation. This is why azobenzene itself is only pale yellow/orange, while extensively substituted azo dyes are vivid reds, blues, or blacks.

### 4.2 Auxochrome Theory

**Auxochrome** (Greek: "colour enhancer"): a saturated functional group that, when attached to a chromophore-bearing molecule, does not itself produce colour but **modifies** the colour and/or intensity produced by the chromophore, typically by donating or withdrawing electron density into the conjugated system through resonance.

**Common auxochromes:**

| Auxochrome | Character | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| –OH (hydroxyl) | Electron-donating (lone pair conjugation) | Deepens colour, aids solubility (as phenolate) |
| –NH₂ (amino) | Strongly electron-donating | Strong bathochromic shift |
| –NHR (secondary amino) | Electron-donating | Bathochromic shift |
| –NR₂ (tertiary amino) | Strongly electron-donating | Strong bathochromic shift |
| –COOH (carboxylic acid) | Weakly electron-withdrawing / solubilising (as carboxylate) | Improves water solubility; minor shift |
| –SO₃H (sulfonic acid) | Solubilising (strongly ionisable) | Provides water solubility, minimal effect on hue; essential for dye–fibre affinity |

**Effects of auxochromes and structural changes on absorption — key vocabulary:**

- **Bathochromic shift ("red shift"):** shift of λmax to a **longer** wavelength (lower energy). Caused by increased conjugation or strong electron-donating auxochromes.
- **Hypsochromic shift ("blue shift"):** shift of λmax to a **shorter** wavelength (higher energy). Caused by decreased conjugation, protonation of an electron-donating group, or removal of an auxochrome.
- **Hyperchromic effect:** an **increase** in the intensity (molar absorptivity, ε) of absorption.
- **Hypochromic effect:** a **decrease** in the intensity (molar absorptivity, ε) of absorption.

Auxochromes act by extending the conjugated π system through resonance donation (lone-pair-into-ring conjugation for –OH, –NH₂, –NR₂) or by withdrawing electron density (for –NO₂-type or –COOH-type groups acting in the opposite sense), thereby narrowing or widening the HOMO–LUMO gap and shifting λmax accordingly. Ionisable auxochromes such as –SO₃H and –COOH additionally confer water solubility, which is essential for dyes applied from aqueous solution, even though their electronic effect on hue is comparatively small.

### 4.3 Modern Electronic (Molecular-Orbital) Theory of Colour

The modern explanation reinterprets the classical chromophore/auxochrome vocabulary in terms of **molecular orbital theory**:

- A **chromophore** is, in modern terms, a group containing π electrons (or non-bonding electrons on a heteroatom conjugated with π electrons) that gives rise to **π→π*** or **n→π*** transitions of relatively low energy.
    - **π→π\*** transitions: promotion of an electron from a bonding π orbital to an antibonding π* orbital; generally high intensity (high ε), and the wavelength decreases as conjugation increases.
    - **n→π\*** transitions: promotion of a non-bonding (lone-pair) electron on a heteroatom (e.g., the oxygen of C=O) to an antibonding π* orbital; generally low intensity, since the transition is symmetry-forbidden.
- An **auxochrome** is, in modern terms, a substituent whose lone pair can conjugate with (donate into) the π system, **raising the energy of the HOMO** more than the LUMO (or, for electron-withdrawing auxochromes, lowering the LUMO more than the HOMO), thereby **narrowing the HOMO–LUMO gap** and producing a bathochromic shift.
- **Conjugation, delocalisation, and resonance** are the underlying structural phenomena: as the conjugated π system becomes longer and more delocalised, the molecular orbitals derived from combining individual p-orbitals split into a denser ladder of energy levels, narrowing the HOMO–LUMO gap (Section 3.4).

**Distinction from classical theory:** The classical chromophore/auxochrome scheme is *descriptive and empirical* — it classifies groups by observed effect on colour without explaining *why* at the electronic level. The modern electronic theory is *mechanistic* — it explains those same empirical observations using quantum mechanical concepts (molecular orbitals, transition probabilities, conjugation-induced level splitting). Undergraduate dye chemistry retains the classical terms because they remain a convenient, structure-based shorthand, but the modern theory is now understood to be the correct underlying physical explanation.

### 4.4 Quinonoid Theory

The **quinonoid theory** (associated with the historical development of triphenylmethane and related dye chemistry) proposed that colour in many synthetic dyes arises specifically from the presence of a **quinonoid ring structure** — a six-membered ring bearing two exocyclic double bonds in a cross-conjugated arrangement (as in *p*-benzoquinone), in contrast to the normal aromatic (**benzenoid**) ring, which has three alternating ring double bonds and is comparatively colourless.

- **Benzenoid form:** the ordinary aromatic (Kekulé) structure of benzene and its simple derivatives; generally colourless or only weakly coloured.
- **Quinonoid form:** a resonance/tautomeric structure in which the ring loses full aromatic character in favour of two exocyclic C=X (X = O, N, or C) double bonds; this cross-conjugated arrangement was found empirically to correlate with intense colour in many triarylmethane and related dyes.

**Representative example:** Malachite Green and related triphenylmethane dyes can be drawn with one ring in the quinonoid form (a cyclohexadiene ring with an exocyclic C=N⁺R₂ double bond) in their coloured, cationic form; the corresponding "leuco" (colourless, reduced) form has all three rings in the ordinary benzenoid arrangement with a central sp³ carbon, interrupting conjugation entirely.

**Limitations:** The quinonoid theory is a **structural correlation observed for a specific historical class of dyes** (triarylmethanes and some azo/hydrazone tautomers); it is not a general theory of colour and does not by itself explain absorption in, e.g., simple polyenes or anthraquinones lacking an exocyclic quinonoid pattern. It is best understood today as an early, partially correct special case that is fully subsumed by the modern electronic (MO) theory: the quinonoid resonance form simply represents one specific type of extended, cross-conjugated π system with a narrowed HOMO–LUMO gap.

# Part II — Colour and Conjugated Systems

## 5. Colour and Conjugation

### 5.1 What Is Conjugation?

**Conjugation** is the arrangement of alternating single and multiple (double or triple) bonds in a molecule such that adjacent p-orbitals on consecutive atoms can overlap sideways, allowing π electrons to be **delocalised** over more than two atoms rather than being confined to one discrete double bond. Conjugation is typically depicted using **resonance structures**, which show the π-electron density distributed across the conjugated framework rather than fixed in a single Lewis structure.

### 5.2 Effect of Conjugation on Energy Levels and Absorption Wavelength

As shown in Section 3.4, each additional conjugated double bond added to a π system:

1. Increases the number of π molecular orbitals (from combining atomic p-orbitals), splitting them into a denser set of energy levels.
2. **Narrows** the HOMO–LUMO energy gap.
3. **Shifts λmax to longer wavelength** (bathochromic shift), moving absorption progressively from the UV toward, and eventually into, the visible region.
4. Generally **increases** the intensity (ε) of absorption as well (hyperchromic effect), because a longer conjugated chromophore has a larger transition dipole moment.

### 5.3 Illustrative Examples (Increasing Conjugation)

| Compound | Number of Conjugated C=C | Approximate λmax (nm) | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethene | 1 (isolated) | ~165–175 | Colourless (absorbs in vacuum-UV) |
| 1,3-Butadiene | 2 (conjugated) | ~217 | Colourless (UV) |
| 1,3,5-Hexatriene | 3 (conjugated) | ~258 | Colourless (UV) |
| β-Carotene | 11 conjugated C=C (polyene chain) | ~450–470 | Orange (visible) |
| Azobenzene (–N=N– conjugated with two phenyl rings) | Azo + 2 aromatic rings | ~320 (main band), weak n→π* near 440 | Pale yellow-orange |
| Extensively substituted azo dye (e.g., an aminoazobenzene with donor/acceptor auxochromes) | Azo + aromatic rings + auxochromes | Extends well into 450–550+ | Deep red/orange to blue, depending on substitution |

> **Trend to remember:** ethene → butadiene → hexatriene → β-carotene is the textbook progression demonstrating that each additional conjugated double bond shifts λmax to longer wavelength; β-carotene's eleven conjugated double bonds bring absorption fully into the visible region, which is why carotenoids are intensely orange/yellow. Azo compounds add a second route to extending conjugation: the azo (–N=N–) chromophore conjugated with aromatic rings and strengthened by amino/hydroxy auxochromes is the structural basis of the largest class of synthetic dyes (Section 11).

## 6. Effect of Molecular Structure on Colour

Beyond simple chain length, several structural factors influence the position and intensity of absorption, and hence the observed colour:

- **Length of the conjugated system:** longer conjugation → smaller HOMO–LUMO gap → bathochromic shift (Section 5.2).
- **Aromaticity:** fused aromatic rings (as in anthraquinone or phthalocyanine dyes) provide rigid, highly delocalised π systems that support strong, well-defined absorption bands.
- **Planarity:** effective conjugation requires the p-orbitals across the system to be able to align in a common plane. Non-planar (twisted) molecules have poorer orbital overlap between adjacent conjugated units, reducing delocalisation and often causing a hypsochromic shift and/or hypochromic effect relative to the planar analogue.
- **Electron-donating groups (EDGs)** (e.g., –OH, –NH₂, –OR): raise the HOMO energy via resonance donation, narrowing the gap and producing a bathochromic shift — classic auxochromic behaviour (Section 4.2).
- **Electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs)** (e.g., –NO₂, –CN, –SO₃H in certain contexts): lower the LUMO energy via resonance/inductive withdrawal; when placed appropriately (often opposite a donor group, as in "push–pull" or donor–acceptor chromophores typical of azo dyes), this also narrows the gap and deepens colour.
- **Resonance:** the ability of a substituent's lone pair or π system to delocalise into the main chromophore is what determines whether it behaves as a true auxochrome; resonance donation/withdrawal is the electronic mechanism underlying essentially all colour-modifying substituent effects.
- **Steric effects:** bulky substituents can force twisting out of plane (loss of conjugation, hypsochromic shift) or can prevent unfavourable non-bonded interactions that would otherwise distort the chromophore.
- **Molecular symmetry:** symmetric conjugated systems can have transitions that are formally forbidden by selection rules, reducing intensity (hypochromic), while unsymmetrical donor–acceptor substitution (as engineered in most commercial dyes) typically gives strongly allowed, intense transitions.
- **Substitution pattern:** the position (ortho/meta/para) of auxochromes relative to the chromophore strongly affects the degree of conjugative interaction and therefore the magnitude of any bathochromic/hypsochromic shift.
- **Protonation/deprotonation:** converting an auxochrome between its neutral and ionised forms (e.g., phenol ⇌ phenolate, amine ⇌ ammonium) changes its electron-donating ability dramatically. Protonating an amino auxochrome (forming –NH₃⁺) removes its lone pair from conjugation, causing a pronounced hypsochromic shift — the basis of many pH indicator dyes (Section 16).
- **Solvent effects (solvatochromism):** polar solvents can stabilise the excited state or ground state differently depending on the dye's dipole moment, shifting λmax (positive or negative solvatochromism) between solvents of different polarity.
- **Aggregation:** at high concentration or on certain substrates, dye molecules can stack (H-aggregates, J-aggregates), which shifts and/or broadens absorption bands relative to the isolated (monomeric) dye in dilute solution.

> **Summary relationship:** Any structural change that **narrows** the HOMO–LUMO gap (more conjugation, stronger donor–acceptor push–pull character, greater planarity) produces a **bathochromic shift**; any change that **widens** the gap (less conjugation, loss of planarity, removal/protonation of a donor auxochrome) produces a **hypsochromic shift**.

# Part III — Dyes

## 7. Definition and Characteristics of Dyes

A **dye** is precisely defined as an intensely coloured (or fluorescent) organic compound that is soluble or can be brought into a fine colloidal dispersion in the application medium, and which possesses **substantivity** — a genuine physical or chemical affinity for a particular substrate — allowing it to be absorbed onto, or chemically bonded into, that substrate to produce a colouration that resists removal under normal conditions of use.

**Characteristics required for a substance to function as a practical dye:**

1. **Colour** — must absorb strongly in the visible region (adequate chromophore/conjugation and molar absorptivity).
2. **Affinity for substrate (substantivity)** — must be attracted to and taken up by the target material (fibre, paper, leather, etc.), whether by ionic attraction, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, or covalent bonding.
3. **Solubility or dispersibility** — must be capable of being applied from an aqueous solution, dispersion, or another suitable medium so it can penetrate the substrate.
4. **Fixation** — once applied, the dye must become "fixed" in the substrate (physically entrapped, ionically bound, or covalently attached) rather than washing out immediately.
5. **Fastness properties** — the colouration must be reasonably resistant to fading or removal under conditions the substrate will encounter in use: washing (**wash fastness**), exposure to sunlight (**light fastness**), rubbing (**rubbing/crocking fastness**), perspiration, and chemical treatments (e.g., bleach, dry cleaning solvents).

**Distinction between a coloured compound and a useful dye:** Many organic compounds absorb visible light and are therefore "coloured" in a purely optical sense (e.g., simple azo or nitro compounds), but they are **not** automatically dyes. A compound only qualifies as a *dye* if it additionally has the solubility/dispersibility and substrate affinity needed to be applied and fixed durably. A coloured compound lacking substantivity for any substrate, or lacking adequate fastness once applied, is chemically "coloured" but not commercially or practically useful as a dye — it may instead be used as a **pigment** (if insoluble, dispersible in a binder) or simply remain a laboratory curiosity.

## 8. Structure of Dyes

Dye molecules are conventionally analysed as being built from several structural components, each contributing a distinct function.

### 8.1 Chromophore

**Definition:** the unsaturated group primarily responsible for light absorption (Section 4.1). **Function:** establishes the basic colour-generating conjugated framework of the dye. **Examples:** –N=N– (azo dyes), C=O conjugated with aromatic rings (anthraquinone dyes), the extended conjugated system of indigo.

### 8.2 Auxochrome

**Definition:** an electron-donating or -withdrawing substituent that modifies the hue and intensity produced by the chromophore, and often also assists solubility/fixation (Section 4.2). **Function:** fine-tunes colour (bathochromic/hypsochromic shifts) and can provide sites for fixation to the substrate (e.g., –NH₂ can form a covalent or hydrogen-bonded link; –SO₃⁻ provides ionic affinity for cationic sites on protein fibres). **Examples:** –OH, –NH₂, –NR₂ (donors); –NO₂, –SO₃H in certain contexts (acceptors/solubilisers).

### 8.3 Conjugated System

The chromophore(s) and auxochrome(s) must be linked through a continuous, sufficiently extended **conjugated π system** (aromatic rings, azo linkages, polyene chains) for their electronic effects to combine into a single, delocalised absorbing unit. Without an intact conjugated pathway connecting donor and acceptor groups, the auxochromic effect cannot be transmitted to the chromophore, and no useful bathochromic shift results. This conjugated framework is therefore the structural "backbone" that determines both the possibility and the extent of visible colour in the dye molecule.

### 8.4 Solubilising Groups

Ionisable, polar groups are incorporated into dye molecules specifically to confer water solubility (or, in the case of disperse dyes, adequate dispersibility) and, frequently, to provide the substrate affinity mechanism itself:

- **–SO₃H / –SO₃⁻ (sulfonic acid / sulfonate):** the most common solubilising group in synthetic dyes; ionises readily in water to give an anionic dye, which is the basis of **acid dyes** and many **direct dyes**.
- **–COOH / –COO⁻ (carboxylic acid / carboxylate):** a weaker acid than sulfonic acid; contributes some water solubility and anionic character, though less strongly ionised at typical dyeing pH.
- **Quaternary ammonium groups (–NR₃⁺):** confer permanent cationic charge, giving water-soluble, positively charged dyes; the structural basis of **basic (cationic) dyes**, which have strong affinity for anionic sites (e.g., on acrylic fibres).

## 9. Nomenclature of Dyes

Dyes are referred to by several distinct naming systems, which serve different purposes and must not be confused with one another.

- **Common/trivial names:** informal names, often derived from the discoverer, historical origin, or an evocative description, e.g., "Congo Red," "Malachite Green," "Aniline Yellow." These names give no systematic structural information.
- **Systematic (IUPAC) chemical names:** precise names describing the full molecular structure according to standard organic nomenclature rules, e.g., the systematic name for methyl orange is *4-[4-(dimethylamino)phenyldiazenyl]benzenesulfonic acid, sodium salt*. Systematic names are exact but often long and impractical for everyday industrial use.
- **Colour Index (C.I.) names:** an internationally standardised reference system (the *Colour Index*, jointly published by the Society of Dyers and Colourists and the American Association of Textile Chemists and Colorists) that assigns every commercial dye/pigment **two** identifiers:
    - **C.I. Generic Name:** describes the dye's **application class** and **hue**, followed by a sequential number, e.g., *C.I. Direct Red 28* (a direct dye, red hue, 28th in that category to be catalogued), *C.I. Acid Blue 9*, *C.I. Vat Blue 1*.
    - **C.I. Constitution Number:** a five-digit number that identifies the dye by its **chemical structural class**, independent of application or hue, e.g., *C.I. 22120* is the constitution number for Congo Red. Dyes with the same constitution number are chemically identical regardless of trade name.

**General format of a Colour Index designation:**

```
C.I. <Application Class> <Hue> <Sequential Number>     (Generic Name)
C.I. <5-digit number>                                   (Constitution Number)
```

**Representative examples:**

| Dye | Chemical Class | C.I. Generic Name | C.I. Constitution No. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Congo Red | Azo (direct) | C.I. Direct Red 28 | 22120 |
| Methyl Orange | Azo (acid/indicator) | C.I. Acid Orange 52 | 13025 |
| Malachite Green | Triphenylmethane (basic) | C.I. Basic Green 4 | 42000 |
| Alizarin | Anthraquinone (mordant) | C.I. Mordant Red 11 | 58000 |
| Indigo | Indigoid (vat) | C.I. Vat Blue 1 | 73000 |
| Reactive Black 5 | Azo (reactive) | C.I. Reactive Black 5 | 20505 |

> **Caution:** A **commercial/trade name** given by a manufacturer (e.g., a proprietary brand name used for marketing) is **not** the same as either the systematic chemical name or the Colour Index name; the same chemical dye may be sold under many different trade names by different manufacturers, but it will have only one systematic name and one C.I. designation.

## 10. Classification of Dyes

Dyes are classified along **two independent axes**, which must be kept conceptually distinct: **(a)** classification by **chemical structure** (what chromophore/framework the molecule contains) and **(b)** classification by **method of application** (how the dye is chemically/physically fixed to a particular substrate). The same structural class (e.g., azo dyes) can contain members belonging to several different application classes (acid azo dyes, direct azo dyes, reactive azo dyes, disperse azo dyes), because structure and application method answer different questions.

### 10.1 Classification According to Chemical Structure

| Class | Structural Feature | Characteristic Chromophore | Representative Example | Application |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Azo dyes** | One or more –N=N– groups linking aromatic rings | –N=N– (azo) | Congo Red, Methyl Orange | Largest class; textiles, indicators, food colouring |
| **Nitro dyes** | Nitro group conjugated with an electron-donating group on an aromatic ring | –NO₂ | Martius Yellow (2,4-dinitro-1-naphthol) | Historically used yellow dyes |
| **Nitroso dyes** | Nitroso group, usually ortho to a phenolic –OH, chelating with metal ions | –N=O | Naphthol Green B (Fe complex) | Mordant/metal-complex dyes |
| **Triphenylmethane dyes** | Central sp²/sp³ carbon bonded to three aromatic rings, one in quinonoid form when coloured | Extended conjugated system through the central carbon | Malachite Green, Crystal Violet | Basic dyes for paper, leather, biological stains |
| **Xanthene dyes** | Tricyclic system with a central pyran-type oxygen bridging two benzene rings | Extended conjugated xanthene ring system | Fluorescein, Rhodamine B | Fluorescent tracers, biological staining |
| **Anthraquinone dyes** | Anthraquinone (9,10-anthracenedione) nucleus with auxochromic substituents | C=O (carbonyl) conjugated with the fused ring system | Alizarin, C.I. Vat Blue 4 | Vat and mordant dyes; excellent light fastness |
| **Indigoid dyes** | Two indoxyl units joined by a central C=C double bond | Central –C=C– cross-conjugated with two carbonyl groups | Indigo, Tyrian Purple (6,6′-dibromoindigo) | Vat dyeing (denim/cotton) |
| **Phthalocyanine dyes** | Macrocyclic ring of four isoindole units, often complexed with a central metal (commonly Cu) | Extensively delocalised 18 π-electron macrocycle | Copper phthalocyanine (also widely used as a pigment) | Reactive/direct dyes and pigments; exceptional fastness |
| **Quinoline dyes** | Quinoline ring fused/condensed with other heterocyclic units | Extended heteroaromatic conjugation | Quinoline Yellow | Food colouring, textile dyeing |
| **Polymethine (cyanine) dyes** | Two nitrogen-containing heterocycles linked by an odd-numbered polymethine (–CH=)ₙ chain | Delocalised polymethine chain, often a resonance-stabilised cationic system | Cyanine dyes used in photography/sensitisers | Photographic sensitisers, biological fluorescent labels |
| **Sulfur dyes** | Complex, poorly defined polymeric structures containing sulfur bridges (thianthrene/polysulfide linkages) formed during synthesis | Sulfur-bridged conjugated units (structure not fully characterised) | C.I. Sulfur Black 1 | Inexpensive black/navy dyeing of cotton |
| **Natural dyes** | Diverse: anthraquinone, indigoid, flavonoid, or carotenoid structures obtained from plant/animal/mineral sources | Varies by source | Indigo (plant), Alizarin (madder root), Cochineal/Carminic acid (insect) | Historical textile dyeing; still used in food/cosmetics |

| **Basic (cationic) dyes** | Cationic, water-soluble dyes (as chloride/salt) | Contain quaternary ammonium or protonated amino groups | Acrylic fibres; historically silk, wool, tannin-mordanted cotton | Ionic attraction between cationic dye and anionic sites on the fibre | Malachite Green, Crystal Violet | Very bright, intense shades; generally poor light fastness on natural fibres |

| **Mordant dyes** | Dyes requiring pre-treatment of the fibre with a metal salt (**mordant**) to form an insoluble coloured metal complex (**lake**) within the fibre | Typically contain adjacent –OH/C=O groups capable of chelating a metal ion | Wool, cotton (historically) | The dye chelates with a metal ion (commonly Al³⁺, Cr³⁺, Fe³⁺) pre-deposited on the fibre, forming an insoluble, fixed coloured complex | Alizarin with an aluminium mordant ("Turkey Red") | Excellent fastness; multi-step, labour-intensive application |

> **Distinguishing the two classification schemes:** "Azo dye" (structural) tells you the molecule contains an –N=N– chromophore; it says nothing about how the dye is applied. "Direct dye" (application-based) tells you the dye is applied directly to cellulose from a neutral bath; it says nothing about the underlying chromophore. Congo Red, for instance, is simultaneously an **azo dye** (structural class) and a **direct dye** (application class) — both classifications are correct and describe different aspects of the same molecule.

## 11. Important Classes of Dyes — Detailed Study

### 11.1 Azo Dyes

**Structure:** characterised by one or more **azo groups (–N=N–)** linking two aromatic (or heteroaromatic) ring systems, e.g., Ar–N=N–Ar′. Mono-azo, di-azo, and poly-azo dyes exist, depending on the number of azo linkages.

**Preparation principle — a two-step process:**

**Step 1: Diazotisation.** A primary aromatic amine is treated with nitrous acid (generated *in situ* from sodium nitrite and a mineral acid) at low temperature (0–5 °C) to form a **diazonium salt**:

$$\text{Ar–NH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0\text{–}5\,^{\circ}\text{C}} \text{Ar–N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Low temperature is essential because diazonium salts are thermally unstable and decompose above ~5–10 °C.

**Step 2: Azo coupling.** The diazonium salt (acting as an electrophile) reacts with a second aromatic compound bearing a strongly activating group (a "coupling component," typically a phenol or an aromatic amine) under mildly acidic (for amine couplers) or alkaline (for phenolic couplers) conditions, via electrophilic aromatic substitution, predominantly at the position **para** to the activating group (or *ortho* if *para* is blocked):

$$\text{Ar–N}_2^+ + \text{Ar}'\text{–OH} \xrightarrow{\text{alkaline, cold}} \text{Ar–N=N–Ar}'\text{–OH} + \text{H}^+$$

**Worked example — Methyl Orange:**

1. Sulfanilic acid is diazotised: 4-H₂N–C₆H₄–SO₃H → 4-⁺N₂–C₆H₄–SO₃H (diazonium salt).
2. Coupling with *N,N*-dimethylaniline occurs para to the dimethylamino group:

$$\text{4-}^+\text{N}_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–SO}_3^- + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{–N(CH}_3)_2 \longrightarrow \text{HO}_3\text{S–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–N=N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–N(CH}_3)_2$$

This gives **Methyl Orange**, a classic azo dye and acid–base indicator.

**Uses:** azo dyes form the largest and most versatile chemical class of synthetic dyes, spanning acid, direct, basic, reactive, and disperse application classes; also widely used as **acid–base indicators** (methyl orange, methyl red) and (for a small number of approved compounds) as **food colourants** (e.g., tartrazine, allura red).

### 11.2 Triphenylmethane Dyes

**Basic structure:** three aromatic (usually *para*-amino-substituted phenyl) rings attached to a central carbon atom. In the coloured, cationic form, one ring adopts a **quinonoid** structure (Section 4.4) with the central carbon part of an extended, delocalised cationic conjugated system; in the colourless "leuco" form, the central carbon is sp³ and all three rings are ordinary benzenoid, interrupting conjugation.

**Examples:** Malachite Green (C.I. Basic Green 4), Crystal Violet (C.I. Basic Violet 3), Fuchsine (magenta).

**Colour origin:** delocalisation of the positive charge and π electrons over all three aminophenyl rings through the central carbon, stabilised by resonance donation from the *para*-amino auxochromes into the quinonoid ring — a textbook illustration of the quinonoid theory in Section 4.4.

**Uses:** basic (cationic) dyes for paper, leather, and acrylic textiles; extensively used as **biological stains** in microscopy (e.g., Crystal Violet in the Gram stain, Section 16).

### 11.3 Anthraquinone Dyes

**Basic structure:** built on the **anthraquinone (9,10-anthracenedione)** nucleus — a linear tricyclic aromatic system with two carbonyl groups at the 9- and 10-positions of the central ring.

**Chromophore:** the two carbonyl groups (C=O), conjugated with the fused aromatic ring system, together with auxochromic –OH/–NH₂ substituents typically placed at the 1-, 2-, 4-, 5-, or 8-positions.

**Examples:** Alizarin (1,2-dihydroxyanthraquinone, from madder root — a natural mordant dye), C.I. Vat Blue 4 (Indanthrone), various disperse anthraquinone dyes used for polyester.

**Properties:** the rigid, fully conjugated fused-ring framework gives anthraquinone dyes outstanding **light fastness** and generally bright, clear hues (particularly blues, reds, and violets), though the class is chemically more costly to synthesise than azo dyes.

**Uses:** vat dyeing and mordant dyeing of cotton and wool; disperse dyeing of polyester; historically important as one of the oldest known dye classes (via natural alizarin).

### 11.4 Indigoid Dyes

**Indigo structure:** two **indoxyl** (3-hydroxyindole-derived) units joined through a central **C=C double bond**, with two carbonyl groups flanking this central double bond in a symmetric, cross-conjugated arrangement (molecular formula C₁₆H₁₀N₂O₂).

**Colour-producing system:** the central –C=C– double bond is conjugated with both flanking carbonyl groups and the two fused benzene rings, creating an extended, planar, hydrogen-bond-stabilised (intramolecular N–H···O=C) chromophoric system responsible for indigo's characteristic deep blue colour.

**Vat-dyeing principle:** indigo is insoluble in water in its normal (oxidised, "keto") form. For dyeing, it is chemically **reduced** (traditionally using sodium dithionite, Na₂S₂O₄, in alkaline solution) to the soluble, pale-yellow **leuco-indigo** (enolate) form, which has affinity for cellulose fibres and penetrates the fibre in solution. After the fabric is removed from the dye bath, atmospheric **oxidation** reconverts leuco-indigo back to the insoluble, blue, parent indigo *inside* the fibre, where it becomes mechanically trapped:

$$\text{Indigo (blue, insoluble)} \xrightarrow[\text{NaOH}]{\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_4} \text{Leuco-indigo (yellow, soluble)} \xrightarrow{\text{O}_2 \text{ (air)}} \text{Indigo (blue, insoluble, fixed in fibre)}$$

**Example:** Indigo (C.I. Vat Blue 1) is the classic representative; 6,6′-dibromoindigo (Tyrian Purple) is a related natural indigoid dye historically obtained from sea snails.

**Uses:** the traditional and continuing dye for denim (blue jeans) and other cotton fabrics dyed by the vat process.

### 11.5 Reactive Dyes

**Reactive group:** a specifically designed electrophilic moiety capable of reacting covalently with nucleophilic –OH (cellulose) or –NH₂/–NH (protein) groups on the fibre. The two most common types are **chlorotriazinyl** groups (which react by nucleophilic aromatic substitution, displacing chloride) and **vinyl sulfone** groups (which react by nucleophilic addition, or, after alkaline elimination from a sulfatoethylsulfone precursor, by Michael-type addition).

**Fibre–dye bond:** unlike direct or acid dyes, which are held to the fibre by non-covalent forces, reactive dyes form a genuine **covalent bond** between the dye molecule and the fibre polymer chain.

**Covalent fixation (illustrative, dichlorotriazine type):**

$$\text{Dye–NH–C}_3\text{N}_3\text{Cl}_2 + \text{Cellulose–OH} \xrightarrow{\text{alkaline}} \text{Dye–NH–C}_3\text{N}_3\text{Cl(O–Cellulose)} + \text{HCl}$$

**Example:** C.I. Reactive Black 5 (a vinyl-sulfone-type diazo reactive dye), widely used for dyeing cotton black/navy.

**Advantages:** exceptionally high wash fastness (because the dye is chemically part of the fibre polymer itself), bright shades, and a wide, versatile colour range; the main limitation is the need for precise pH/temperature control and generation of hydrolysed (unfixed) dye that must be washed off, contributing to dyehouse effluent.

### 11.6 Disperse Dyes

**Structure:** small, essentially **non-ionic** organic molecules (commonly azo or anthraquinone based) with only limited polar substitution (e.g., small amounts of –OH, –NHCOCH₃, or nitro/cyano groups), keeping overall polarity low.

**Solubility:** only sparingly soluble in water; applied as a very fine, stabilised aqueous **dispersion** (with dispersing agents) rather than a true solution.

**Application to hydrophobic fibres:** at elevated dyeing temperature (typically ~120–130 °C for polyester, often under pressure, or with a carrier at lower temperature), individual dye molecules dissolve out of the dispersion directly into the amorphous regions of the hydrophobic polymer (e.g., polyester, cellulose acetate) and diffuse through the fibre matrix, driven by favourable non-polar (dye–polymer) interactions rather than by ionic or hydrogen-bonding affinity.

**Examples:** C.I. Disperse Blue 3, C.I. Disperse Red 1 (both simple aminoazobenzene-type or anthraquinone-type structures).

### 11.7 Acid and Basic Dyes

**Ionic character:** acid dyes are **anionic** (bearing –SO₃⁻/–COO⁻ groups); basic (cationic) dyes are **cationic** (bearing –NR₃⁺ or protonated amino groups).

**Substrate:** acid dyes are used on protein fibres (wool, silk) and polyamide (nylon), which carry protonatable amino groups; basic dyes are used principally on acrylic fibres, which carry anionic (sulfonate-derived) sites built into the polymer.

**Application principle:** in both cases, fixation occurs primarily through **ionic (electrostatic) attraction** between the charged dye and oppositely charged sites on the fibre, applied from an aqueous bath under conditions (pH, temperature) that maximise the number of accessible charged sites on the substrate.

**Examples:** C.I. Acid Blue 9 (acid dye, triarylmethane sulfonate type), Malachite Green and Crystal Violet (basic/cationic dyes, triphenylmethane type).

# Part IV — Raw Materials for Synthesis of Dyes

## 12. Raw Materials

Synthetic dyes are built up, historically and industrially, from a relatively small set of simple **aromatic hydrocarbons and their basic derivatives**, obtained originally from coal tar and now largely from petroleum-derived aromatics, which are progressively functionalised into more complex **dye intermediates** (Part V) and finally coupled/condensed into finished dye molecules.

**Key aromatic raw materials:**

- **Benzene (C₆H₆):** the simplest aromatic raw material; converted by nitration/reduction/sulfonation sequences into aniline, phenol, benzenesulfonic acid, and related simple intermediates.
- **Toluene (C₆H₅CH₃):** nitrated to give ortho/para-nitrotoluenes, which are reduced to toluidines (methylanilines), important amine intermediates and precursors to azo coupling components.
- **Naphthalene (C₁₀H₈):** a bicyclic aromatic; the principal source of naphthols (1-naphthol, 2-naphthol) and naphthylamines (via nitration/reduction) and naphthalenesulfonic acids (via sulfonation) — key coupling components for azo dyes.
- **Anthracene (C₁₄H₁₀):** a tricyclic aromatic; oxidised to **anthraquinone**, the parent nucleus of the entire anthraquinone dye class.
- **Phenol (C₆H₅OH):** obtained from benzene (via the cumene process industrially, or historically from coal tar); itself an important coupling component and precursor to further intermediates.
- **Aniline (C₆H₅NH₂):** obtained by reduction of nitrobenzene; the single most important amine intermediate in dye chemistry, used directly in diazotisation and as a building block for numerous derivatives.
- **Nitrotoluene:** obtained by nitration of toluene; reduced to toluidines, which serve as diazo components or coupling components bearing an extra methyl group that modifies dye hue and fastness.
- **Naphthols (1-naphthol, 2-naphthol):** obtained from naphthalene (via sulfonation followed by alkali fusion, or via naphthylamine hydrolysis); among the most widely used coupling components in azo dye synthesis (the classical "Naphthol AS" dyeing systems).
- **Aromatic amines** (aniline, toluidines, naphthylamines, phenylenediamines): essential as the diazo component (the amine that is diazotised) in nearly every azo dye synthesis.
- **Aromatic sulfonic acids** (benzenesulfonic acid, naphthalenesulfonic acids): introduced via sulfonation to provide the water-solubilising –SO₃H group present in the majority of synthetic dyes.

**General conversion pathway (illustrative):**

$$\text{Benzene} \xrightarrow{\text{HNO}_3/\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4} \text{Nitrobenzene} \xrightarrow{\text{Fe/HCl (reduction)}} \text{Aniline} \xrightarrow{\text{NaNO}_2/\text{HCl}, 0\text{–}5\,^{\circ}\text{C}} \text{Benzenediazonium chloride} \xrightarrow{\text{coupling with a phenol/amine}} \text{Azo dye}$$

## 13. Important Functional Transformations in Dye Manufacture

| Reaction | Starting Material | Reagent(s)/Conditions | Product | Importance in Dye Synthesis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nitration** | Benzene | Conc. HNO₃ / conc. H₂SO₄, ~50–60 °C | Nitrobenzene | Introduces –NO₂, the precursor functional group for making aromatic amines |
| **Reduction of nitro compounds** | Nitrobenzene | Fe/HCl (Béchamp reduction), or H₂/catalyst | Aniline | Converts –NO₂ to –NH₂, generating the key diazotisable amine |
| **Sulfonation** | Benzene / Naphthalene | Fuming/conc. H₂SO₄ (oleum), heat | Benzenesulfonic acid / Naphthalenesulfonic acid | Introduces –SO₃H, the principal water-solubilising group |
| **Halogenation** (where relevant) | Aromatic amine/ring system | Cl₂ or Br₂, often with a Lewis acid catalyst (e.g., FeCl₃) | Chloro-/bromo-aromatic derivative | Modifies hue, improves fastness, or introduces a leaving group (e.g., for reactive dye triazine chemistry) |
| **Amination** | Chlorobenzene (or phenol, via other routes) | NH₃, high temperature/pressure with Cu catalyst | Aniline | Alternative industrial route to aromatic amines |
| **Diazotisation** | Primary aromatic amine (e.g., aniline) | NaNO₂ + HCl (or H₂SO₄), 0–5 °C | Aryldiazonium salt | Generates the reactive electrophilic diazonium ion essential for azo coupling |
| **Azo coupling** | Aryldiazonium salt + coupling component (phenol or amine) | Alkaline (for phenols) or mildly acidic (for amines), cold | Azo compound (Ar–N=N–Ar′) | Forms the –N=N– chromophore; the key colour-generating step for the entire azo dye class |
| **Condensation reactions** | E.g., phthalic anhydride + urea + metal salt (phthalocyanine synthesis); or aromatic aldehyde + amine (triphenylmethane synthesis) | Heat, catalyst as required | Fused heterocyclic or polycyclic dye framework | Builds the core conjugated ring systems of anthraquinone, phthalocyanine, and triarylmethane dyes |
| **Alkylation/acylation** (where relevant) | Aromatic amine (e.g., aniline) | Alkyl halide/dialkyl sulfate (alkylation) or acid anhydride/acid chloride (acylation) | N-alkyl/N-acyl aromatic amine | Modifies the auxochromic strength of amino groups (e.g., converting –NH₂ to –N(CH₃)₂ increases donor strength; acylation can protect an amine during a later step) |

# Part V — Dye Intermediates

## 14. What Are Dye Intermediates?

A **dye intermediate** is a chemically well-defined organic compound, derived from a basic raw material by one or more functional-group transformations (nitration, reduction, sulfonation, etc.), that is not itself a finished dye but serves as a necessary **building block** in the multi-step synthesis of one or more dyes.

**Difference between a raw material and a dye intermediate:** a **raw material** (Section 12) is typically a simple, cheap, bulk aromatic hydrocarbon obtained directly from petroleum/coal-tar processing (benzene, toluene, naphthalene). A **dye intermediate** is a more highly functionalised, structurally specific compound (e.g., aniline, 2-naphthol, benzenediazonium chloride) produced from a raw material through one or more of the transformations in Section 13, and it stands as a distinct, often independently sold and traded, chemical closer in structure to the eventual dye. In short: **raw material → (one or more reactions) → dye intermediate → (further reaction, e.g., coupling/condensation) → finished dye.**

**Role in multi-step synthesis:** because most dye molecules (especially azo dyes) are assembled from two separately prepared halves — a **diazo component** (an aromatic amine that will be diazotised) and a **coupling component** (a phenol or amine that will undergo electrophilic substitution) — dye intermediates allow manufacturers to prepare, purify, store, and stock these building blocks independently, then combine different diazo and coupling components combinatorially to generate a very large number of distinct dyes from a comparatively small set of intermediates.

**Why intermediates are industrially important:** a limited "toolbox" of well-characterised intermediates (a few dozen key amines, naphthols, and sulfonic acids) can be combined pairwise and in different substitution patterns to produce **thousands** of distinct commercial dyes, making intermediate manufacture a distinct and economically significant branch of the dye industry, separate from (though feeding into) final dye synthesis.

## 15. Important Dye Intermediates

### 15.1 Aromatic Amines

- **Aniline (C₆H₅NH₂):** prepared by reduction of nitrobenzene (Fe/HCl or catalytic hydrogenation). The single most important diazo component in azo dye manufacture; also a precursor to numerous N-alkylated/N-acylated derivatives used as coupling components (e.g., *N,N*-dimethylaniline, used in Methyl Orange synthesis, Section 11.1).
- ***p*-Phenylenediamine (1,4-diaminobenzene):** prepared by reduction of *p*-nitroaniline. Contains two amino groups, allowing it to act as a diazo component at one end while the second amino group remains available for further coupling or condensation; important in the synthesis of certain azo and oxidation-type (fur/hair) dyes.
- **α-Naphthylamine (1-aminonaphthalene):** prepared by reduction of 1-nitronaphthalene. A diazo component providing a larger, more strongly conjugated aromatic system than aniline, giving deeper-coloured azo dyes.
- **β-Naphthylamine (2-aminonaphthalene):** prepared by amination of 2-naphthol (Bucherer reaction) or reduction of 2-nitronaphthalene. Historically important as both a diazo and coupling component; now largely discontinued industrially due to its recognised human carcinogenicity, but retained here for its historical/structural significance in dye chemistry education.

> **Note:** β-Naphthylamine is a well-documented human bladder carcinogen and its industrial manufacture/use has been phased out or tightly regulated in most jurisdictions. It is discussed here only for its historical and structural role in classical dye chemistry, not as a recommendation for laboratory handling.

### 15.2 Phenols and Naphthols

- **Phenol (C₆H₅OH):** obtained industrially via the cumene process, or historically from coal tar. Acts as a coupling component in azo dye synthesis (coupling occurs *para* to the –OH group, or *ortho* if *para* is blocked); also a precursor to further chlorinated/nitrated/sulfonated intermediates.
- **1-Naphthol:** prepared from naphthalene via sulfonation followed by alkali fusion (or from 1-naphthylamine by diazotisation/hydrolysis). A strongly activating coupling component used to make deep-coloured azo dyes.
- **2-Naphthol:** prepared analogously from naphthalene-2-sulfonic acid via alkali fusion. One of the most important coupling components in the entire azo dye industry (classical example: coupling with diazotised aniline gives **Sudan I**, a simple azo dye/colourant).

### 15.3 Aromatic Nitro Compounds

- **Nitrobenzene (C₆H₅NO₂):** prepared by direct nitration of benzene (HNO₃/H₂SO₄). Not itself coloured strongly enough to be a dye, but is the essential precursor to aniline (via reduction) and hence to the entire aniline-based branch of azo dye chemistry; also used directly as an oxidising agent in some dye-manufacturing steps.
- **Nitrobenzene derivatives** (e.g., *o*- and *p*-nitrotoluene, dinitrobenzenes, nitroanilines): prepared by nitration of the corresponding substituted benzene; reduced selectively (partial or complete) to give a range of substituted amine intermediates with different substitution patterns for tailoring dye hue and fastness.

### 15.4 Aromatic Sulfonic Acids

- **Benzenesulfonic acid:** prepared by sulfonation of benzene with fuming sulfuric acid (oleum). Serves both as a solubilising-group source (via further reactions) and, via alkali fusion, as a route to phenol.
- **Naphthalenesulfonic acid derivatives** (e.g., naphthalene-1-sulfonic acid, naphthalene-2-sulfonic acid, and their further sulfonated/aminated derivatives such as the Schaeffer's and Koch's acid families used in azo dye manufacture): prepared by controlled sulfonation of naphthalene, with the position of sulfonation depending on reaction temperature (kinetic vs thermodynamic control — lower temperature favours the 1-sulfonic acid, higher temperature favours the more thermodynamically stable 2-sulfonic acid). These are essential for building water-soluble, high-substantivity azo dyes for cellulosic and protein fibres.

### 15.5 Diazonium Compounds

- **Benzenediazonium salts (e.g., benzenediazonium chloride, C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻):** prepared by diazotisation of aniline with NaNO₂/HCl at 0–5 °C (Section 11.1). Although diazonium salts are themselves generally too unstable to be isolated or stored for long, they are the pivotal **reactive intermediate** in azo dye synthesis, generated *in situ* immediately before the coupling step, and are therefore classified as dye intermediates in the mechanistic (rather than the storable/tradeable) sense.

# Part VI — Non-Textile Uses of Dyes

## 16. Non-Textile Applications of Dyes

Although historically developed for textile colouration, synthetic and natural dyes have extensive applications far beyond fibre dyeing, exploiting the same fundamental property — strong, selective, and often reversible interaction with visible light or with specific chemical/biological environments.

### 16.1 Food Colouring

A small, strictly regulated subset of synthetic azo and other dyes, individually tested and approved by food-safety authorities, are permitted as **food colourants** at controlled concentrations, e.g., Tartrazine (C.I. Food Yellow 4, an azo dye) and Allura Red AC. **Key relevant property:** intense, stable colour at low concentration and demonstrated toxicological safety at approved usage levels.

> **Important distinction:** the vast majority of industrial textile dyes (including many discussed in Part III) are **not** approved for food or cosmetic use; only compounds specifically evaluated and listed by regulatory bodies (e.g., under E-number systems in the EU, or FD&C listings in the US) may be used as food colourants. Never assume a textile dye is safe for ingestion.

### 16.2 Biological Staining, Histology, and Microscopy

Dyes are fundamental tools in **histology** (the microscopic study of tissue) and **microbiology**, used to selectively stain cell structures, tissue components, or specific organisms so they become visible under a light microscope. **Key relevant property:** differential affinity of a dye for particular cellular components (nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, cell walls) based on charge, polarity, or specific chemical binding.

- **Haematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining:** the standard histological stain; haematoxylin (a natural mordant dye, basic in character when complexed with a metal ion) stains cell nuclei blue-purple, while eosin (an acidic xanthene dye) stains cytoplasm and extracellular matrix pink.
- **Crystal Violet** (a triphenylmethane basic dye): the primary stain in the **Gram stain** procedure, used to differentiate Gram-positive from Gram-negative bacteria based on differential retention of the dye–iodine complex within the cell wall.
- **Methylene Blue** (a thiazine dye related in application to triphenylmethane basic dyes): widely used as a simple bacterial stain and vital stain (staining living cells) in microbiology.

### 16.3 Medical Diagnostics

Certain dyes are used directly in clinical and diagnostic settings, e.g., **Methylene Blue** as a redox indicator and as a treatment for methaemoglobinaemia; **Indocyanine Green**, a polymethine (cyanine-type) near-infrared fluorescent dye, used in medical imaging to assess blood flow, liver function, and cardiac output. **Key relevant property:** specific, biologically compatible optical absorption/fluorescence combined with rapid clearance and low toxicity at diagnostic doses.

### 16.4 Ink, Printing, and Writing Materials

Both dyes and pigments are used in inks, but dye-based inks (soluble, e.g., in fountain pen inks and many inkjet formulations) provide bright, vivid colours and fine penetration into paper fibres, exploiting the same solubility/substrate-affinity mechanisms described for textile dyes. **Key relevant property:** solubility in the ink vehicle and rapid, even absorption into the paper/writing substrate.

### 16.5 Photographic Applications

Historically, **cyanine (polymethine) dyes** were of central importance in silver-halide photography as **spectral sensitisers** — adsorbed onto silver halide crystals, they extend the light sensitivity of the photographic emulsion beyond the blue region (to which silver halides are intrinsically sensitive) into the green and red regions, enabling panchromatic (full-colour-range) black-and-white and colour photographic film. **Key relevant property:** strong, tunable visible/near-IR absorption combined with the ability to transfer absorbed photon energy to the silver halide crystal (sensitisation).

### 16.6 Analytical Chemistry and pH Indicators

Many azo and triphenylmethane dyes function as **acid–base indicators**, changing colour reversibly as a substituent auxochrome (typically an –OH or –NR₂ group) is protonated or deprotonated, which alters conjugation/electron donation and hence shifts λmax (a hypsochromic or bathochromic shift, Section 4.2 and Section 6). **Examples:** Methyl Orange (azo dye, colour change red↔yellow around pH 3.1–4.4), Phenolphthalein (a triphenylmethane-related lactone/quinonoid system, colourless↔pink around pH 8.2–10.0). **Key relevant property:** a structurally accessible ionisable auxochrome whose protonation state directly and reversibly changes the compound's conjugation/colour.

### 16.7 Fluorescent Labelling and Research/Biotechnology

Dyes with strong **fluorescence** (re-emission of absorbed light at a longer wavelength) are used extensively as **fluorescent labels/tags** in molecular biology and biochemistry, e.g., attaching a xanthene dye (fluorescein, rhodamine derivatives) or a cyanine dye to an antibody, nucleic acid probe, or protein to visualise or quantify it by fluorescence microscopy, flow cytometry, or DNA sequencing. **Key relevant property:** high fluorescence quantum yield and the ability to be chemically conjugated (via a reactive handle, analogous in principle to reactive-dye chemistry) to a biomolecule of interest without destroying its function.

### 16.8 Laser and Optical Applications

Certain highly fluorescent, photostable dyes (notably **Rhodamine 6G**, a xanthene dye) are used as the active gain medium in **dye lasers**, which are tunable across a range of visible wavelengths by exploiting the dye's broad fluorescence emission spectrum. **Key relevant property:** high fluorescence efficiency, broad tunable emission, and adequate photostability under intense optical pumping.

### 16.9 Cosmetics

A limited range of dyes, separately regulated and approved for cosmetic (as opposed to food or textile) use, are used to colour cosmetic products such as lipsticks, hair dyes, and nail products, e.g., certain approved xanthene and azo dyes, and oxidative "para-dyes" (based on *p*-phenylenediamine chemistry) used in permanent hair colouring. **Key relevant property:** approval for direct or prolonged skin/hair contact under the relevant cosmetic safety regulations, which is a distinct and generally stricter approval pathway than that for industrial textile dyes.

> **General caution across all non-textile applications:** approval for food, cosmetic, or medical/diagnostic use is compound-specific and jurisdiction-specific. An industrial textile dye must never be assumed usable in any of these contexts without confirming it appears on the relevant approved list (e.g., E-numbers, FD&C colours, or the equivalent national pharmacopoeia/cosmetic regulation).

# Part VII — Dyes vs Pigments

## 17. Pigments

**Definition:** a **pigment** is a coloured, insoluble, finely divided particulate solid that is dispersed (not dissolved) mechanically throughout a **binding medium** (vehicle) such as a paint resin, printing-ink varnish, or plastic polymer, imparting colour by remaining as discrete particles rather than by molecular-level interaction with the substrate.

- **Organic pigments:** insoluble organic compounds, often chemically related to dye classes (azo pigments, phthalocyanine pigments) but engineered or selected for very low solubility so that they remain as stable, non-migrating particles in the medium.
- **Inorganic pigments:** insoluble inorganic compounds (metal oxides, sulfides, chromates, complex salts) valued for opacity, weather resistance, and, in some cases, toxicity-related historical use (now largely phased out for toxic examples such as lead- and chromium-based pigments in consumer products).

**Key physical characteristics:**

- **Insolubility:** by definition, pigments do not dissolve in the application medium; this is the primary physical distinction from dyes.
- **Particle size:** pigment colour strength, opacity, and gloss depend critically on the particle size and size distribution (typically sub-micron to a few micrometres) achieved during manufacture and dispersion.
- **Dispersion:** pigments must be mechanically ground and dispersed uniformly throughout the binder using dispersing agents/surfactants and mechanical energy (milling), unlike dyes, which dissolve spontaneously.
- **Binding medium:** the pigment particles are held in place purely by the binder (resin, oil, polymer matrix) once it dries/cures/solidifies; there is generally no chemical bond between pigment and binder analogous to dye–fibre fixation.
- **Opacity and light scattering:** because pigment particles are physically discrete and often have a refractive index different from the surrounding medium, they scatter as well as absorb light, contributing to **opacity** (hiding power) — a property dyes (which act at the molecular/solution level and are typically transparent) do not generally provide.

**Representative pigments:**

| Pigment | Type | Colour | Notable Property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) | Inorganic | White | Extremely high refractive index; the dominant modern white pigment, providing maximum opacity |
| Iron oxides (e.g., Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄) | Inorganic | Red, yellow, brown, black | Excellent lightfastness and weather resistance; used in paints, cosmetics, ceramics |
| Ultramarine | Inorganic (complex sodium aluminosilicate with polysulfide chromophore) | Blue | Historically extracted from lapis lazuli; now manufactured synthetically |
| Phthalocyanine pigments (e.g., Copper Phthalocyanine Blue/Green) | Organic (metal complex, macrocyclic) | Blue/Green | Exceptional colour strength, lightfastness, and chemical resistance |
| Azo pigments | Organic | Wide range (yellow, orange, red) | Related in core structure to azo dyes but engineered for insolubility (e.g., via larger molecular size or metal-salt formation) |

> **Important clarification:** **phthalocyanine dyes** and **phthalocyanine pigments** share the same fundamental macrocyclic chromophore (Section 10.1) but are **chemically and functionally distinct products**: the dye form is typically sulfonated (or otherwise substituted) to impart water solubility for textile/paper application, whereas the pigment form is deliberately kept unsubstituted or substituted in a way that preserves very low solubility, so it remains as stable particles in paints, inks, and plastics. They must not be treated as interchangeable simply because they share a parent ring system.

## 18. Dyes vs Pigments — Detailed Comparison

| Property | Dyes | Pigments |
|---|---|---|
| **Solubility** | Soluble (or brought into solution/fine dispersion) in the application medium | Insoluble in the application medium |
| **Application** | Applied from solution; penetrates and is fixed within the substrate | Mechanically dispersed as particles throughout a binder/vehicle |
| **Substrate affinity** | Requires genuine chemical/physical affinity (substantivity) for the specific substrate | No chemical affinity required; held in place purely by the binder once solidified |
| **Particle size** | Molecular (true solution); no discrete particles once dissolved | Discrete solid particles, typically sub-micron to a few micrometres |
| **Binding medium** | Generally not required (dye interacts directly with substrate); some processes use auxiliary chemicals (mordants, fixing agents) but not a bulk binder | Essential; pigment cannot function without a binder to hold particles in place |
| **Opacity** | Generally transparent/translucent (colour arises from molecular absorption in solution) | Often opaque; contributes hiding power via light scattering as well as absorption |
| **Typical applications** | Textile fibres, paper, leather, food, biological stains, ink (dye-based), cosmetics | Paints, printing inks, plastics, coatings, artists' colours, cosmetics (pigment-based) |
| **Examples** | Congo Red, Methyl Orange, Indigo (as applied via vat dyeing), Malachite Green | Titanium dioxide, iron oxides, ultramarine, copper phthalocyanine (pigment form), azo pigments |

**Why pigments generally require a binder while dyes can often interact directly with the substrate:** a dye is designed at the molecular level to have genuine chemical affinity (ionic attraction, hydrogen bonding, covalent bonding, or van der Waals interaction along an extended conjugated chain) for the specific substrate it is intended to colour, so it can be taken up from solution and become fixed by that direct interaction. A pigment, by contrast, is deliberately **insoluble** and has no comparable chemical affinity mechanism for most substrates; its particles must therefore be mechanically held in place by a separate binding medium (resin, oil, polymer) that physically traps the particles once it solidifies. This is the fundamental mechanistic reason underlying nearly every other row of the comparison table above.

# Part VIII — Important Concepts and Terminology

## 19. Essential Colour-Chemistry Terminology

- **Chromophore:** an unsaturated functional group primarily responsible for a molecule's absorption of visible/UV light (e.g., –N=N–, C=O, –C=C–).
- **Auxochrome:** a substituent (often bearing a lone pair) that modifies the colour/intensity produced by a chromophore, typically via resonance donation or withdrawal, and often also assists solubility (e.g., –OH, –NH₂, –SO₃H).
- **Colourant:** general umbrella term for any dye or pigment that imparts colour.
- **Dye:** a soluble, substantive coloured compound applied from solution and fixed within a substrate.
- **Pigment:** an insoluble, particulate coloured solid dispersed in a binder.
- **Conjugation:** alternating single/multiple bonds allowing continuous π-orbital overlap and electron delocalisation.
- **Delocalisation:** spreading of π-electron density over more than two adjacent atoms as a result of conjugation/resonance.
- **Bathochromic shift:** shift of λmax to a longer wavelength (red shift).
- **Hypsochromic shift:** shift of λmax to a shorter wavelength (blue shift).
- **Hyperchromic effect:** increase in absorption intensity (molar absorptivity, ε).
- **Hypochromic effect:** decrease in absorption intensity (molar absorptivity, ε).
- **Absorption maximum (λmax):** the wavelength at which a compound's UV-visible absorption is strongest.
- **Visible spectrum:** the region of the electromagnetic spectrum (~400–700 nm) detectable by the human eye.
- **Fastness:** the resistance of a dyeing to fading or removal under conditions such as washing, light exposure, or rubbing.
- **Mordant:** a metal salt (e.g., Al³⁺, Cr³⁺, Fe³⁺) pre-applied to a substrate to form an insoluble coloured complex ("lake") with a mordant dye, fixing it to the fibre.
- **Dye intermediate:** a compound derived from a raw material by one or more functional-group transformations that serves as a building block in dye synthesis, but is not itself a finished dye.
- **Diazotisation:** conversion of a primary aromatic amine to a diazonium salt using nitrous acid (NaNO₂/HCl) at 0–5 °C.
- **Azo coupling:** electrophilic aromatic substitution of a diazonium salt onto an activated aromatic ring (phenol or amine), forming the –N=N– azo linkage.
- **Substrate:** the material (fibre, paper, tissue, etc.) onto or into which a dye/pigment is applied.
- **Fixation:** the process by which a dye becomes durably attached to (physically entrapped in, ionically bound to, or covalently bonded to) a substrate.

# Part IX — Reaction Schemes to Memorise

## 20. Important Reactions

**1. Nitration of aromatic compounds (benzene → nitrobenzene):**

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_6 + \text{HNO}_3 \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**2. Reduction of nitrobenzene to aniline (Béchamp reduction):**

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NO}_2 + 3\text{Fe} + 6\text{HCl} \longrightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + 3\text{FeCl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**3. Sulfonation of benzene:**

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_6 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_3\text{H} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**4. Diazotisation of aniline:**

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0\text{–}5\,^{\circ}\text{C}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**5. Azo coupling (diazotised aniline + phenol, giving 4-hydroxyazobenzene):**

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow[\text{cold}]{\text{NaOH (mild alkali)}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{–N=N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–OH} \; (\textit{para}) + \text{HCl}$$

**6. Representative dye synthesis — Methyl Orange:**

$$\text{4-H}_2\text{N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–SO}_3\text{H} \xrightarrow[\text{HCl},\ 0\text{–}5\,^{\circ}\text{C}]{\text{NaNO}_2} \text{4-}^+\text{N}_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–SO}_3^- \xrightarrow[\text{cold}]{\ (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{NC}_6\text{H}_5\ } \text{HO}_3\text{S–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–N=N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–N(CH}_3)_2$$

**7. Important intermediate-forming reaction — alkali fusion of naphthalenesulfonic acid to 2-naphthol:**

$$\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_7\text{SO}_3\text{Na} + 2\text{NaOH} \xrightarrow[\text{fusion}]{\Delta} \text{C}_{10}\text{H}_7\text{ONa} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
$$\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_7\text{ONa} + \text{HCl} \longrightarrow \text{C}_{10}\text{H}_7\text{OH (2-naphthol)} + \text{NaCl}$$

# Part X — Exam Preparation

## 21. Important Short Questions

**Q1. Define colour.**
Colour is the visual sensation produced when the eye perceives light remaining after a substance has selectively absorbed certain wavelengths of visible radiation (400–700 nm).

**Q2. Distinguish between a dye and a pigment.**
A dye is soluble in the application medium and has genuine substantivity for a substrate, becoming fixed within it; a pigment is insoluble and is dispersed as particles in a binding medium, held in place by the binder rather than by substrate affinity.

**Q3. What is a chromophore?**
An unsaturated group (e.g., –N=N–, C=O, –C=C–) responsible for a molecule's absorption of visible/UV light.

**Q4. What is an auxochrome?**
A substituent (e.g., –OH, –NH₂, –SO₃H) that modifies the colour/intensity produced by a chromophore, typically by resonance donation/withdrawal, and often assists solubility.

**Q5. Define bathochromic and hypsochromic shift.**
A bathochromic shift is a shift of λmax to longer wavelength; a hypsochromic shift is a shift of λmax to shorter wavelength.

**Q6. What is diazotisation?**
Conversion of a primary aromatic amine to a diazonium salt by treatment with nitrous acid (NaNO₂/HCl) at 0–5 °C.

**Q7. What is a mordant?**
A metal salt pre-applied to a substrate to form an insoluble coloured complex with a mordant dye, fixing the dye to the fibre.

**Q8. Give one example each of an acid dye, a basic dye, and a vat dye.**
Acid dye: C.I. Acid Blue 9. Basic dye: Malachite Green. Vat dye: Indigo.

**Q9. What is a dye intermediate?**
A compound derived from a raw material by chemical transformation, used as a building block in the multi-step synthesis of a finished dye, but not itself a finished dye.

**Q10. Name two non-textile uses of dyes.**
Biological staining (e.g., Crystal Violet in the Gram stain) and acid–base indication (e.g., Methyl Orange).

## 22. Important 5-Mark Questions

**Q1. Discuss the chromophore and auxochrome theories of colour.**
*Model answer:* The chromophore theory (Witt, 1876) states that colour in organic molecules arises from unsaturated groups called chromophores (e.g., –N=N–, C=O, –NO₂, –C=C–). However, a single chromophore alone often gives only weak UV absorption. The auxochrome theory supplements this: auxochromes (e.g., –OH, –NH₂, –NR₂) are electron-donating (or withdrawing) substituents that, when conjugated with a chromophore, shift and intensify absorption — producing bathochromic/hyperchromic effects for donors — thereby converting a weakly UV-absorbing chromophore-bearing molecule into an intensely visibly-coloured compound. Modern electronic theory explains both effects in molecular-orbital terms: auxochromic donation raises the HOMO energy, narrowing the HOMO–LUMO gap and red-shifting absorption.

**Q2. Explain, with examples, how increasing conjugation affects colour.**
*Model answer:* Conjugation is the alternation of single and multiple bonds allowing continuous p-orbital overlap and π-electron delocalisation. As conjugation length increases, the molecular orbitals formed from combining atomic p-orbitals split into progressively closer energy levels, narrowing the HOMO–LUMO gap. Since $E = hc/\lambda$, a smaller gap corresponds to absorption of longer-wavelength (lower-energy) light — a bathochromic shift. This is illustrated by the series ethene (λmax ≈ 165 nm, colourless) → 1,3-butadiene (≈217 nm) → 1,3,5-hexatriene (≈258 nm) → β-carotene (11 conjugated double bonds, λmax ≈ 450–470 nm, visibly orange). The same principle explains why azo dyes, with –N=N– conjugated to aromatic rings and strengthened by amino/hydroxy auxochromes, absorb well into the visible region.

**Q3. Classify dyes according to method of application, giving one example of each.**
*Model answer:* See Section 10.2 table — Acid dyes (C.I. Acid Blue 9, wool/silk, ionic attraction under acidic bath), Basic dyes (Malachite Green, acrylic, ionic attraction to anionic sites), Direct dyes (Congo Red, cotton, hydrogen bonding/van der Waals from neutral bath), Reactive dyes (C.I. Reactive Black 5, cotton, covalent bond formation), Mordant dyes (Alizarin with Al³⁺, wool, metal-complex/lake formation), Vat dyes (Indigo, cotton, reduction–oxidation cycle), Disperse dyes (C.I. Disperse Blue 3, polyester, dissolution/diffusion into hydrophobic fibre), Sulfur dyes (C.I. Sulfur Black 1, cotton, reduction–oxidation cycle), Solvent dyes (C.I. Solvent Yellow 14, plastics/waxes, physical dissolution).

**Q4. Describe the preparation of an azo dye, illustrating with diazotisation and coupling reactions.**
*Model answer:* Azo dyes are prepared in two steps. First, diazotisation: a primary aromatic amine (e.g., sulfanilic acid) is treated with NaNO₂/HCl at 0–5 °C to form a diazonium salt (low temperature is essential for diazonium stability). Second, azo coupling: the diazonium salt reacts as an electrophile with an activated aromatic ring (a phenol under mild alkali, or an amine under mild acid), substituting predominantly at the position para to the activating group, forming the –N=N– azo linkage. For example, diazotised sulfanilic acid coupled with N,N-dimethylaniline gives Methyl Orange. (See Section 11.1 and Section 20 for full equations.)

**Q5. Compare dyes and pigments with respect to solubility, application, and typical use.**
*Model answer:* See Section 18 table and accompanying explanation — the key distinction is that dyes are soluble and interact directly (ionically, covalently, or by hydrogen bonding) with a substrate from solution, whereas pigments are insoluble particulate solids requiring a binding medium to hold them in place, contributing opacity via light scattering as well as absorption.

**Q6. Explain the raw materials and dye intermediates used in azo dye manufacture.**
*Model answer:* Azo dyes derive from simple aromatic raw materials (benzene, toluene, naphthalene) converted through nitration, reduction, and sulfonation into key intermediates: aromatic amines (aniline, naphthylamines, phenylenediamine) that serve as diazo components, and phenols/naphthols (phenol, 1-naphthol, 2-naphthol) and further amines that serve as coupling components. Aromatic sulfonic acids provide the –SO₃H solubilising group present in most water-soluble azo dyes. These intermediates are combined pairwise via diazotisation and coupling (Section 11.1, Section 20) to give the finished azo dye. (See Section 12–15 for full detail.)

**Q7. Describe three non-textile applications of dyes with examples.**
*Model answer:* (1) Biological staining: Crystal Violet (basic triphenylmethane dye) is used in the Gram stain to differentiate bacterial cell wall types by differential dye retention. (2) Analytical chemistry: Methyl Orange (azo dye) functions as a pH indicator, its colour changing (red↔yellow) as its amino auxochrome is protonated/deprotonated around pH 3.1–4.4. (3) Medical diagnostics/optics: Indocyanine Green (a cyanine dye) is used as a near-infrared fluorescent contrast agent to assess blood flow and organ function. (See Section 16 for full treatment.)

**Q8. Discuss the quinonoid theory of colour and its limitations.**
*Model answer:* The quinonoid theory proposes that intense colour in certain dye classes (notably triphenylmethane dyes) arises when a benzenoid ring converts to a quinonoid form — a cross-conjugated ring with two exocyclic double bonds — as seen in the coloured cationic form of Malachite Green versus its colourless "leuco" (benzenoid, sp³-carbon) form. Its main limitation is that it is a structural correlation specific to certain dye classes rather than a general theory; it does not explain colour in simple polyenes or most anthraquinone dyes, and is now understood as one specific case fully explained by the general modern electronic (molecular-orbital) theory of colour. (See Section 4.4.)

## 23. Common Examination Mistakes

- **Confusing chromophores with auxochromes:** a chromophore is the group primarily responsible for absorption (e.g., –N=N–); an auxochrome only modifies that absorption (e.g., –OH). Students often list –OH or –NH₂ as chromophores — this is incorrect.
- **Confusing dyes with pigments:** the defining distinction is solubility/substrate affinity (dye) versus insolubility/binder-dependence (pigment), not merely "used in textiles" versus "used in paint."
- **Confusing structural classification with application classification:** "azo dye" describes chemical structure; "direct dye" describes application method. A single dye (e.g., Congo Red) can and often does belong to both an application class and a structural class simultaneously.
- **Incorrect diazotisation conditions:** diazotisation must be carried out at **0–5 °C**; students frequently forget the low-temperature requirement, which is essential because diazonium salts decompose above this range.
- **Incorrect azo-coupling position:** coupling occurs predominantly **para** to the activating auxochrome on the coupling component (or ortho if para is blocked) — not at a random ring position.
- **Incorrect dye structures:** e.g., drawing indigo without its correct two-indoxyl/central C=C arrangement, or omitting the anthraquinone's two carbonyl groups.
- **Calling every coloured compound a dye:** a compound must have substrate affinity, appropriate solubility, and adequate fastness to be classified as a practical dye (Section 7); merely absorbing visible light is not sufficient.
- **Confusing dye intermediates with raw materials:** raw materials (benzene, toluene, naphthalene) are simple bulk aromatics; dye intermediates (aniline, naphthols, sulfonic acids, diazonium salts) are the more functionalised compounds derived from raw materials that are used directly in dye synthesis.
- **Using incorrect examples:** e.g., citing a food dye as an example of a reactive dye, or citing a pigment (e.g., TiO₂) as an example of a dye — always verify the example matches the specific class being discussed.

## 24. Quick Revision Sheet

### Colour

- Core concepts: colour = perception of light remaining after selective absorption of visible wavelengths (400–700 nm); governed by electronic transitions between HOMO and LUMO.
- Key equations: $c = \lambda\nu$; $E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$.
- Theories: Chromophore theory (Witt) → Auxochrome theory → Modern electronic (MO) theory → Quinonoid theory (special case, historical).
- Key terminology: chromophore, auxochrome, bathochromic/hypsochromic shift, hyperchromic/hypochromic effect, λmax.

### Dyes

- Definition: soluble, substantive, coloured compound applied from solution and fixed to a substrate with adequate fastness.
- Structure: chromophore + auxochrome(s) + conjugated system + solubilising group(s) (–SO₃H, –COOH, –NR₃⁺).
- Classification: by **structure** (azo, triphenylmethane, anthraquinone, indigoid, phthalocyanine, sulfur, natural, etc.) and by **application** (acid, basic, direct, reactive, mordant, vat, disperse, sulfur, solvent).
- Major examples: Congo Red (azo/direct), Methyl Orange (azo/acid-indicator), Malachite Green (triphenylmethane/basic), Indigo (indigoid/vat), Alizarin (anthraquinone/mordant), Reactive Black 5 (azo/reactive).

### Dye Manufacture

- Raw materials: benzene, toluene, naphthalene, anthracene, phenol, aniline.
- Important intermediates: aniline, naphthols, naphthylamines, aromatic sulfonic acids, diazonium salts.
- Key reactions: nitration, reduction, sulfonation, diazotisation, azo coupling, condensation.

### Applications

- Textile: fibre dyeing via the application classes above.
- Non-textile: food colouring (approved dyes only), biological staining (Crystal Violet, H&E), medical diagnostics (Indocyanine Green), ink/printing, photographic sensitisers (cyanine dyes), pH indicators (Methyl Orange, Phenolphthalein), fluorescent labelling (fluorescein, rhodamine), dye lasers (Rhodamine 6G), cosmetics (approved dyes only).

### Pigments

- Definition: insoluble, particulate coloured solid dispersed in a binder.
- Types: organic (azo pigments, phthalocyanine pigments) and inorganic (TiO₂, iron oxides, ultramarine).
- Examples: Titanium dioxide (white), iron oxides (red/yellow/brown/black), Copper Phthalocyanine (blue/green).
- Difference from dyes: insoluble vs soluble; particle-dispersed-in-binder vs molecularly dissolved-and-fixed; opacity via light scattering vs generally transparent; no substrate affinity required vs substantivity essential.
