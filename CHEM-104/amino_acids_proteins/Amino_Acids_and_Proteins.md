## 1. Introduction

**Amino acids** are organic compounds that contain both a **basic amino group (–NH₂)** and an **acidic carboxyl group (–COOH)** within the same molecule. They are important as the fundamental structural units from which **peptides** and **proteins** are built, and they also participate independently in metabolism (e.g., as precursors of neurotransmitters, hormones, and nucleotides).

**Relationship between amino acids, peptides, and proteins:** individual amino acids are joined together through **peptide bonds** (amide linkages) to form chains. Short chains are called **peptides**; longer chains are called **polypeptides**; and one or more polypeptide chains folded into a specific three-dimensional structure constitute a **protein**. This progression is summarised as:

$$\text{Amino acids} \longrightarrow \text{Peptides} \longrightarrow \text{Polypeptides} \longrightarrow \text{Proteins}$$

**General structure of an α-amino acid:**

$$\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R){-}COOH}$$

- **α-carbon:** the carbon atom immediately adjacent to the carboxyl carbon; it bears the amino group, the carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and the side chain (R group). It is called "α" because it is the carbon directly next to (in the α-position relative to) the carboxylic acid functional group.
- **Amino group (–NH₂):** the basic functional group attached to the α-carbon.
- **Carboxyl group (–COOH):** the acidic functional group attached to the α-carbon.
- **Hydrogen atom (–H):** attached to the α-carbon in all amino acids.
- **Variable R group (side chain):** the group that differs between the twenty standard amino acids and determines each amino acid's individual chemical character (polarity, charge, aromaticity, etc.).

**Zwitterionic form:** in aqueous solution near neutral pH, an amino acid does not exist predominantly as the neutral form shown above. Instead, the carboxyl group loses a proton and the amino group gains one, giving the **zwitterion** (a dipolar ion bearing both a positive and a negative charge, with zero net charge overall):

$$\mathrm{^{+}H_3N{-}CH(R){-}COO^{-}}$$

Amino acids are therefore **amphoteric** compounds — they can act as either an acid (donating a proton from –NH₃⁺) or a base (accepting a proton at –COO⁻) — and the predominant ionic form present in solution depends on the pH (Section 6, Section 8).

# Part I — Classification of Amino Acids

## 2. Classification of Amino Acids

### 2.1 Classification According to Position of the Amino Group

Amino acids can be classified by how far the amino group is located from the carboxyl group along the carbon chain:

- **α-amino acids:** the amino group is on the carbon atom immediately adjacent to the carboxyl group (the α-carbon). General structure: $\mathrm{R{-}CH(NH_2){-}COOH}$. Example: alanine, $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH(NH_2){-}COOH}$.
- **β-amino acids:** the amino group is on the second carbon from the carboxyl group. General structure: $\mathrm{R{-}CH(NH_2){-}CH_2{-}COOH}$. Example: β-alanine, $\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}COOH}$ (a component of pantothenic acid and coenzyme A).
- **γ-amino acids:** the amino group is on the third carbon from the carboxyl group. Example: γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), $\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}COOH}$, an important inhibitory neurotransmitter.
- **δ-amino acids:** the amino group is on the fourth carbon from the carboxyl group. Example: δ-aminolevulinic acid, a precursor in porphyrin (haem) biosynthesis.

> **Key point:** the amino acids that are incorporated into proteins via peptide-bond formation (the twenty standard "proteinogenic" amino acids, with the structural exception of proline, which is technically an **imino acid** — see Section 2.5) are overwhelmingly **α-amino acids**. β-, γ-, and δ-amino acids occur naturally but are not building blocks of standard proteins.

### 2.2 Classification According to Number of Amino and Carboxyl Groups

**Neutral amino acids** — one amino group and one carboxyl group; the side chain carries no additional ionisable acidic or strongly basic group.
*Examples:* Glycine ($\mathrm{H_2NCH_2COOH}$), Alanine ($\mathrm{CH_3CH(NH_2)COOH}$), Valine ($\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH(NH_2)COOH}$).

**Acidic amino acids** — one amino group and **two** carboxyl groups (an additional carboxylic acid group on the side chain).
*Examples:* Aspartic acid, $\mathrm{HOOC{-}CH_2{-}CH(NH_2){-}COOH}$; Glutamic acid, $\mathrm{HOOC{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}CH(NH_2){-}COOH}$.

**Basic amino acids** — one carboxyl group and **two or more** basic nitrogen-containing groups.
*Examples:* Lysine, $\mathrm{H_2N{-}(CH_2)_4{-}CH(NH_2){-}COOH}$ (a second primary amino group on the side chain); Arginine, containing a **guanidino** side-chain group; Histidine, containing an **imidazole** ring side chain.

### 2.3 Classification According to Side-Chain Character

A more chemically informative classification groups the twenty standard amino acids by the polarity, charge, and functional character of the side chain:

- **Non-polar/hydrophobic:** side chains are aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbon groups with no significant polarity — glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, phenylalanine, methionine, tryptophan.
- **Polar, uncharged:** side chains contain a polar but non-ionisable (at physiological pH) group such as –OH or –CONH₂ — serine, threonine, cysteine, tyrosine, asparagine, glutamine.
- **Acidic (negatively charged at physiological pH):** aspartic acid, glutamic acid.
- **Basic (positively charged at physiological pH):** lysine, arginine, histidine (histidine's imidazole side chain has a pKa close to physiological pH and is only partially protonated).
- **Aromatic:** phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan (histidine's imidazole ring is also aromatic, though histidine is usually classified functionally with the basic amino acids).
- **Sulfur-containing:** cysteine (–SH, thiol), methionine (–S–CH₃, thioether).
- **Hydroxyl-containing:** serine, threonine, tyrosine (the latter via its phenolic –OH).

### 2.4 Essential and Non-essential Amino Acids

- **Essential amino acids:** those that the human body **cannot synthesise (or cannot synthesise in sufficient quantity)** and which must therefore be obtained from the diet. The nine essential amino acids for humans are: **Histidine, Isoleucine, Leucine, Lysine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Valine.**
- **Non-essential amino acids:** those that the human body can synthesise in adequate amounts from other metabolic precursors, so dietary intake is not strictly required. Examples: alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, serine.
- **Conditionally essential amino acids:** normally synthesisable by the body, but required from the diet under certain physiological conditions (e.g., illness, infancy, metabolic stress) when biosynthetic capacity is insufficient. Examples: arginine, cysteine, glutamine, glycine, proline, tyrosine.

> **Important clarification:** "essential" here refers strictly to **dietary requirement in humans** — it is a nutritional classification, not a statement about an amino acid's chemical importance, biological significance, or frequency of occurrence in proteins. A "non-essential" amino acid (e.g., glutamic acid) can be just as biochemically important as an "essential" one.

### 2.5 Proteinogenic and Non-proteinogenic Amino Acids

**Proteinogenic amino acids** are the amino acids that are genetically encoded and incorporated into proteins during ribosomal translation — the twenty standard amino acids listed in the table above (a small number of additional proteinogenic amino acids, selenocysteine and pyrrolysine, are incorporated in specific organisms via specialised mechanisms, but are outside the standard undergraduate list).

**Non-proteinogenic amino acids** are amino acids that occur naturally (often as metabolic intermediates or signalling molecules) but are **not** incorporated into proteins via the standard genetic/ribosomal machinery. Examples:

- **γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA):** a γ-amino acid (Section 2.1) that functions as the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system.
- **Ornithine:** an intermediate of the urea cycle, structurally related to lysine but with one fewer methylene group.
- **Citrulline:** another urea-cycle intermediate, formed from ornithine.

> Proline is sometimes noted separately here: although it **is** proteinogenic (one of the standard twenty), it is structurally an **imino acid** (secondary amine, because its nitrogen is part of a five-membered pyrrolidine ring and is bonded to two carbons rather than bearing two free N–H bonds), not a primary-amine α-amino acid in the strict sense. This distinction affects its behaviour in some reactions (e.g., the ninhydrin test, Section 9).

# Part II — Nomenclature of Amino Acids

## 3. Nomenclature

### 3.1 Common Names

Most amino acids are, in routine use, referred to by their traditional **common (trivial) names** rather than systematic IUPAC names, e.g., glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, serine, tyrosine. These names are historically derived (often from the natural source from which the amino acid was first isolated, e.g., "glycine" from the Greek *glykys*, "sweet," reflecting its sweet taste; "asparagine" from asparagus) and are universally used in biochemistry.

### 3.2 IUPAC Nomenclature

Systematic IUPAC names treat the amino acid as a substituted carboxylic acid:

1. The **parent chain** is chosen as the longest carbon chain that includes the carboxyl carbon, named as the corresponding carboxylic acid (e.g., propanoic acid, butanoic acid).
2. **Numbering** begins at the carboxyl carbon (C-1); the α-carbon bearing the amino group is therefore **C-2**.
3. The amino group is cited as a **2-amino** substituent prefix.
4. Any further substituents from the side chain are named and numbered in the usual systematic way.

**Example:**

$$\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH(NH_2){-}COOH} \;=\; \textbf{2-aminopropanoic acid (alanine)}$$

Further examples: glycine = aminoacetic acid (2-aminoethanoic acid); valine = 2-amino-3-methylbutanoic acid; aspartic acid = 2-aminobutanedioic acid; lysine = 2,6-diaminohexanoic acid.

### 3.4 D/L and R/S Nomenclature

**D/L system:** a configurational naming system, historically related to the reference compound **glyceraldehyde**. The two enantiomers of glyceraldehyde were assigned **D** and **L** by convention (based on the Fischer-projection position of the –OH group on the reference stereocentre); other chiral molecules, including amino acids, are then assigned D or L by structural analogy — comparing the Fischer projection of the amino acid (drawn with the carboxyl group at the top) to that of D- or L-glyceraldehyde, based on which side the amino group (–NH₂) appears on. In naturally occurring amino acids, the α-amino group appears on the **left** in the standard Fischer projection, which by this convention defines the **L-series**. Essentially all amino acids found in natural proteins are **L-amino acids**.

**R/S (CIP) system:** an absolute configurational descriptor based on the **Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) priority rules**, assigned by ranking the four substituents on a stereocentre by atomic number (and, where needed, further shells) and determining whether the sequence of decreasing priority is clockwise (**R**) or anticlockwise (**S**) when viewed with the lowest-priority group pointing away from the viewer.

**Why D/L and R/S are not interchangeable:** D/L is a *relative* configurational label tied to a single historical reference compound (glyceraldehyde) and does not, by itself, indicate the actual spatial priority ranking of a given molecule's substituents. R/S is an *absolute*, substituent-priority-based label that must be worked out independently for every distinct compound, because it depends on the specific atoms attached to the stereocentre. Two compounds can both be "L" by the glyceraldehyde-relational convention yet have **different** R/S descriptors if their substituent priority orders differ.

**Important exceptions and points to verify carefully:**

- For most L-amino acids, the CIP priority order at the α-carbon is $\mathrm{NH_2 > COOH > R > H}$, and this order corresponds to the **S** configuration. For example, **L-alanine = (S)-alanine**.
- **L-Cysteine is the standard undergraduate exception: L-cysteine = (R)-cysteine.** This occurs because cysteine's side chain is $\mathrm{-CH_2SH}$: at the first point of difference in CIP comparison, the side-chain carbon is attached to a **sulfur** atom (atomic number 16), which outranks the **oxygen** atoms (atomic number 8) attached to the carboxyl carbon. This reverses the relative priority of the side chain and the carboxyl group compared with the other amino acids (giving the order $\mathrm{NH_2 > CH_2SH > COOH > H}$), which flips the CIP descriptor from S to R **even though the actual spatial (three-dimensional) arrangement of atoms is the same L-configuration found in all other natural amino acids.**
- **Glycine has no R/S configuration**, because its α-carbon bears **two identical substituents** (two hydrogen atoms, since its "side chain" is simply –H); a carbon with two identical substituents is not a stereocentre, so glycine is **achiral** and has neither a D/L nor an R/S designation (see Section 4.1).

> **Do not equate D/L configuration with R/S configuration** — always check each amino acid's CIP priorities individually rather than assuming L always equals S.

# Part III — Optical Activity

## 4. Optical Activity of Amino Acids

**Chirality:** a molecule is **chiral** if it is non-superimposable on its mirror image, typically because it contains a **chiral centre** (a carbon atom bonded to four different groups).

**Enantiomers:** a pair of stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of one another; they have identical physical properties (melting point, solubility, etc.) except for their interaction with **plane-polarised light** and with other chiral environments (e.g., enzymes, chiral reagents).

**Plane-polarised light:** ordinary light restricted (e.g., by a polariser) so that its electric field oscillates in a single plane.

**Optical activity:** the property of a chiral compound (or a solution containing an excess of one enantiomer) of **rotating the plane of plane-polarised light** as it passes through the sample.

**Polarimeter:** the instrument used to measure the angle through which a sample rotates plane-polarised light (the **observed rotation**, α).

**Specific rotation** is defined as:

$$[\alpha]_{\lambda}^{T} = \frac{\alpha}{l \, c}$$

where:
- $[\alpha]_{\lambda}^{T}$ = specific rotation at temperature *T* and wavelength λ (commonly the sodium D-line, giving $[\alpha]_D^{25}$, for example)
- $\alpha$ = observed rotation (degrees), as measured on the polarimeter
- $l$ = path length of the sample cell (usually expressed in decimetres, dm)
- $c$ = concentration of the solution (usually expressed in g mL⁻¹, or equivalent standardised units)

Specific rotation is an intrinsic physical constant for a given pure enantiomer under specified conditions, allowing comparison between different compounds and different measurements.

### 4.1 Optical Activity and Amino-Acid Structure

Most α-amino acids contain a **chiral α-carbon**, because it is typically bonded to four different groups: the amino group, the carboxyl group, the side chain (R), and a hydrogen atom. Since R differs from –H, –NH₂, and –COOH for every standard amino acid except glycine, the α-carbon of these amino acids is a genuine stereocentre, giving rise to two possible enantiomers (**D**- and **L**-forms).

**Glycine is the sole exception.** Its side chain is a second hydrogen atom, so its α-carbon is bonded to **two identical hydrogen atoms** (–NH₂, –COOH, –H, –H) — it is therefore **not** a stereocentre, and **glycine is achiral and optically inactive.**

Naturally occurring proteins are built almost exclusively from **L-amino acids** (with glycine, which has no D/L distinction, present as well). D-amino acids do occur in nature (notably in some bacterial cell walls and certain peptide antibiotics) but are not found in standard ribosomally synthesised proteins.

> A Fischer projection of a generic L-α-amino acid places the carboxyl group at the top, the R group at the bottom, the amino group on the **left**, and the hydrogen on the **right** of the central (α) carbon — directly analogous to the arrangement used to define L-glyceraldehyde.

### 4.2 D/L Configuration vs (+)/(−) Rotation

This distinction is essential and frequently confused:

- **D/L** describes a molecule's **configuration** — the spatial arrangement of its substituents, assigned by structural relationship to glyceraldehyde (Section 3.4). It is determined by chemical structure alone and does not depend on any experimental measurement.
- **(+)/(−)** (equivalently, *d*/*l*, dextrorotatory/laevorotatory) describes the **experimentally observed direction** in which a compound rotates plane-polarised light: **(+)** means the plane is rotated **clockwise** (as viewed facing the oncoming light), and **(−)** means it is rotated **anticlockwise**. This is a measured physical property, obtained directly from a polarimeter.

**D does not necessarily mean (+), and L does not necessarily mean (−).** The relationship between configuration (D/L or R/S) and the sign of optical rotation ((+)/(−)) is **not fixed by any general rule** — it depends on the specific electronic and structural environment of each individual molecule and must be determined experimentally for each compound. For example:

- **L-Alanine** is dextrorotatory, i.e., **L-alanine = (+)-alanine** (specifically (S)-(+)-alanine).
- **L-Leucine** is laevorotatory, i.e., **L-leucine = (−)-leucine.**
- **L-Cysteine** is laevorotatory in some standard reference conditions, i.e., commonly cited as **L-cysteine = (−)-cysteine** (specifically (R)-(−)-cysteine).

> **Rule to remember for examinations:** always keep D/L (or R/S) — a structural/configurational label — conceptually and notationally separate from (+)/(−) — an experimentally measured optical-rotation sign. Never assume one from the other without either a lookup table or an explicit calculation/measurement.

### 4.3 Racemic Mixture

A **racemic mixture** (or racemate) is a mixture containing **exactly equal amounts (50:50)** of two enantiomers of a chiral compound.

- Because the optical rotation contributed by each enantiomer is equal in magnitude but opposite in sign, the rotations **cancel exactly**, and a racemic mixture shows **zero net optical rotation** — it is **optically inactive**, even though it is composed entirely of individually chiral, optically active molecules.
- **Racemisation** is the process by which a single, optically pure enantiomer is converted (partially or completely) into a 50:50 mixture of both enantiomers, typically via a mechanism that temporarily destroys the stereocentre (e.g., enolisation at the α-carbon under acidic or basic conditions, which is why amino acids can racemise under harsh acid/base treatment).

**Why laboratory synthesis from achiral starting materials typically gives racemic amino acids:** if the starting materials and reagents used in a synthesis are themselves achiral (or if the reaction proceeds through an achiral planar intermediate, such as an imine or an enolate at the future stereocentre), then there is no chiral influence to favour formation of one enantiomer over the other. Both faces of the planar intermediate are equally accessible to attack, so the two enantiomeric products are formed in **equal amounts**, giving a racemic mixture. To obtain a single enantiomer preferentially, an **asymmetric (enantioselective)** method must be used — one that introduces a chiral influence (a chiral catalyst, chiral auxiliary, chiral reagent, or an enzyme) at the bond-forming step (Section 20).

# Part IV — Physical and Chemical Properties

## 5. Physical Properties of Amino Acids

- **Physical state and crystalline nature:** amino acids are typically obtained as **white crystalline solids** at room temperature.
- **Melting/decomposition behaviour:** amino acids generally have **high melting points**, and most **decompose** (char) at or near their melting point rather than melting cleanly, typically in the range of roughly 200–300 °C.
- **Solubility:** amino acids are generally **soluble in water** (and other polar solvents) but are **poorly soluble or insoluble in non-polar organic solvents** (e.g., diethyl ether, benzene, hexane).
- **Polarity and dipole moments:** amino acids possess substantial **dipole moments** and behave, in the solid state and in solution near neutral pH, as **ionic (zwitterionic) species** rather than as simple neutral organic molecules.

**Why amino acids resemble ionic compounds more than simple amines or carboxylic acids:** in the solid crystal and in neutral aqueous solution, amino acids exist predominantly as the **zwitterion**, $\mathrm{^{+}H_3NCH(R)COO^{-}}$ (Section 6) — a dipolar species held together in the crystal lattice by strong electrostatic (ionic) forces, analogous to an inorganic salt. This is fundamentally different from a simple amine (e.g., methylamine, a volatile liquid with a comparatively low melting point) or a simple carboxylic acid (e.g., acetic acid, also a low-melting liquid), neither of which is internally ionised in this way. The ionic, salt-like character of the zwitterion explains amino acids' characteristically **high melting/decomposition points**, **high water solubility**, and **low solubility in non-polar organic solvents**, all of which are properties typical of ionic solids rather than of simple neutral organic amines or acids.

## 6. Zwitterions

$$\mathrm{H_2N{-}CHR{-}COOH} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{^{+}H_3N{-}CHR{-}COO^{-}}$$

This equilibrium represents an **internal (intramolecular) acid–base reaction**: the more acidic –COOH group transfers its proton to the more basic –NH₂ group **within the same molecule**, without any external acid or base being required. The product, bearing both a positive charge (on nitrogen) and a negative charge (on oxygen) with an overall net charge of zero, is called a **zwitterion** (from German *Zwitter*, "hybrid"). At physiological/neutral pH, the zwitterionic form is overwhelmingly the predominant species for a simple neutral amino acid in aqueous solution.

Because an amino acid can donate a proton (from the –NH₃⁺ of the zwitterion) or accept a proton (at the –COO⁻ of the zwitterion), it displays **amphoteric behaviour** — the same defining property already introduced in Section 1.

**Ionic forms present at different pH values (for a simple neutral amino acid):**

- **Strongly acidic solution (low pH):** both functional groups are protonated; the amino acid exists predominantly as the **fully protonated cation**, $\mathrm{^{+}H_3N{-}CHR{-}COOH}$ (net charge +1).
- **Neutral solution (pH close to the isoelectric point):** the amino acid exists predominantly as the **zwitterion**, $\mathrm{^{+}H_3N{-}CHR{-}COO^{-}}$ (net charge 0).
- **Strongly basic solution (high pH):** both functional groups are deprotonated; the amino acid exists predominantly as the **fully deprotonated anion**, $\mathrm{H_2N{-}CHR{-}COO^{-}}$ (net charge −1).

## 7. Isoelectric Point

The **isoelectric point (pI)** is the pH at which a given amino acid (or, more generally, a peptide or protein) carries **zero net electrical charge** — i.e., the pH at which the molecule exists predominantly in its neutral **zwitterionic** form (for a simple amino acid) or, more generally, the pH at which the sum of all positive and negative charges on the molecule is exactly zero.

**Relation between pKa values and pI:**

For a **simple neutral amino acid** (one with only the α-carboxyl and α-amino groups as ionisable centres, no ionisable side chain), the isoelectric point lies exactly midway between the two relevant pKa values — $pK_{a1}$ (for deprotonation of the α-carboxyl group) and $pK_{a2}$ (for deprotonation of the α-ammonium group):

$$pI = \frac{pK_{a1}+pK_{a2}}{2}$$

**Why the formula must be modified for amino acids with an ionisable side chain:** for **acidic** amino acids (an extra ionisable –COOH on the side chain, e.g., aspartic acid, glutamic acid) or **basic** amino acids (an extra ionisable basic group on the side chain, e.g., lysine, arginine, histidine), there are **three** relevant pKa values rather than two. The pI must then be calculated as the average of the **two pKa values that bracket the pH range in which the neutral (zero-net-charge) zwitterionic species predominates** — not simply the average of all three pKa values.

- For an **acidic** amino acid (three groups: α-COOH, side-chain –COOH, α-NH₃⁺, with two acidic pKa values lower than the amino pKa), the neutral species lies **between the two carboxyl-group pKa values**, so:
$$pI = \frac{pK_{a1}+pK_{a2}}{2} \quad (\text{averaging the two carboxyl pKa values})$$
- For a **basic** amino acid (two basic pKa values higher than the single carboxyl pKa), the neutral species lies **between the two amino-group pKa values**, so:
$$pI = \frac{pK_{a2}+pK_{a3}}{2} \quad (\text{averaging the two amino-group pKa values})$$

**Worked examples:**

- **Glycine** (representative pKa₁ ≈ 2.34 for –COOH, pKa₂ ≈ 9.60 for –NH₃⁺):
$$pI = \frac{2.34 + 9.60}{2} = 5.97$$

- **Aspartic acid** (representative pKa₁ ≈ 1.88 for α-COOH, pKa₂ ≈ 3.65 for side-chain –COOH, pKa₃ ≈ 9.60 for α-NH₃⁺): the two lowest pKa values (both carboxyl groups) bracket the neutral zwitterion, so:
$$pI = \frac{1.88 + 3.65}{2} = 2.77$$
(Aspartic acid's pI is low, consistent with its net negative charge at physiological pH.)

- **Lysine** (representative pKa₁ ≈ 2.18 for α-COOH, pKa₂ ≈ 8.95 for α-NH₃⁺, pKa₃ ≈ 10.53 for side-chain –NH₃⁺): the two highest pKa values (both amino groups) bracket the neutral zwitterion, so:
$$pI = \frac{8.95 + 10.53}{2} = 9.74$$
(Lysine's pI is high, consistent with its net positive charge at physiological pH.)

> **Note:** exact pKa values reported for amino acids vary slightly between textbooks/sources depending on measurement conditions; the calculation *method* shown above (identify the two pKa values bracketing the neutral species, then average them) is the standard, examinable procedure, and is what should be reproduced regardless of the specific numerical pKa values supplied in a given problem.

# Part V — Chemical Reactions of Amino Acids

## 8. Acid-Base Reactions

Because amino acids are amphoteric, the zwitterion can react further with either an added acid or an added base:

**Reaction with acid** (protonation of the carboxylate):

$$\mathrm{^{+}H_3NCHRCOO^{-} + H^{+} \longrightarrow \ ^{+}H_3NCHRCOOH}$$

**Reaction with base** (deprotonation of the ammonium ion):

$$\mathrm{^{+}H_3NCHRCOO^{-} + OH^{-} \longrightarrow H_2NCHRCOO^{-} + H_2O}$$

These reactions show explicitly how the amino acid moves between its three principal ionic forms (cationic, zwitterionic, anionic — Section 6) as the pH of the surrounding solution changes.

## 9. Reaction with Ninhydrin

**Principle:** ninhydrin (triketohydrindene hydrate) reacts with the free **α-amino group** of amino acids (and, more generally, with free amino groups at the N-terminus of peptides) under mildly acidic, heated conditions. The amino acid is oxidatively deaminated and decarboxylated by ninhydrin, and the reduced ninhydrin product then condenses with a second molecule of ninhydrin and the liberated ammonia to form a deep **blue-purple** coloured product known as **Ruhemann's purple**.

**Use:** the ninhydrin test is a standard, highly sensitive **qualitative and semi-quantitative test for the detection of α-amino acids** (and free amino groups generally), widely used for visualising amino acid spots on paper/thin-layer chromatograms and in amino-acid analysers.

**Special behaviour of imino acids (proline, hydroxyproline):** because proline's nitrogen is a **secondary amine** (part of its pyrrolidine ring, Section 2.5) rather than a primary amine, it reacts with ninhydrin via a different pathway that does **not** produce the typical blue-purple Ruhemann's purple; instead, proline (and hydroxyproline) characteristically give a distinctive **yellow** colour with ninhydrin. This is a well-known, examinable exception to the general blue-purple ninhydrin colour reaction.

## 10. Reaction with Formaldehyde

Formaldehyde reacts with the free **amino group** of an amino acid to form a **hydroxymethyl / methylol derivative** (and, with excess formaldehyde, further N,N-dimethylol-type products), which effectively "masks" or removes the basicity of the amino group by tying up its lone pair in a hemiaminal-type adduct rather than allowing it to remain a free, basic –NH₂/–NH₃⁺ group.

**Analytical significance — the Sørensen formaldehyde (formol) titration:** in the free zwitterion, the protonated ammonium group (–NH₃⁺) is not readily titratable directly against a base because it is a comparatively weak acid, and its titration end point is masked by the presence of the more strongly acidic carboxyl group's conjugate behaviour. By first reacting the amino group with formaldehyde, the basicity of the nitrogen is effectively suppressed, "liberating" or unmasking the acidic character of the carboxyl group so that it can be **titrated accurately against standard sodium hydroxide** using a normal acid–base indicator. This formaldehyde (formol) titration method is used to determine the amino-acid content of a sample quantitatively.

## 11. Reaction with Nitrous Acid

$$\mathrm{RCH(NH_2)COOH + HNO_2 \longrightarrow RCH(OH)COOH + N_2\uparrow + H_2O}$$

**Deamination:** the primary α-amino group is converted, via a diazonium-ion-type intermediate (analogous to the diazotisation of a primary aliphatic amine), into a hydroxyl group, with loss of **nitrogen gas**. This reaction — sometimes called the **Van Slyke reaction/method** — converts the amino acid into the corresponding **α-hydroxy acid**.

**Analytical significance:** because exactly one mole of N₂ gas is evolved per mole of free primary amino group present, the volume of nitrogen gas liberated can be measured and used to **quantify the amount of free α-amino nitrogen** in a sample — a classical quantitative method for amino-acid/protein amino-nitrogen determination.

## 12. Esterification

$$\mathrm{RCH(NH_2)COOH + R'OH \; \underset{}{\overset{\text{H}^+}{\rightleftharpoons}} \; RCH(NH_2)COOR' + H_2O}$$

The carboxyl group of an amino acid undergoes normal **Fischer esterification** with an alcohol under acid catalysis (e.g., dry HCl gas in the alcohol, or with a mineral acid catalyst), forming the corresponding amino-acid ester. Esterification is reversible, and the equilibrium is driven towards the ester by using excess alcohol and/or removing water.

**Why amino groups may require protection:** because the free amino group is nucleophilic and basic, it can interfere with reactions intended to occur selectively at the carboxyl group (or vice versa) during multi-step synthetic sequences (e.g., peptide synthesis or the synthetic routes in Part VI). To avoid unwanted side reactions, the amino group is frequently first converted to a **protecting group** (e.g., an N-acyl derivative, Section 13, or other standard protecting groups such as Boc or Cbz) that can later be selectively removed once the desired carboxyl-group chemistry has been completed.

## 13. Reaction with Acylating Agents

$$\mathrm{RCH(NH_2)COOH + (CH_3CO)_2O \longrightarrow RCH(NHCOCH_3)COOH + CH_3COOH}$$

**Acylation of the amino group:** the nucleophilic amino group reacts with an acid anhydride (e.g., acetic anhydride, shown above) or an acid chloride to form an **N-acyl amino acid** (here, an N-acetyl amino acid), converting the basic, nucleophilic free amine into a much less basic, non-nucleophilic **amide**.

**Importance of protecting groups in synthesis:** N-acylation is one of the standard ways of "protecting" the amino group of an amino acid so that it will not interfere as a competing nucleophile/base during subsequent reactions carried out at the carboxyl group or elsewhere in the molecule (this is exactly the strategy exploited in the amidomalonate synthesis, Section 18, where the amino group is introduced already in its N-acetyl-protected form).

## 14. Decarboxylation

$$\mathrm{RCH(NH_2)COOH \longrightarrow RCH_2NH_2 + CO_2\uparrow}$$

Loss of the carboxyl group as carbon dioxide (typically requiring heating, or, biologically, catalysis by a **decarboxylase enzyme** with a pyridoxal phosphate cofactor) converts an α-amino acid into the corresponding **primary amine**, generally with one fewer carbon in the chain relative to the parent acid (since the carboxyl carbon itself is lost as CO₂).

**Representative, verified examples:**

- **Histidine → Histamine:** decarboxylation of histidine's α-carboxyl group gives histamine, $\mathrm{HO_2C\text{-}CH(NH_2)\text{-}CH_2\text{-}(imidazolyl)} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2N\text{-}CH_2\text{-}CH_2\text{-}(imidazolyl)} + \mathrm{CO_2}$, an important mediator of allergic and inflammatory responses.
- **Glutamic acid → GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid):** decarboxylation of glutamic acid's **α**-carboxyl group (leaving the side-chain carboxyl and the amino group intact) gives GABA, $\mathrm{HOOC(CH_2)_2CH(NH_2)COOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{HOOC(CH_2)_2CH_2NH_2} + \mathrm{CO_2}$ — consistent with the γ-amino acid structure of GABA already introduced in Section 2.1.

# Part VI — Synthesis of Amino Acids

## 15. General Principles of Amino-Acid Synthesis

The chemical interest in α-amino acid synthesis centres on two linked challenges:

- **Formation of the C–N bond** at (or adjacent to) the future α-carbon, introducing the amino group at the correct position relative to the carboxyl group.
- **Control of stereochemistry:** because the α-carbon is normally a stereocentre (Section 4.1), any synthetic method that generates this centre from an achiral (planar, e.g., sp²-hybridised) precursor will, in the absence of a chiral influence, generate **both enantiomers in equal amounts** — i.e., a **racemic** product (Section 4.3). Producing a single desired enantiomer requires either a subsequent resolution step or a genuinely **enantioselective (asymmetric)** synthetic method (Section 20).
- **Protecting groups** (Section 12, Section 13) are frequently required during synthesis to prevent the amino and carboxyl groups from interfering with each other or with other reagents at intermediate stages.

## 16. Synthesis by α-Halo Acid Method (Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky Route)

**General sequence:**

$$\text{Carboxylic acid} \xrightarrow{\alpha\text{-halogenation}} \alpha\text{-Halo acid} \xrightarrow{\text{ammonolysis}} \alpha\text{-Amino acid}$$

**Step 1 — α-Halogenation (Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky reaction):** a carboxylic acid is treated with **bromine (Br₂) in the presence of a catalytic amount of red phosphorus (or PBr₃)**, which brominates the acid specifically at the **α-carbon** (via the acid bromide/enol mechanism characteristic of the HVZ reaction):

$$\mathrm{RCH_2COOH \xrightarrow[\text{P (red)}]{Br_2} RCHBrCOOH + HBr}$$

**Step 2 — Ammonolysis:** the resulting α-halo acid is then treated with **excess aqueous or ethanolic ammonia**, which displaces the halide by nucleophilic substitution to install the amino group at the α-position:

$$\mathrm{RCHBrCOOH + 2NH_3 \longrightarrow RCH(NH_2)COOH + NH_4Br}$$

**Representative example:** propanoic acid → 2-bromopropanoic acid (via HVZ bromination) → alanine (via ammonolysis).

**Stereochemical outcome:** because the nucleophilic substitution of ammonia on the α-halo acid proceeds without any chiral influence, the product amino acid is obtained as a **racemic (DL) mixture**.

## 17. Strecker Synthesis

A classical and mechanistically instructive route to α-amino acids from an aldehyde.

**Overall scheme:**

$$\mathrm{RCHO \longrightarrow RCH(NH_2)CN \longrightarrow RCH(NH_2)COOH}$$

**Step-by-step mechanism:**

1. **Imine (Schiff base) formation:** the aldehyde reacts with ammonia (or ammonium chloride, providing $\mathrm{NH_3}$/$\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ in equilibrium) to form an **imine** intermediate, $\mathrm{RCH{=}NH}$, via nucleophilic addition of ammonia to the carbonyl followed by loss of water.
2. **Cyanide addition:** cyanide ion (from added KCN or NaCN, or HCN itself) adds to the electrophilic imine carbon, giving an **α-aminonitrile**, $\mathrm{RCH(NH_2)CN}$.
3. **Hydrolysis:** the nitrile group of the α-aminonitrile is hydrolysed (typically under acidic aqueous reflux conditions, e.g., with HCl/H₂O, or alternatively under basic hydrolysis followed by acidification) via the amide intermediate to the carboxylic acid, giving the **α-amino acid**.

**Reagents and conditions (summary):** aldehyde + NH₃ (or NH₄Cl) and KCN/NaCN in aqueous solution, followed by acidic (or basic) hydrolysis of the resulting aminonitrile.

**Representative example:** acetaldehyde ($\mathrm{CH_3CHO}$) + NH₃ + HCN → 2-aminopropanenitrile ($\mathrm{CH_3CH(NH_2)CN}$) → hydrolysis → **alanine**.

**Stereochemical outcome:** the imine intermediate is planar (sp² at the carbon that will become the stereocentre), so cyanide can attack from either face with equal probability; the Strecker synthesis therefore gives a **racemic** amino acid unless a chiral catalyst or chiral auxiliary is used.

**Advantages and limitations:** the Strecker synthesis is a short, conceptually simple, and historically important route that can be applied to a wide range of aldehydes (allowing access to many different side chains, R); its main limitations are the racemic outcome (Section 4.3, Section 20) and the use of toxic cyanide reagents.

## 18. Amidomalonate (Acetamidomalonate) Synthesis

A modern, widely used undergraduate variant of the classical **malonic ester synthesis**, adapted for amino-acid preparation using **diethyl acetamidomalonate** as the key starting material.

**Starting material:** diethyl acetamidomalonate, $\mathrm{CH_3CONH{-}CH(CO_2Et)_2}$ — a malonic ester derivative already bearing a protected (N-acetylated) amino group on its central carbon.

**Step 1 — Alkylation:** the acidic central C–H (flanked by two ester carbonyls) is deprotonated by a base (e.g., sodium ethoxide, NaOEt) to form a stabilised carbanion, which is then alkylated by reaction with an alkyl halide (R–X) via $S_N2$ substitution, installing the future side chain:

$$\mathrm{CH_3CONH{-}CH(CO_2Et)_2 \xrightarrow[\text{then }RX]{\text{NaOEt}} CH_3CONH{-}CR(CO_2Et)_2}$$

**Step 2 — Hydrolysis:** the alkylated product is hydrolysed under aqueous acidic (or basic) conditions, which hydrolyses **both** the two ester groups (to give a malonic-acid-type diacid) **and** the N-acetyl protecting group (regenerating the free amino group):

$$\mathrm{CH_3CONH{-}CR(CO_2Et)_2 \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+, \Delta} H_2N{-}CR(COOH)_2}$$

**Step 3 — Decarboxylation:** the resulting substituted malonic-acid derivative, being a 1,1-dicarboxylic acid, undergoes thermal **decarboxylation** (loss of one CO₂, as is standard for malonic-acid-type systems) to give the final **α-amino acid**:

$$\mathrm{H_2N{-}CR(COOH)_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} RCH(NH_2)COOH + CO_2}$$

**Stereochemical outcome:** as with the other classical routes above, this synthesis proceeds without chiral control and gives a **racemic** α-amino acid.

## 19. Reductive Amination of α-Keto Acids

**General scheme:**

$$\alpha\text{-Keto acid} \xrightarrow{\text{NH}_3} \text{Imine/iminium intermediate} \xrightarrow{\text{reduction}} \alpha\text{-Amino acid}$$

**Mechanism:** an α-keto acid (a carboxylic acid bearing a ketone at the α-position, $\mathrm{RCOCOOH}$) reacts with ammonia, forming an **imine** (or, under the reaction's aqueous/acidic conditions, the protonated **iminium ion**) at the carbonyl carbon by the same nucleophilic addition–elimination process used in Strecker imine formation. This imine/iminium intermediate is then **reduced** — commonly using **sodium borohydride (NaBH₄)** in the laboratory (biologically, the analogous transformation is catalysed by transaminase/dehydrogenase enzymes using NADH or NADPH as the reducing cofactor) — to give the saturated α-amino acid.

**Alanine example (from pyruvic acid):**

$$\mathrm{CH_3COCOOH \xrightarrow{NH_3} CH_3C(=NH)COOH \xrightarrow{NaBH_4} CH_3CH(NH_2)COOH}$$

(pyruvic acid → imine intermediate → **alanine**)

**Stereochemical outcome:** because the imine/iminium intermediate is planar at the future stereocentre, reduction by an achiral hydride source such as NaBH₄ delivers hydride to both faces with equal probability, again giving a **racemic** product unless a chiral reducing agent/catalyst or enzyme is employed.

## 20. Enantioselective Synthesis

As emphasised throughout Part VI (Section 4.3, Sections 16–19), ordinary synthetic routes to α-amino acids that proceed through an **achiral, planar intermediate** (an alkene, an enolate/carbanion, or an imine) at the future stereocentre give **racemic** products, because there is no inherent preference for attack from one face of the planar intermediate over the other.

To obtain a **single enantiomer** preferentially, a **chiral influence** must be introduced somewhere in the synthetic sequence:

- **Chiral catalysts:** e.g., transition-metal complexes bearing chiral phosphine ligands, which can differentiate between the two faces of a prochiral substrate.
- **Enantioselective (asymmetric) hydrogenation:** catalytic hydrogenation of a prochiral **dehydroamino acid** precursor (an α,β-unsaturated amino-acid derivative) using a chiral transition-metal catalyst (the classical, historically important example being rhodium complexes bearing chiral bisphosphine ligands, as pioneered in industrial L-DOPA manufacture) delivers hydrogen preferentially to one face of the double bond, giving the amino acid in high **enantiomeric excess** rather than as a racemate.
- **Enzymatic/biocatalytic methods:** enzymes are inherently chiral catalysts and can be used (e.g., in resolution of racemic mixtures, or in direct enzymatic synthesis) to obtain a single amino-acid enantiomer with very high selectivity.

Such methods are of major industrial importance for the large-scale, cost-effective production of single-enantiomer (typically L-) amino acids for pharmaceutical, food, and research use, avoiding the need to separately resolve a racemic mixture after a non-selective synthesis.

# Part VII — Peptides and Proteins

## 21. What Are Peptides and Proteins?

- **Dipeptide:** two amino-acid residues joined by a single peptide bond.
- **Tripeptide:** three amino-acid residues joined by two peptide bonds.
- **Oligopeptide:** a general term for a relatively short chain of amino-acid residues (commonly used loosely for chains of roughly a few to around ten residues).
- **Polypeptide:** a longer chain of amino-acid residues joined by peptide bonds.
- **Protein:** one or more polypeptide chains that have folded into a specific, functional three-dimensional structure.

$$\text{Amino acids} \longrightarrow \text{Peptides} \longrightarrow \text{Polypeptides} \longrightarrow \text{Proteins}$$

> **Note on terminology:** the boundaries between "oligopeptide," "polypeptide," and "protein" (in terms of an exact number of residues) are **not rigidly fixed** by any single universal convention — different textbooks use somewhat different cut-offs. The more chemically meaningful distinction is that a **protein** additionally implies a folded, biologically functional three-dimensional structure, not merely a chain of a particular length. Avoid treating any specific residue-count boundary as an absolute, universally agreed definition.

# Part VIII — Peptide Linkage

## 22. Peptide Bond / Peptide Linkage

The **peptide bond (peptide linkage)** is the covalent amide bond formed between the **carboxyl group of one amino acid** and the **amino group of a second amino acid**, with loss of one molecule of water (a **condensation** reaction):

$$\mathrm{H_2NCHR^1COOH + H_2NCHR^2COOH \longrightarrow H_2NCHR^1{-}CO{-}NH{-}CHR^2COOH + H_2O}$$

In the product dipeptide:

- The **peptide bond** is the newly formed $\mathrm{{-}CO{-}NH{-}}$ (amide) linkage joining the two residues.
- The **N-terminal residue** is the residue that retains a **free α-amino group** (residue 1, on the left as conventionally drawn).
- The **C-terminal residue** is the residue that retains a **free α-carboxyl group** (residue 2, on the right as conventionally drawn).

The peptide bond is, chemically, simply an **amide bond** — the same functional group formed whenever a carboxylic acid (or an activated derivative of one) reacts with an amine — but the term "peptide bond" specifically denotes this amide linkage when it occurs **between the α-carboxyl group of one amino acid and the α-amino group of another**.

## 23. Structure and Properties of the Peptide Bond

- **Resonance and partial double-bond character:** the peptide bond's nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the adjacent carbonyl π system by resonance, giving the $\mathrm{C{-}N}$ bond significant **partial double-bond character** (intermediate between a pure single and a pure double bond).
- **Restricted rotation:** as a direct consequence of this partial double-bond character, rotation about the $\mathrm{C{-}N}$ peptide bond is strongly hindered (a high rotational energy barrier), in contrast to free rotation about a normal C–N single bond.
- **Planarity:** the restricted rotation means that the six atoms directly involved in the peptide-bond unit — the α-carbon, carbonyl carbon, carbonyl oxygen, amide nitrogen, amide hydrogen, and the following α-carbon — lie approximately in a single **plane** (the "peptide plane" or "amide plane").
- **Trans/cis character:** because of this planarity and restricted rotation, the peptide bond can adopt either a **trans** or a **cis** configuration (referring to the relative arrangement of the two flanking α-carbons across the C–N bond). The **trans** configuration is strongly favoured energetically (due to reduced steric clash between adjacent side chains) and is found in the overwhelming majority of peptide bonds in natural proteins; the **cis** configuration occurs only rarely, and is somewhat more common specifically at peptide bonds preceding a **proline** residue (an X-Pro bond), because proline's cyclic secondary-amine structure reduces the usual steric preference for the trans form.
- **Hydrogen bonding:** the peptide-bond carbonyl oxygen (a hydrogen-bond acceptor) and the peptide-bond N–H (a hydrogen-bond donor) can each participate in **hydrogen bonding** with other peptide bonds elsewhere in the chain; the systematic, repeating pattern of such hydrogen bonds is responsible for stabilising regular secondary-structure elements (α-helices, β-sheets) in proteins.

# Part IX — Composition of Proteins

## 24. Chemical Composition of Proteins

**Elemental composition:** proteins are composed principally of **carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen**, with **sulfur** present in proteins that contain cysteine and/or methionine residues (and, in some specific proteins, additional elements such as phosphorus in phosphoproteins, or various metals in metalloproteins — see below).

**Structural organisation:** a protein consists of one or more **polypeptide chains**, each built from a specific, genetically determined sequence of **amino-acid residues** (the term "residue" refers to an amino acid unit once it has been incorporated into the chain via peptide-bond formation, having lost the elements of water relative to the free amino acid) joined by **peptide bonds**. The identity and chemical character of each residue's **side chain** (Section 2.3) collectively determine the protein's overall physicochemical properties and, through folding, its three-dimensional structure and function.

**Simple proteins vs conjugated proteins:**

- **Simple proteins:** proteins that, upon complete hydrolysis, yield **only amino acids** (and no other type of chemical component). Example: many structural and globular proteins composed purely of polypeptide chain(s).
- **Conjugated proteins:** proteins that contain, in addition to the polypeptide chain, a covalently or non-covalently associated **non-protein component**, called a **prosthetic group**.

**Representative examples of conjugated proteins:**

| Class of conjugated protein | Prosthetic group | Representative example |
|---|---|---|
| Glycoproteins | Carbohydrate | Many cell-surface receptor proteins, antibodies (immunoglobulins) |
| Lipoproteins | Lipid | Low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) |
| Phosphoproteins | Phosphate group(s), typically on serine/threonine residues | Casein (milk protein) |
| Metalloproteins | Metal ion(s) | Haemoglobin (iron), carbonic anhydrase (zinc) |
| Chromoproteins | A coloured prosthetic group | Haemoglobin (haem group), chlorophyll-binding proteins |

> Note that haemoglobin is a classic example that is simultaneously classified as both a **metalloprotein** (bound iron) and a **chromoprotein** (the coloured haem prosthetic group), illustrating that these categories are not mutually exclusive.

## 25. Hydrolysis of Proteins

Proteins (and peptides generally) can be broken down into smaller fragments, and ultimately into free amino acids, by hydrolytic cleavage of their peptide bonds — the reverse of the condensation reaction that formed them (Section 22):

- **Acid hydrolysis:** treatment with a strong mineral acid (commonly 6 M HCl) under prolonged reflux, which cleaves peptide bonds essentially completely to give a mixture of free amino acids; some amino acids (notably tryptophan) are partially destroyed under these harsh acidic conditions, and asparagine/glutamine are hydrolysed to aspartic acid/glutamic acid (with loss of the amide group as ammonia) during acid hydrolysis.
- **Alkaline hydrolysis:** treatment with strong base, which can also hydrolyse peptide bonds, but is generally used less often for quantitative amino-acid analysis because it causes racemisation of amino acids and destruction of certain residues (e.g., cysteine, serine, threonine).
- **Enzymatic hydrolysis:** treatment with specific proteolytic enzymes (**proteases/peptidases**, e.g., trypsin, chymotrypsin, pepsin), which cleave peptide bonds selectively at specific positions determined by the identity of the flanking amino-acid residues, generating a reproducible, specific set of smaller peptide fragments rather than complete hydrolysis to free amino acids (unless prolonged/exhaustive digestion is used).

**Distinction between protein composition analysis and sequence determination:** complete hydrolysis (by any of the above methods) breaks every peptide bond and destroys the original sequence information, yielding only a mixture of free amino acids whose **relative amounts** can be quantified (telling you **what** amino acids, and in what proportions, the protein contains — its **composition**). This is fundamentally different from **sequence determination**, which requires methods that preserve (or systematically reveal, one residue at a time) the **order** in which the residues occur along the chain — such as the terminal-residue-identification methods discussed in Parts XI and XII, and, for longer chains, selective enzymatic/chemical cleavage into overlapping fragments followed by their individual sequencing.

# Part X — N-Terminal and C-Terminal Residues

## 26. N-Terminal and C-Terminal Residues

**N-terminal residue:** the amino-acid residue located at the end of a peptide/polypeptide chain that retains a **free (unreacted) α-amino group** (i.e., this residue's amino group has not been consumed in peptide-bond formation).

**C-terminal residue:** the amino-acid residue located at the end of a peptide/polypeptide chain that retains a **free (unreacted) α-carboxyl group**.

**Peptide notation:** by convention, peptide sequences are written and read from the **N-terminus to the C-terminus** (left to right). For example:

$$\mathrm{Gly{-}Ala{-}Ser}$$

means: **N-terminus → Glycine → Alanine → Serine → C-terminus**, i.e., glycine is the N-terminal residue (its free α-amino group is unreacted), serine is the C-terminal residue (its free α-carboxyl group is unreacted), and alanine is the single internal residue, joined to glycine and to serine by two peptide bonds.

> **Convention to remember:** peptides/proteins are always written **N-terminus first, C-terminus last** — never the reverse — both in simple three-letter/one-letter sequence notation and in full structural depictions.

# Part XI — Identification of N-Terminal Residue

## 27. Sanger's Method

**Principle:** **Sanger's reagent**, **1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (FDNB, also written as DNFB)**, reacts selectively with the **free α-amino group** of the N-terminal residue of a peptide (via nucleophilic aromatic substitution, in which the amine displaces the activated aromatic fluorine), forming a stable, covalently labelled **DNP-peptide** (2,4-dinitrophenyl derivative).

**General scheme:**

$$\text{Peptide} \xrightarrow{\text{FDNB, mild base}} \text{DNP-peptide} \xrightarrow{\text{complete acid hydrolysis}} \text{DNP-amino acid (N-terminal)} + \text{free amino acids (all other residues)}$$

**Procedure and interpretation:**

1. The intact peptide is first treated with FDNB under mildly basic conditions, which labels only the free N-terminal amino group (side-chain amino groups, such as the ε-amino group of an internal lysine residue, can also react and must be accounted for when interpreting results for lysine-containing peptides, but the reaction with the N-terminal α-amino group is the basis of the method).
2. The resulting DNP-peptide is then subjected to **complete acid hydrolysis** (Section 25), which cleaves **every** peptide bond in the chain.
3. Because the DNP label is a stable **covalent bond** to the N-terminal residue's α-amino group, this residue survives hydrolysis as a **DNP-amino acid**, distinguishable from all the other (unlabelled) free amino acids released by hydrolysis.
4. The DNP-amino acid is isolated (e.g., by chromatography) and identified by comparison with authentic DNP-amino-acid standards, thereby identifying the **original N-terminal residue** of the peptide.

**Why only the N-terminal amino acid is labelled (and identifiable after hydrolysis):** every other residue in the chain has its α-amino group tied up in a peptide bond (not free) prior to FDNB treatment, so FDNB cannot react with them at the α-position; only the single residue bearing the free N-terminal α-amino group is available for DNP labelling **before** hydrolysis. Since hydrolysis subsequently destroys all peptide-bond connectivity anyway, only this one residue carries a distinguishing chemical "tag" that survives to identify it specifically as having been the N-terminus.

> **Limitation:** Sanger's method as described determines only the **identity of the N-terminal residue**; the original peptide is completely destroyed by the hydrolysis step, so this method cannot be repeated on the same molecule to read further residues along the chain (in contrast to Edman degradation, Section 28).

## 28. Edman Degradation

**Reagent:** **phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC)**, $\mathrm{C_6H_5{-}N{=}C{=}S}$.

**Principle:** PITC reacts with the free N-terminal α-amino group of a peptide under mildly basic conditions to form a **phenylthiocarbamyl (PTC)-peptide**. Under mild acidic conditions, this PTC derivative undergoes **selective cyclisation and cleavage**, releasing the N-terminal residue as a cyclic **phenylthiohydantoin (PTH)-amino acid** derivative, while the **remainder of the peptide chain, shortened by exactly one residue, is released intact** (with a new free N-terminus exposed, ready for the next round).

**General scheme:**

$$\text{Peptide} \xrightarrow{\text{PITC, mild base}} \text{PTC-peptide} \xrightarrow{\text{mild acid}} \text{PTH-(N-terminal amino acid)} + \text{peptide shortened by one residue}$$

**Repetition for sequencing:** because the cleavage step under Edman degradation removes **only** the N-terminal residue and leaves the rest of the peptide chain **intact and unhydrolysed** (unlike Sanger's method, which destroys the whole peptide via complete hydrolysis), the shortened peptide can be **subjected to another round of PITC treatment**, releasing the next residue (originally the second residue) as a new PTH-amino acid. This cycle can, in principle, be repeated **sequentially, residue by residue**, from the N-terminus inward, allowing the amino-acid sequence of a peptide to be read out step by step.

**Why Edman degradation is more useful than Sanger's method for stepwise sequencing:** Sanger's method requires **complete hydrolysis** of the peptide to reveal even the single N-terminal residue, which destroys the peptide entirely and can therefore only ever identify **one** residue (the original N-terminus) per sample of peptide. Edman degradation, by contrast, selectively removes **only** the terminal residue at each cycle while leaving the rest of the chain intact, so the **same peptide molecule** can be carried through multiple successive cycles, each one revealing the next residue in sequence — making Edman degradation the practical basis of automated, multi-cycle protein/peptide **sequencing** (as used, historically, in automated protein sequencers), whereas Sanger's method is limited to a single N-terminal identification.

# Part XII — Identification of C-Terminal Residue

## 29. C-Terminal Analysis

### Hydrazinolysis

**Principle:** the intact peptide is treated with **anhydrous hydrazine (H₂N–NH₂)**, typically with heating. Hydrazine reacts with every peptide bond in the chain, converting each internal and N-terminal residue into the corresponding **amino-acid hydrazide** ($\mathrm{RCH(NH_2)CONHNH_2}$), because hydrazine acts as a nucleophile that aminolyses each amide (peptide) linkage.

**Why the C-terminal residue is distinguished:** the **C-terminal residue** is the only residue in the chain whose carboxyl group is **not** part of a peptide bond (it is the free terminal –COOH) — it therefore has **no peptide-bond carbonyl available to react with hydrazine at its own carboxyl end**, and consequently survives the reaction **as the free, unreacted C-terminal amino acid**, rather than being converted into a hydrazide like every other residue.

**Identification:** after the hydrazinolysis reaction, the reaction mixture is separated (e.g., chromatographically); all the internal and N-terminal residues are present as amino-acid **hydrazides**, while the C-terminal residue alone is present as the **free amino acid**, allowing it to be identified.

> **Limitation:** hydrazinolysis is a comparatively harsh classical method with practical limitations (e.g., some amino-acid hydrazides can be partially hydrolysed back to free amino acid under the reaction conditions, and certain residues such as glycine and asparagine/glutamine behave anomalously), so it is now regarded mainly as the classical/theoretical example taught at undergraduate level rather than a routinely preferred modern laboratory method.

### Enzymatic Method — Carboxypeptidase

**Principle:** **carboxypeptidase** is an **exopeptidase** — an enzyme that hydrolyses peptide bonds specifically **from one end of the chain**, rather than at internal (random) positions. Carboxypeptidase specifically recognises and cleaves the peptide bond adjacent to the **free carboxyl (C-terminal) end** of the chain, sequentially **releasing the terminal amino-acid residue** (the current C-terminal residue) one at a time.

**Use for C-terminal identification:** upon brief treatment of a peptide with carboxypeptidase, the **first (and, initially, most rapidly released) free amino acid** appearing in solution corresponds to the **original C-terminal residue**. If the enzymatic digestion is allowed to continue, the enzyme will proceed to remove the next residue (now newly exposed as the C-terminus), and so on, in principle allowing a stepwise C-terminal sequencing analogous in concept (though enzymatic rather than chemical, and generally less uniformly reliable in rate) to Edman degradation from the N-terminal side.

# Part XIV — Worked Problems

## 31. Amino-Acid Nomenclature Problems

**Problem 1.** Give the IUPAC name of $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH(NH_2)COOH}$ and identify the common name.
*Solution:* The parent chain is a four-carbon acid (butanoic acid) with a methyl branch and an amino group. Numbering from the carboxyl carbon: C-1 = COOH, C-2 = CH(NH₂), C-3 = CH(CH₃), with a methyl substituent at C-3. IUPAC name: **2-amino-3-methylbutanoic acid**. Common name: **valine**.

**Problem 2.** Draw the Fischer projection of L-alanine and assign its R/S configuration.
*Solution:* In the standard Fischer projection (COOH at top, R group — here CH₃ — at bottom), L-alanine has –NH₂ on the **left** and –H on the **right** at the central carbon. Applying CIP priority (NH₂ > COOH > CH₃ > H) with H (lowest priority) pointing toward the viewer in this particular Fischer arrangement requires reversing the apparent direction once determined: the assignment for L-alanine is **(S)**, i.e., **L-alanine = (S)-alanine**.

**Problem 3.** Classify glutamic acid by (a) position of amino group, and (b) number of amino/carboxyl groups.
*Solution:* (a) The amino group of glutamic acid is on the carbon adjacent to one of its carboxyl groups — it is an **α-amino acid**. (b) Glutamic acid has one amino group and two carboxyl groups (α-COOH and side-chain –COOH) — it is classified as an **acidic amino acid**.

## 32. Optical-Activity Problems

**Problem 1.** A solution of an amino acid with concentration 0.05 g mL⁻¹ is placed in a polarimeter tube of path length 2 dm. The observed rotation is +2.5°. Calculate the specific rotation.
*Solution:*
$$[\alpha] = \frac{\alpha}{lc} = \frac{+2.5}{2 \times 0.05} = \frac{2.5}{0.1} = +25^{\circ}$$

**Problem 2.** A sample of an amino acid known to have $[\alpha]_D = +25^{\circ}$ for the pure L-enantiomer is found experimentally to have an observed specific rotation of only $+10^{\circ}$ under identical conditions. What does this indicate about the sample, and what is its approximate enantiomeric composition?
*Solution:* Since the measured rotation ($+10^{\circ}$) is less than the rotation expected for the pure single enantiomer ($+25^{\circ}$), the sample must be a **mixture of both enantiomers** (partially racemised), not optically pure. The **enantiomeric excess (ee)** can be estimated as:
$$\text{ee} = \frac{\text{observed rotation}}{\text{rotation of pure enantiomer}} \times 100\% = \frac{10}{25}\times 100\% = 40\%$$
This corresponds to a mixture that is 70% of the (+)-enantiomer and 30% of the (–)-enantiomer (since 70% − 30% = 40% ee), i.e. it is partially, but not completely, racemised.

**Problem 3.** Explain why a perfectly 1:1 (racemic) mixture of D- and L-alanine shows zero observed rotation even though each pure enantiomer individually is optically active.
*Solution:* Each enantiomer rotates plane-polarised light by an equal magnitude but in **opposite directions** (one clockwise, one anticlockwise). In an exact 50:50 mixture, the rotation contributed by the (+)-form is exactly cancelled by the equal and opposite rotation contributed by the (−)-form, giving a **net observed rotation of zero**, even though the individual molecules present are each still chiral and individually optically active (Section 4.3).

## 33. pI Problems

**Problem 1 — Glycine** (pKa₁ = 2.34 for α-COOH; pKa₂ = 9.60 for α-NH₃⁺):
As a simple neutral amino acid with only two ionisable groups, $pI = \dfrac{pK_{a1}+pK_{a2}}{2} = \dfrac{2.34+9.60}{2} = \mathbf{5.97}$.

**Problem 2 — Aspartic acid** (pKa₁ = 1.88, α-COOH; pKa₂ = 3.65, side-chain –COOH; pKa₃ = 9.60, α-NH₃⁺):
The neutral (zero-net-charge) species is bracketed by the two carboxyl-group pKa values (both must be deprotonated before the amino group loses its proton, given the ordering of the pKa values), so $pI = \dfrac{pK_{a1}+pK_{a2}}{2} = \dfrac{1.88+3.65}{2} = \mathbf{2.77}$.

**Problem 3 — Lysine** (pKa₁ = 2.18, α-COOH; pKa₂ = 8.95, α-NH₃⁺; pKa₃ = 10.53, side-chain –NH₃⁺):
The neutral species is bracketed by the two amino-group pKa values (the single carboxyl group is already fully deprotonated well below this range), so $pI = \dfrac{pK_{a2}+pK_{a3}}{2} = \dfrac{8.95+10.53}{2} = \mathbf{9.74}$.

## 34. Peptide Identification Problems

**Problem: Gly-Ala-Ser**

- **N-terminal residue:** Glycine (retains the free α-amino group).
- **C-terminal residue:** Serine (retains the free α-carboxyl group).
- **Number of peptide bonds:** 2 (Gly–Ala and Ala–Ser).
- **Number of amino-acid residues:** 3 (Gly, Ala, Ser).
- **Molecular relationship:** the tripeptide's molecular formula is obtained by summing the molecular formulas of the three free amino acids and **subtracting two molecules of water** (one water molecule is lost for each of the two peptide bonds formed): $\text{Tripeptide} = \text{Gly} + \text{Ala} + \text{Ser} - 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

# Part XV — Exam Preparation

## 35. Important Short Questions

**Q1. Define an amino acid.** An organic compound containing both a basic amino group and an acidic carboxyl group within the same molecule.

**Q2. What is an α-amino acid?** An amino acid in which the amino group is attached to the carbon atom immediately adjacent to the carboxyl group (the α-carbon).

**Q3. Give one basis for classifying amino acids.** By position of the amino group (α, β, γ, δ); by number of amino/carboxyl groups (neutral, acidic, basic); or by side-chain character (non-polar, polar, aromatic, sulfur-containing, etc.).

**Q4. Is glycine optically active?** No — glycine's α-carbon bears two identical hydrogen substituents, so it is not a stereocentre; glycine is achiral and optically inactive.

**Q5. Define zwitterion.** A dipolar ion, bearing both a positive charge (protonated amino group) and a negative charge (deprotonated carboxyl group), with an overall net charge of zero.

**Q6. Define isoelectric point (pI).** The pH at which an amino acid (or peptide/protein) carries zero net electrical charge.

**Q7. What colour does ninhydrin normally give with an α-amino acid?** Blue-purple (Ruhemann's purple); proline and hydroxyproline give yellow instead, due to their secondary (imino) amino group.

**Q8. What is produced when an amino acid is decarboxylated?** The corresponding primary amine, with loss of CO₂.

**Q9. Name the reagent used in the Strecker synthesis to introduce the carbon–nitrogen and carbon–carbon bonds.** Ammonia (for the C–N bond, via imine formation) and cyanide (for the C–C bond, via nucleophilic addition to the imine).

**Q10. What reagent is used for α-halogenation in the HVZ route to amino acids?** Bromine (Br₂) with a catalytic amount of red phosphorus.

**Q11. Define peptide linkage.** The covalent amide bond formed by condensation between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another, with loss of water.

**Q12. What are simple proteins and conjugated proteins?** Simple proteins yield only amino acids upon complete hydrolysis; conjugated proteins additionally contain a non-protein prosthetic group (e.g., carbohydrate, lipid, metal ion).

**Q13. Define N-terminal residue and C-terminal residue.** N-terminal residue: the residue bearing the free α-amino group at one end of a peptide chain. C-terminal residue: the residue bearing the free α-carboxyl group at the other end.

**Q14. Which reagent is used in Sanger's method?** 1-Fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (FDNB).

**Q15. Which reagent is used in Edman degradation?** Phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC).

**Q16. Which reagent is used for classical C-terminal hydrazinolysis?** Anhydrous hydrazine (H₂N–NH₂).

**Q17. Which enzyme is used for enzymatic C-terminal identification?** Carboxypeptidase.

## 36. Important 5-Mark Questions

**Q1. Classify amino acids with suitable examples.**
*Model answer:* Amino acids can be classified in several complementary ways. By position of the amino group relative to the carboxyl group: α-amino acids (e.g., alanine), β-amino acids (e.g., β-alanine), γ-amino acids (e.g., GABA), and δ-amino acids (e.g., δ-aminolevulinic acid); proteinogenic amino acids are almost exclusively α-amino acids. By number of amino/carboxyl groups: neutral amino acids (one of each, e.g., glycine, alanine), acidic amino acids (one amino, two carboxyl groups, e.g., aspartic acid, glutamic acid), and basic amino acids (two or more basic nitrogen groups, one carboxyl group, e.g., lysine, arginine, histidine). By side-chain character: non-polar/hydrophobic, polar uncharged, acidic, basic, aromatic, sulfur-containing, and hydroxyl-containing amino acids (Section 2.3 table). Additionally, amino acids are classified nutritionally as essential, non-essential, or conditionally essential, and biochemically as proteinogenic or non-proteinogenic.

**Q2. Explain the D/L and R/S systems of nomenclature for amino acids, with reference to any important exceptions.**
*Model answer:* The D/L system is a relative configurational label assigned by structural comparison with glyceraldehyde: natural amino acids, with the α-amino group on the left in the standard Fischer projection, are designated L. The R/S system is an absolute label assigned using CIP priority rules based on atomic number at the stereocentre. For most amino acids, the priority order NH₂ > COOH > R > H corresponds to L = S (e.g., L-alanine = (S)-alanine). The important exception is cysteine: because its side chain (–CH₂SH) contains sulfur, which outranks the oxygens of the carboxyl group in CIP priority, the priority order changes, and L-cysteine is designated (R), despite having the same spatial arrangement as other L-amino acids. Glycine has no D/L or R/S designation because its α-carbon (bonded to two hydrogens) is not a stereocentre.

**Q3. Discuss optical activity in amino acids, distinguishing configuration from observed rotation.**
*Model answer:* See Section 4 and Section 4.2. Most α-amino acids are chiral (glycine excepted) due to four different substituents at the α-carbon, and rotate plane-polarised light, quantified as specific rotation, $[\alpha] = \alpha/(lc)$. D/L (or R/S) describes configuration, a structural property independent of measurement; (+)/(−) describes the experimentally measured direction of rotation. These are not linked by any general rule — e.g., L-alanine is (+), but L-leucine is (−) — so each must be determined/looked up individually rather than inferred from the other.

**Q4. Describe the Strecker synthesis of amino acids, with a suitable example.**
*Model answer:* See Section 17. The Strecker synthesis proceeds via: (1) reaction of an aldehyde with ammonia to form an imine; (2) nucleophilic addition of cyanide to the imine to give an α-aminonitrile; (3) hydrolysis of the nitrile to a carboxylic acid, giving the α-amino acid. Example: acetaldehyde + NH₃ + HCN → 2-aminopropanenitrile → hydrolysis → alanine. Because the imine intermediate is planar, the product is obtained as a racemic mixture unless a chiral catalyst is used.

**Q5. Describe the properties and important chemical reactions of amino acids.**
*Model answer:* Amino acids are amphoteric, existing as zwitterions near neutral pH, and react with both acids (protonating –COO⁻) and bases (deprotonating –NH₃⁺). Characteristic reactions include: the ninhydrin reaction (giving Ruhemann's purple, used for detection); the formaldehyde (formol) reaction (masking basicity to allow direct titration of the carboxyl group); reaction with nitrous acid (deamination to the corresponding α-hydroxy acid with evolution of N₂, used for quantification); esterification of the carboxyl group; acylation of the amino group (used for protection); and decarboxylation to the corresponding primary amine (e.g., histidine → histamine, glutamic acid → GABA).

**Q6. Explain the structure and properties of the peptide bond.**
*Model answer:* See Section 22–23. The peptide bond is the amide linkage formed by condensation of the carboxyl group of one amino acid with the amino group of another, with loss of water. Resonance delocalisation of the nitrogen lone pair into the carbonyl gives the C–N bond partial double-bond character, restricting rotation and enforcing near-planarity of the six-atom peptide unit. The trans configuration is strongly favoured over cis (except commonly at X-Pro bonds). The peptide-bond carbonyl and N–H groups participate in the hydrogen bonding that stabilises protein secondary structure.

**Q7. Explain the composition of proteins, distinguishing simple and conjugated proteins.**
*Model answer:* See Section 24. Proteins consist of one or more polypeptide chains built from amino-acid residues joined by peptide bonds, composed principally of C, H, O, N (and S where cysteine/methionine are present). Simple proteins yield only amino acids on hydrolysis. Conjugated proteins additionally contain a non-protein prosthetic group: glycoproteins (carbohydrate, e.g., many antibodies), lipoproteins (lipid, e.g., LDL/HDL), phosphoproteins (phosphate, e.g., casein), metalloproteins (metal ion, e.g., carbonic anhydrase), and chromoproteins (coloured prosthetic group, e.g., haemoglobin).

**Q8. Describe Sanger's method for identifying the N-terminal residue of a peptide.**
*Model answer:* See Section 27. Sanger's reagent, FDNB, reacts with the free N-terminal α-amino group of a peptide to form a stable DNP-peptide. Complete acid hydrolysis then cleaves every peptide bond; because the N-terminal residue alone carries the covalent DNP label, it survives hydrolysis as an identifiable DNP-amino acid, while all other residues are released as free (unlabelled) amino acids. The method destroys the peptide and identifies only the original N-terminal residue.

**Q9. Describe Edman degradation and explain why it is preferred for sequencing over Sanger's method.**
*Model answer:* See Section 28. PITC reacts with the free N-terminal amino group to form a PTC-peptide; mild acid then selectively cleaves and cyclises only the N-terminal residue, releasing it as a PTH-amino acid while leaving the rest of the peptide chain intact with a new free N-terminus. Because the chain is not destroyed, this cycle can be repeated multiple times on the same molecule, allowing sequential, residue-by-residue sequencing from the N-terminus — unlike Sanger's method, which requires complete hydrolysis and can identify only one (the original N-terminal) residue.

**Q10. Compare the classical chemical and enzymatic methods for C-terminal identification.**
*Model answer:* See Section 29–30. Hydrazinolysis treats the peptide with anhydrous hydrazine, which converts every residue except the C-terminal one into an amino-acid hydrazide (since only the C-terminal residue's carboxyl group is not part of a peptide bond and thus cannot react); the C-terminal residue survives as the free amino acid and is identified after separation. Carboxypeptidase, an exopeptidase, enzymatically and sequentially releases residues from the C-terminal end, with the first residue released corresponding to the original C-terminus; continued digestion can, in principle, allow stepwise C-terminal sequencing analogous in concept to Edman degradation.

## 37. Common Examination Mistakes

- **Saying glycine is optically active** — glycine's α-carbon is not a stereocentre (two identical H substituents); it is achiral.
- **Confusing D/L notation with (+)/(−) rotation** — D/L is configuration (structural), (+)/(−) is measured optical rotation; one cannot be inferred from the other without explicit information.
- **Incorrectly assigning the configuration of cysteine** — remember L-cysteine is the standard exception and is designated **(R)**, not (S), due to sulfur's CIP priority in the side chain.
- **Calling every amino acid an α-amino acid** — β-, γ-, and δ-amino acids exist (e.g., GABA is a γ-amino acid), even though proteinogenic amino acids are predominantly α.
- **Incorrectly classifying amino acids** — e.g., misclassifying histidine (basic) as neutral, or forgetting that "essential" refers strictly to dietary requirement, not chemical importance.
- **Confusing amino-acid residue with free amino acid** — a "residue" has lost the elements of water upon incorporation into a peptide chain; it is not chemically identical to the free amino acid.
- **Drawing the peptide bond incorrectly** — remember the correct condensation direction (–COOH of residue 1 + –NH₂ of residue 2 → –CO–NH– + H₂O), and its partial double-bond, planar, predominantly-trans character.
- **Reversing N- and C-terminal directions, or writing peptide sequences from C → N instead of N → C** — peptides are always written N-terminus first.
- **Confusing Sanger degradation with Edman degradation** — Sanger's method (FDNB) destroys the peptide via complete hydrolysis and identifies only the original N-terminal residue; Edman degradation (PITC) selectively removes one residue at a time, leaving the rest of the chain intact for repeated sequencing.
- **Confusing N-terminal analysis with C-terminal analysis** — check carefully which functional group (amino vs carboxyl) and which reagent/enzyme (FDNB/PITC vs hydrazine/carboxypeptidase) is relevant to the question asked.
- **Incorrectly describing hydrazinolysis** — the C-terminal residue survives as the **free amino acid** because it is the one residue whose carboxyl group is *not* part of a peptide bond; all other residues become hydrazides.
- **Using the wrong enzyme for C-terminal analysis** — carboxypeptidase (not aminopeptidase) is used for C-terminal identification; aminopeptidase acts from the N-terminal end.
- **Confusing peptide bonds with other amide bonds** — a peptide bond specifically joins the α-carboxyl group of one amino acid to the α-amino group of another; not every amide bond in a biomolecule is necessarily termed a "peptide bond."
- **Incorrectly writing synthesis reactions** — always check reagents and conditions carefully (e.g., HVZ requires Br₂/red P before ammonolysis; Strecker requires both NH₃ and cyanide before hydrolysis).
- **Forgetting that ordinary achiral synthesis generally gives racemic products** — unless an explicitly asymmetric/enantioselective method or resolution step is stated, assume classical syntheses (HVZ, Strecker, amidomalonate, reductive amination with NaBH₄) give racemic (DL) amino acids.

## 39. Quick Revision Sheet

### Amino Acids

- General structure: $\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R){-}COOH}$; predominant form in solution is the zwitterion, $\mathrm{^{+}H_3N{-}CH(R){-}COO^{-}}$.
- Classification: by position of amino group (α/β/γ/δ); by number of amino/carboxyl groups (neutral/acidic/basic); by side-chain character (non-polar, polar, acidic, basic, aromatic, sulfur-containing, hydroxyl-containing); nutritionally (essential/non-essential/conditionally essential); proteinogenic vs non-proteinogenic.
- 20 standard amino acids, each with a 3-letter and 1-letter code (Section 2.3 table).
- Stereochemistry: all α-amino acids except **glycine** (achiral) are chiral; natural amino acids are **L**; **L-cysteine is the standard R-configuration exception**; D/L ≠ R/S ≠ (+)/(−) — keep configuration and observed rotation conceptually separate.
- Optical activity: $[\alpha] = \alpha/(lc)$; racemic mixtures show zero net rotation despite consisting of chiral molecules.
- Zwitterions and pI: $pI = (pK_{a1}+pK_{a2})/2$ for a simple neutral amino acid, adjusted to average the two pKa values bracketing the neutral species for acidic/basic amino acids.
- Key reactions: acid–base reactions, ninhydrin, formaldehyde (formol titration), nitrous acid (deamination), esterification, acylation, decarboxylation.
- Synthesis methods: HVZ/α-halo acid method, Strecker synthesis, amidomalonate synthesis, reductive amination — all give **racemic** products unless an enantioselective method (e.g., asymmetric hydrogenation, chiral catalysis) is used.

### Proteins

- Definition: one or more folded polypeptide chains built from amino-acid residues.
- Composition: C, H, O, N (S in cysteine/methionine-containing proteins); simple proteins (amino acids only) vs conjugated proteins (with a prosthetic group — glyco-, lipo-, phospho-, metallo-, chromoproteins).
- Peptide bond: amide linkage formed by condensation, with partial double-bond character, restricted rotation, planarity, and predominantly trans geometry.
- Peptide notation: always written **N-terminus → C-terminus**.
- N-terminus: free α-amino group end. C-terminus: free α-carboxyl group end.
- Protein hydrolysis: acid, alkaline, or enzymatic — breaks peptide bonds down to smaller peptides/free amino acids; distinct from sequence determination.

### Terminal Analysis

- **Sanger:** FDNB labels the N-terminal residue → complete hydrolysis → DNP-amino acid identified (peptide destroyed; single identification only).
- **Edman:** PITC selectively removes the N-terminal residue as a PTH-amino acid each cycle, leaving the rest of the chain intact → repeatable, enabling stepwise sequencing.
- **Hydrazinolysis:** hydrazine converts all but the C-terminal residue into hydrazides; the C-terminal residue alone survives as the free amino acid.
- **Carboxypeptidase:** an exopeptidase that sequentially releases residues from the C-terminal end, enabling C-terminal (and potentially stepwise) identification.
