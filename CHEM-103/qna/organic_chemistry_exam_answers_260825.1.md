# Q1. Amines: Classification, Separation, and Named Reactions (5+5)

## (A) 1°, 2°, 3° Amines — Definition

**Amines** are organic derivatives of ammonia (NH₃) obtained by replacing one, two, or three hydrogen atoms with alkyl/aryl groups.

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary (1°)** | R–NH₂ (one H replaced) | CH₃NH₂ (methylamine) |
| **Secondary (2°)** | R₂NH (two H replaced) | (CH₃)₂NH (dimethylamine) |
| **Tertiary (3°)** | R₃N (three H replaced) | (CH₃)₃N (trimethylamine) |

**Key point:** Classification is based on the number of carbon groups attached to **N**, unlike alcohols (based on C attached to OH-bearing carbon).

## (B) Separation of 1°, 2°, 3° Amines from a Mixture — **Hinsberg's Method**

**Reagent:** Benzenesulfonyl chloride (C₆H₅SO₂Cl) + KOH

**Step 1:** Mixture treated with C₆H₅SO₂Cl/KOH.

- **1° amine** → forms N-alkyl benzenesulfonamide, **soluble in KOH** (acidic N–H, due to –SO₂ group):
$$C_6H_5SO_2Cl + RNH_2 \xrightarrow{KOH} C_6H_5SO_2NHR + HCl$$
(soluble salt: C₆H₅SO₂NR⁻K⁺)

- **2° amine** → forms N,N-dialkyl benzenesulfonamide, **insoluble in KOH** (no acidic H left):
$$C_6H_5SO_2Cl + R_2NH \xrightarrow{KOH} C_6H_5SO_2NR_2 \, (\text{insoluble solid})$$

- **3° amine** → **does not react** (no H on N), remains as free amine, separated by simple extraction with dil. HCl (forms water-soluble salt).

**Step 2:** Filter → insoluble 2° amide separated.
**Step 3:** Filtrate acidified with HCl → 1° sulfonamide hydrolyzed on heating with HCl to regenerate 1° amine.

**Exam scoring points:**
- Reagent must be named: benzenesulfonyl chloride (Hinsberg's reagent)
- Solubility criterion in KOH is the key differentiating principle
- 3° amine gives NO reaction (this is often the examiner's trap)
- Regeneration of 1° amine needs acidic hydrolysis
- Mention this is called **Hinsberg's test/separation**

## (i) Sandmeyer and Gattermann Reactions

**Definition:** Both are methods to replace the **diazonium group (–N₂⁺)** of an aromatic diazonium salt with **–Cl, –Br, or –CN**.

**Sandmeyer Reaction**
Reagent: **Cu(I) salt** (Cu₂Cl₂/HCl, Cu₂Br₂/HBr, or CuCN/KCN)

$$ArN_2^+Cl^- + CuCl \xrightarrow{\Delta} ArCl + N_2 + CuCl$$
$$ArN_2^+Cl^- + CuCN \xrightarrow{\Delta} ArCN + N_2$$

**Gattermann Reaction**
Reagent: **Cu powder** (freshly precipitated copper) instead of cuprous salt.

$$ArN_2^+Cl^- + Cu/HCl \xrightarrow{\Delta} ArCl + N_2$$

**Example:** Benzenediazonium chloride → chlorobenzene (Sandmeyer, using Cu₂Cl₂) or (Gattermann, using Cu/HCl).

**Exam scoring points:**
- Sandmeyer uses **cuprous salt**; Gattermann uses **copper powder** — this distinction is the main mark
- Both proceed via **free radical mechanism**
- N₂ gas is evolved (visible effervescence, testable observation)
- Used to introduce Cl, Br, CN onto benzene ring where direct substitution is not possible

## (ii) Coupling Reaction and Sulpha Drugs

**Coupling Reaction — Definition:** Reaction of a diazonium salt with an activated aromatic compound (phenol or aromatic amine) in cold, weakly alkaline/acidic medium to form an **azo compound** (–N=N– linkage).

$$C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + C_6H_5OH \xrightarrow{NaOH, 273-278K} C_6H_5-N=N-C_6H_4OH \; (\text{p-hydroxyazobenzene})$$

This is an **electrophilic aromatic substitution** where ArN₂⁺ acts as electrophile, attacking the para position of phenol/aniline. Azo dyes (e.g., methyl orange) are made this way.

**Sulpha Drugs — Definition:** Sulphonamide derivatives with antibacterial action, synthesized starting from diazonium coupling/sulfonation chemistry.

**Synthesis of Sulphanilamide (prototype sulpha drug):**
1. Acetanilide + ClSO₃H → p-acetamidobenzenesulfonyl chloride
2. + NH₃ → p-acetamidobenzenesulfonamide
3. Hydrolysis (dil. HCl) → **Sulphanilamide** (p-aminobenzenesulfonamide)

$$CH_3CONHC_6H_4SO_2NH_2 \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} H_2N-C_6H_4-SO_2NH_2$$

**Exam scoring points:**
- Coupling requires **cold conditions (0–5°C)** and weakly alkaline medium — very commonly tested
- Product has **azo linkage (–N=N–)**
- Sulphanilamide = p-aminobenzenesulfonamide, acts as **PABA antagonist** (competitive inhibitor of folic acid synthesis in bacteria)
- Acetanilide is used to protect –NH₂ before sulfonation (protecting group logic)

## (iii) Hofmann and Curtius Degradation

**Both convert an acid derivative into a primary amine with ONE CARBON LESS than starting material.**

**Hofmann Bromamide Degradation**
Reagent: **Br₂/NaOH (or KOH)**

$$RCONH_2 + Br_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow RNH_2 + 2NaBr + Na_2CO_3 + 2H_2O$$

Mechanism (brief): amide → N-bromoamide → base removes H → nitrene/isocyanate intermediate (R–N=C=O) → hydrolysis → amine + CO₂.

**Example:** CH₃CONH₂ (acetamide) → CH₃NH₂ (methylamine) + CO₂

**Curtius Degradation**
Starting material: **Acyl azide** (RCON₃), obtained from acid chloride + NaN₃

$$RCOCl \xrightarrow{NaN_3} RCON_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} RN=C=O \; (\text{isocyanate}) \xrightarrow{H_2O} RNH_2 + CO_2$$

**Exam scoring points:**
- Both give **1° amine with one carbon less** — this is the defining outcome
- Hofmann uses **amide + Br₂/NaOH**; Curtius uses **acyl azide, heat**
- Common intermediate concept: **isocyanate (R–N=C=O)** in both mechanisms
- Reaction proceeds via **migration of R group from carbonyl carbon to N** (1,2-shift, retention of configuration) — high-value mechanistic point if extra marks available

# Q2. Amino Acids and Peptides (5+5, multi-part)

## (i) Definitions with Examples

**Protein:** High molecular weight biopolymer formed by condensation of a large number of α-amino acids linked through **peptide bonds**.
*Example:* Insulin, Haemoglobin.

**Amino acid:** An organic compound containing both an **amino group (–NH₂)** and a **carboxylic group (–COOH)** attached to the same carbon (α-carbon).
*Example:* Glycine (H₂NCH₂COOH), Alanine (CH₃CH(NH₂)COOH).

**Essential amino acid:** Amino acid that **cannot be synthesized by the human body** and must be obtained from diet.
*Example:* Valine, Leucine, Lysine.

**Non-essential amino acid:** Amino acid that **can be synthesized in the body** from metabolic intermediates.
*Example:* Glycine, Alanine, Serine.

**Zwitter ion (dipolar ion):** The form of an amino acid in which **–COOH loses H⁺ (–COO⁻)** and **–NH₂ gains H⁺ (–NH₃⁺)**, giving a net neutral, dipolar species that exists in aqueous/solid state.

$$H_2N-CH(R)-COOH \rightleftharpoons {}^+H_3N-CH(R)-COO^-$$

**Isoelectric point (pI):** The **pH at which an amino acid exists entirely as zwitter ion** (net charge = zero) and does not migrate under an electric field.
*Example:* pI of glycine = 5.97.

**Peptide linkage:** The **amide bond (–CO–NH–)** formed between the **–COOH group of one amino acid and –NH₂ group of another**, with loss of one water molecule.

$$H_2N-CHR_1-COOH + H_2N-CHR_2-COOH \rightarrow H_2N-CHR_1-CO-NH-CHR_2-COOH + H_2O$$

**Exam scoring points:**
- Zwitterion: must show **both** –COO⁻ and –NH₃⁺ in same structure
- pI definition must include "**net charge zero**" and "**no migration in electric field**"
- Peptide bond = **–CONH–**, formed with **loss of water** (condensation)
- Give at least one example per definition — examiners deduct marks for missing examples

## (ii) Synthesis of α-Amino Acids

**(a) Strecker Synthesis** (most important — write in full)

$$RCHO + NH_3 \rightarrow RCH(NH_2)OH \xrightarrow{-H_2O} RCH=NH$$
$$RCH=NH + HCN \rightarrow RCH(NH_2)CN \; (\alpha\text{-aminonitrile})$$
$$RCH(NH_2)CN \xrightarrow{H_3O^+/\Delta} RCH(NH_2)COOH + NH_3$$

**Reagents/conditions:** Aldehyde + NH₃ + HCN, then acid hydrolysis.

**(b) Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis** (via malonic ester route for amino acids)

$$Phthalimide \xrightarrow{KOH} \text{Potassium phthalimide} \xrightarrow{RCH(Br)COOC_2H_5} N\text{-substituted phthalimide}$$
$$\xrightarrow{H_3O^+/hydrolysis} RCH(NH_2)COOH + Phthalic\ acid$$

**(c) Malonic Ester Synthesis (Amino) Route:**
$$CH_2(COOC_2H_5)_2 \xrightarrow{Br_2} CHBr(COOC_2H_5)_2 \xrightarrow{NH_3} CH(NH_2)(COOC_2H_5)_2 \xrightarrow{H_3O^+, \Delta} CH(NH_2)COOH \; (\text{glycine})$$

**Exam scoring points:**
- Name the method: **Strecker synthesis** is the most expected answer
- Show **all three steps**: imine formation → HCN addition → hydrolysis
- Reagents HCN and NH₃ must be explicitly shown
- Mention product is **racemic (DL)** mixture since a new chiral centre is formed without chiral control

## (iii) End Group Analysis of Peptides

**Definition:** Chemical methods to identify the **terminal amino acids** (N-terminal and C-terminal) of a peptide/protein chain, used to determine sequence and chain composition.

**(A) N-terminal analysis**

**1. Sanger's Method (DNFB method)**
Reagent: **1-Fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (FDNB/Sanger's reagent)**

$$Peptide-NH_2 + F-C_6H_3(NO_2)_2 \xrightarrow{mild\ alkali} DNP-Peptide$$

Complete acid hydrolysis of the DNP-peptide gives **DNP-amino acid (yellow)**, identified by chromatography → reveals the N-terminal residue.

**2. Edman Degradation** (modern, repeatable method)
Reagent: **Phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC)**

$$Peptide-NH_2 + C_6H_5-N=C=S \rightarrow \text{PTC-peptide} \xrightarrow{mild\ acid} \text{PTH-amino acid} + \text{shortened peptide}$$

Advantage: only **removes and identifies one residue at a time**, rest of peptide remains intact — can be repeated sequentially to sequence the whole chain.

**(B) C-terminal analysis**
**Enzymatic method:** **Carboxypeptidase enzyme** cleaves the C-terminal amino acid selectively, which is then identified.

**Exam scoring points:**
- Sanger's reagent name: **FDNB (1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene)**
- Edman reagent name: **phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC)**
- Key advantage of Edman over Sanger: **sequential/repeatable**, doesn't destroy whole peptide
- Carboxypeptidase used for **C-terminal**, not N-terminal
- State that hydrolysis (6N HCl, reflux) is needed to break peptide into individual amino acids for identification

## (iv) Synthesis of Gly-Ala Peptide

**Target:** Glycylalanine (Gly-Ala) — peptide bond between **–COOH of Glycine** and **–NH₂ of Alanine**.

**Why protecting groups are required:**
Both amino acids possess **both –NH₂ and –COOH** groups. Without protection, glycine's –NH₂ could react with its own –COOH (self-condensation) or with alanine's –COOH randomly, and alanine's free –NH₂ could likewise interfere — giving a **mixture of di-, tri-, and polypeptides** instead of the single desired dipeptide. Protecting groups **block unwanted reactive sites**, ensuring the peptide bond forms **only between the intended –COOH (Gly) and –NH₂ (Ala)**.

**Step 1: Protect the amino group of Glycine** (using carbobenzoxy chloride, **Cbz-Cl**, i.e., benzyl chloroformate)
$$H_2NCH_2COOH + C_6H_5CH_2OCOCl \rightarrow C_6H_5CH_2OCO-NH-CH_2COOH \; (\text{Cbz-Gly-OH})$$

**Step 2: Protect the carboxyl group of Alanine** (as methyl/ethyl ester)
$$CH_3CH(NH_2)COOH + CH_3OH/H^+ \rightarrow CH_3CH(NH_2)COOCH_3 \; (\text{Ala-OMe})$$

**Step 3: Activate the free –COOH of protected glycine** (via DCC, dicyclohexylcarbodiimide, or acid chloride)
$$Cbz-Gly-OH \xrightarrow{DCC} Cbz-Gly-O-C(=NC_6H_{11})NHC_6H_{11} \; (\text{activated ester})$$

**Step 4: Coupling — form the peptide (amide) bond**
$$Cbz-Gly-(activated) + H_2N-CH(CH_3)COOCH_3 \rightarrow Cbz-NH-CH_2-CO-NH-CH(CH_3)-COOCH_3$$

**This –CO–NH– bond (between Gly's carbonyl C and Ala's N) is the peptide linkage.**

**Step 5: Deprotection**
- Remove Cbz group: H₂/Pd (catalytic hydrogenolysis)
- Remove methyl ester: dilute NaOH/hydrolysis

$$Cbz-Gly-Ala-OCH_3 \xrightarrow{H_2/Pd; \; then \; OH^-} H_2N-CH_2-CO-NH-CH(CH_3)-COOH \; (\text{Gly-Ala})$$

**Exam scoring points:**
- Clearly **identify the peptide bond**: –CO–NH– between Gly-COOH and Ala-NH₂
- Explain reason for protecting groups: **prevent self-condensation / polymerization / uncontrolled reaction**
- Name protecting groups: **Cbz (carbobenzoxy) for –NH₂**, **ester for –COOH**
- Mention **DCC** as the coupling/activating agent
- Final deprotection step must be shown to regenerate free peptide

## (v) Essential vs Non-Essential Amino Acids

| Feature | Essential Amino Acid | Non-Essential Amino Acid |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesis in body** | Cannot be synthesized by the body | Can be synthesized by the body |
| **Source** | Must be obtained from diet | Obtained from diet or body's own metabolism |
| **Deficiency effect** | Deficiency causes growth/health disorders | Deficiency rarely causes disorders (body compensates) |
| **Examples** | Valine, Leucine, Isoleucine, Lysine, Threonine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan | Glycine, Alanine, Serine, Glutamic acid, Aspartic acid |
| **Number (in humans)** | 8–10 (varies with age/species) | Remaining ~10–12 amino acids |

**Exam scoring points:**
- Core distinguishing line: "essential = **not synthesized by body**, must come from **diet**"
- At least 3 examples on each side
- Mention deficiency consequence for essential amino acids (adds a mark in descriptive answers)

# FINAL REVISION SUMMARY

## ★ Important Reactions to Memorize
1. **Hinsberg's test** — C₆H₅SO₂Cl/KOH separation of 1°/2°/3° amines
2. **Sandmeyer reaction** — ArN₂⁺ + CuCl/CuBr/CuCN → ArCl/ArBr/ArCN
3. **Gattermann reaction** — ArN₂⁺ + Cu powder/HCl → ArCl
4. **Coupling reaction** — ArN₂⁺ + phenol/aniline (cold, alkaline) → azo dye
5. **Hofmann bromamide degradation** — RCONH₂ + Br₂/NaOH → RNH₂ (one C less)
6. **Curtius degradation** — RCON₃ → RN=C=O → RNH₂
7. **Strecker synthesis** — RCHO + NH₃ + HCN → amino acid
8. **Sanger's method** — FDNB for N-terminal identification
9. **Edman degradation** — PITC for sequential N-terminal sequencing
10. **Peptide coupling** — DCC-mediated amide bond formation

## ★ Important Definitions to Memorize
- 1°/2°/3° amine
- Zwitterion
- Isoelectric point
- Peptide linkage
- Essential/non-essential amino acid

## ★ Important Structures to Practice
- Zwitterion form of glycine: ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻
- Diazonium salt: Ar–N≡N⁺ Cl⁻
- Azo linkage: –N=N–
- Peptide bond: –CO–NH–
- Isocyanate intermediate: R–N=C=O

## ★ Common Mistakes to Avoid
- Don't confuse **Sandmeyer (Cu salt)** with **Gattermann (Cu powder)** — examiners specifically test this difference
- Don't forget **N₂ gas evolution** as observation in Sandmeyer/Gattermann
- Don't write coupling reaction at room temperature — it **must be cold (0–5°C)**
- Don't forget Hofmann/Curtius give amine with **one carbon LESS**
- Don't skip protecting-group justification in peptide synthesis — this is a guaranteed mark
- Don't write zwitterion without **both** charges (+ and –) shown simultaneously
- Don't confuse **pI** (net charge zero) with just "neutral pH"
