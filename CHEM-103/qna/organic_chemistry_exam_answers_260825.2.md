# 1. Amines and Important Reactions — 5 Marks

## (a) 1°, 2° and 3° Amines

Amines are derivatives of ammonia, NH₃, in which one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups.

**Primary (1°) amine** — N attached to **one** alkyl/aryl group.
$$R-NH_2$$
Example: Methylamine, CH₃NH₂

**Secondary (2°) amine** — N attached to **two** alkyl/aryl groups.
$$R_2NH$$
Example: Dimethylamine, (CH₃)₂NH

**Tertiary (3°) amine** — N attached to **three** alkyl/aryl groups.
$$R_3N$$
Example: Trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N

---

## Separation of a Mixture of 1°, 2° and 3° Amines — Hinsberg Method

The mixture is treated with **benzenesulphonyl chloride (C₆H₅SO₂Cl) in aqueous NaOH**.

**1° amine:** forms a sulphonamide with an acidic N–H → **dissolves in NaOH**.
$$RNH_2 + C_6H_5SO_2Cl \rightarrow C_6H_5SO_2NHR + HCl$$
On acidification, the sulphonamide precipitates back out.

**2° amine:** forms an N,N-disubstituted sulphonamide with **no acidic N–H** → **insoluble in NaOH**.
$$R_2NH + C_6H_5SO_2Cl \rightarrow C_6H_5SO_2NR_2 + HCl$$
Separated directly by filtration.

**3° amine:** **does not react** with benzenesulphonyl chloride; stays as free amine in the alkaline layer; extracted by shaking with dilute HCl:
$$R_3N + HCl \rightarrow R_3NH^+Cl^-$$

**1°, 2° and 3° amines are thus separated by the Hinsberg method.**

## (b) Sandmeyer and Gattermann Reactions

Both convert an **aryl diazonium salt** into an aryl halide/nitrile by replacing –N₂⁺.

**Sandmeyer Reaction** — uses a **cuprous salt**:
$$ArN_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{CuCl} ArCl + N_2$$
$$ArN_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{CuBr} ArBr + N_2$$
$$ArN_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{CuCN} ArCN + N_2$$

Example: $C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{CuCl} C_6H_5Cl + N_2$

**Gattermann Reaction** — uses **copper powder + HX** (no cuprous salt needed):
$$ArN_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{Cu/HCl} ArCl + N_2$$
$$ArN_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{Cu/HBr} ArBr + N_2$$

**Key difference:** Sandmeyer requires a **cuprous salt** (CuCl/CuBr/CuCN); Gattermann uses **Cu powder with the corresponding acid**. Both proceed via a free-radical mechanism and evolve N₂ gas.

---

## (c) Coupling Reaction

Aryl diazonium salts couple with activated aromatics (**phenols, aromatic amines**) in **cold (0–5°C), weakly alkaline medium** to give **azo compounds** (–N=N– linkage).

$$C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + C_6H_5OH \xrightarrow{NaOH, 0-5°C} C_6H_5-N=N-C_6H_4OH + HCl$$

The **para**-azo product predominates. Used to make **azo dyes** (e.g., methyl orange).

---

## (d) Sulfa Drug Reaction — and its link to Coupling

**Sulfa drugs** (sulfonamides) contain the –SO₂NH– group. The classic example, **sulfanilamide**, is historically and mechanistically connected to the coupling reaction through **Prontosil**:

Prontosil is an **azo dye**, made by a diazonium **coupling reaction** between a diazotized sulfanilamide-precursor and an aromatic amine. Prontosil itself is inactive *in vitro*, but inside the body it is **reduced (azo bond cleaved)** to release **free sulfanilamide**, the actual antibacterial agent. This discovery (Domagk, 1930s) is why coupling reactions and sulfa drugs are taught together — the azo dye is a **prodrug** for the sulfa drug.

**Synthesis of sulfanilamide:**
$$\text{Acetanilide} \xrightarrow{ClSO_3H} p\text{-acetamidobenzenesulfonyl chloride}$$
$$\xrightarrow{NH_3} p\text{-acetamidobenzenesulfonamide} \xrightarrow{H^+/H_2O} p\text{-aminobenzenesulfonamide (sulfanilamide)}$$

Sulfanilamide acts by competing with **PABA (p-aminobenzoic acid)**, blocking bacterial folic acid synthesis — this is the mechanistic basis of sulfa drug action.

## (e) Hofmann and Curtius Degradation

Both convert an acid derivative to a **primary amine with one carbon less**.

**Hofmann Bromamide Degradation** (balanced equation):
$$RCONH_2 + Br_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow RNH_2 + Na_2CO_3 + 2NaBr + 2H_2O$$

Example: $CH_3CONH_2 \xrightarrow{Br_2/NaOH} CH_3NH_2$ (acetamide → methylamine)

Mechanism outline: amide → N-bromoamide → base abstracts N–H → nitrene rearranges with **migration of R from C to N** → isocyanate (RNCO) → hydrolysis → amine.

**Curtius Degradation** (complete sequence — acid must first become the acid chloride):
$$RCOOH \xrightarrow{SOCl_2} RCOCl \xrightarrow{NaN_3} RCON_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} RNCO + N_2 \xrightarrow{H_2O} RNH_2 + CO_2$$

**Both reactions share a common intermediate — the isocyanate, R–N=C=O — and both proceed with retention of configuration at the migrating carbon.**

# 2. Amino Acids, Peptides and Proteins — 5 Marks

## (i) Important Terms with Examples

**Protein:** High-molecular-mass polymer of α-amino acids joined by peptide bonds.
Example: Insulin, haemoglobin.

**Amino acid:** Contains both an amino group (–NH₂) and a carboxyl group (–COOH) on the same carbon (α-carbon).
$$H_2N-CH(R)-COOH$$
Example: Glycine, NH₂CH₂COOH

**Essential amino acids:** Cannot be synthesized sufficiently by the human body; must come from diet.
Examples: Valine, leucine, lysine, methionine, tryptophan.

**Non-essential amino acids:** Can be synthesized by the human body.
Examples: Glycine, alanine, glutamic acid.

**Zwitterion:** Internal salt form of an amino acid, carrying both a positive and negative charge simultaneously:
$$^+NH_3-CH(R)-COO^-$$

**Isoelectric point (pI):** The pH at which an amino acid carries **zero net charge** (exists entirely as zwitterion) and does not migrate in an electric field.
$$pI = \frac{pK_{a1}+pK_{a2}}{2}$$
*(Note: this simple average applies only to neutral amino acids with no ionizable side chain, e.g., glycine, alanine. For amino acids with an acidic or basic side chain — e.g., glutamic acid, lysine — pI is the average of the two pKa values flanking the zwitterion form, not simply pKa1 and pKa2.)*

**Peptide linkage:** The –CO–NH– bond between the –COOH of one amino acid and the –NH₂ of another.
$$-COOH + H_2N- \rightarrow -CO-NH- + H_2O$$

---

## (ii) Synthesis of an α-Amino Acid — Strecker Synthesis

**Step 1 — Formation of aminonitrile:** aldehyde + ammonia + HCN:
$$RCHO + NH_3 + HCN \rightarrow RCH(NH_2)CN$$

**Step 2 — Hydrolysis** of the nitrile:
$$RCH(NH_2)CN \xrightarrow{H_2O/H^+} RCH(NH_2)COOH$$

Example:
$$CH_3CHO \xrightarrow{NH_3, HCN} CH_3CH(NH_2)CN \xrightarrow{H_2O/H^+} CH_3CH(NH_2)COOH \; (\text{alanine})$$

*(Note: the new stereocentre is formed without chiral control, so the product is racemic — DL-alanine.)*

---

## (iii) End-Group Analysis of Peptides

Determines the **N-terminal and C-terminal** amino acids of a peptide chain.

**N-terminal — Sanger's method:** treat with **FDNB (1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene)**:
$$Peptide-NH_2 \xrightarrow{FDNB} DNP\text{-peptide} \xrightarrow{\text{complete hydrolysis}} DNP\text{-amino acid (identified)}$$

**N-terminal — Edman degradation** (preferred, repeatable): **phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC)** reacts selectively with the N-terminal residue, which is cleaved off as a PTH-amino acid, leaving the rest of the peptide intact — so the process can be **repeated cycle by cycle** to sequence the whole chain.

**C-terminal:** treatment with **carboxypeptidase** enzyme selectively cleaves and releases the C-terminal residue.

## (iv) Synthesis of Gly-Ala Peptide

$$H_2N-CH_2-CO-NH-CH(CH_3)-COOH$$

**The peptide bond (–CO–NH–) forms between the –COOH of glycine and the –NH₂ of alanine.**

**Why protecting groups are required:** Both glycine and alanine carry a free –NH₂ and a free –COOH. Without protection, glycine could self-condense, or its –NH₂ could react with its own –COOH, or with alanine's –COOH in an uncontrolled way — giving a mixture of di-, tri-, and polypeptides instead of the single desired Gly-Ala product. Protecting groups **block the unwanted reactive site** so coupling occurs only at the intended –COOH(Gly)/–NH₂(Ala) pair.

**Step 1 — Protect the amino group of glycine** (Boc protection):
$$Gly \xrightarrow{Boc_2O} Boc-Gly-OH$$

**Step 2 — Protect the carboxyl group of alanine** (as methyl ester):
$$Ala \xrightarrow{CH_3OH/H^+} H_2N-CH(CH_3)-COOCH_3$$

**Step 3 — Activate and couple** using **DCC (dicyclohexylcarbodiimide)** as coupling agent (an acid chloride route is avoided here — Boc is acid-labile and SOCl₂ would risk cleaving it, and acyl chlorides of protected amino acids are prone to racemization):
$$Boc-Gly-OH + H_2N-CH(CH_3)COOCH_3 \xrightarrow{DCC} Boc-Gly-Ala-OCH_3$$

The new **–CO–NH–** bond formed here is the peptide linkage.

**Step 4 — Deprotection:**
$$Boc-Gly-Ala-OCH_3 \xrightarrow{TFA} \; then \; \xrightarrow{dil.\ NaOH} \; H_2N-CH_2-CO-NH-CH(CH_3)-COOH \; (\text{Gly-Ala})$$

## (v) Essential vs Non-Essential Amino Acids

| Essential amino acids | Non-essential amino acids |
|---|---|
| Cannot be synthesized sufficiently by the body | Can be synthesized by the body |
| Must be obtained from food | Need not be obtained from food |
| Examples: leucine, lysine, valine, methionine, tryptophan | Examples: glycine, alanine, glutamic acid |
| Deficiency may occur if diet is inadequate | Body regulates synthesis as required |

**One-line conclusion:** Essential amino acids must be supplied through diet; non-essential amino acids can be synthesized by the body.
