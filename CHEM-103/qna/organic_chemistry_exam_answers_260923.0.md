# Chemistry Q&A

## 1. Carbohydrates

### Q1(i). Asymmetric carbon atom and Mutarotation

**Asymmetric (chiral) carbon atom:** A carbon atom bonded to four different atoms/groups. It has no plane of symmetry and gives rise to optical isomerism (the compound and its mirror image are non-superimposable). Example: C2–C5 of glucose are each asymmetric.

**Mutarotation:** The spontaneous change in optical rotation of a freshly prepared solution of a sugar (e.g., glucose) toward an equilibrium value, due to interconversion between the α- and β-anomeric forms through the open-chain form.

$$\alpha\text{-D-glucose} \; (+112.2°) \rightleftharpoons \text{open-chain} \rightleftharpoons \beta\text{-D-glucose} \;(+18.7°) \rightarrow \text{equilibrium} \;(+52.5°)$$

- Occurs because the anomeric (C1) hydroxyl is formed via a hemiacetal, which can reopen and recyclize.
- Reason for change in specific rotation with time; equilibrium mixture ≈ 36% α + 64% β (traces of open form).

### Q1(iii). Conversion of an aldose into the next higher and lower aldose

img["../../assets/aldose-chain-length-change.svg"]

**Chain lengthening (Kiliani–Fischer synthesis):** Aldose + HCN → cyanohydrin → partial hydrolysis (aldonolactone) → controlled reduction (Na–Hg) → aldose with one more carbon. Since the new C1 becomes a fresh chiral centre, **two epimeric aldoses** are obtained.

**Chain shortening (Ruff degradation):** Aldose → (Br₂/H₂O) aldonic acid → Ca-salt → oxidative decarboxylation with H₂O₂/Fe₂(SO₄)₃ → loses CO₂ → aldose with one fewer carbon (configuration of remaining centres retained).

### Q1(iv). Determination of ring size of D-glucose

Method (Haworth's methylation method):
1. Glucose is **fully methylated** (Ag₂O/CH₃I or Me₂SO₄/NaOH) → pentamethyl glucose (all free –OH, including the ring-forming one, converted to –OCH₃).
2. Pentamethyl glucose is **hydrolysed** with dilute acid — only the glycosidic (anomeric) –OCH₃ at C1 is hydrolysed back to –OH (acetal vs. simple ether reactivity difference), giving **2,3,4,6-tetramethyl glucose**.
3. This tetramethyl glucose is **oxidised** (e.g., HNO₃/Br₂ water then further degraded, or historically identified by comparison) and the position bearing the free –OH (C5) is shown to be the ring-oxygen-bearing carbon.
4. Since C5 (not C4) carries the free –OH after hydrolysis, the ring oxygen bridges **C1–C5**, proving glucose is a **six-membered pyranose ring** (not a 5-membered furanose, which would leave C4 free).

## 2. Dyes and Pigments

### Q2(i). Colour, dyes, pigment, chromophore, auxochrome, dye intermediate, raw materials

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Colour** | Sensation produced when light of certain wavelengths (400–700 nm) is selectively absorbed by a substance and the complementary wavelengths are reflected/transmitted | Absorption of blue light → substance appears yellow/orange |
| **Dye** | A coloured, organic compound that can be applied to a substrate (fibre, leather, paper) and forms a fairly permanent, wash-fast attachment, usually from aqueous solution | Methyl orange, Indigo |
| **Pigment** | A coloured substance, generally insoluble in the medium/vehicle, applied as a fine dispersion (not chemically bonded to the substrate) | Titanium dioxide, Carbon black |
| **Chromophore group** | An unsaturated group capable of absorbing light in the visible region, giving colour to a compound | –N=N– (azo), >C=O, –N=O, quinoid ring |
| **Auxochrome group** | A group that itself has no colour but, when attached to a chromophore-bearing compound, deepens/intensifies colour and confers solubility/affinity for the fibre (salt-forming) | –NH₂, –OH, –COOH, –SO₃H |
| **Dye intermediate** | An organic compound derived from basic raw materials (e.g., benzene, naphthalene) used as a starting material to synthesize dyes | Aniline, β-naphthol, sulfanilic acid |
| **Raw materials for dye manufacture** | Basic feedstocks, chiefly coal-tar/petroleum aromatics | Benzene, toluene, naphthalene, anthracene |

### Q2(ii). Name and structure of three dyes

img["../../assets/three-dyes-structures.svg"]

1. **Methyl orange** — azo dye, acid–base indicator: $(CH_3)_2N-C_6H_4-N=N-C_6H_4-SO_3Na$
2. **Malachite green** — triphenylmethane (basic) dye: $[4\text{-}Me_2N\text{-}C_6H_4]_2C{=}C_6H_4{=}N^+Me_2\,Cl^-$
3. **Indigo** — vat dye (bis-indole), chromophore = conjugated C=C/C=O system, auxochrome = –NH–

### Q2(iii). Classification of dyes

**(a) By chemical structure (constitution):**
- Azo dyes (–N=N–): Methyl orange, Congo red
- Triphenylmethane dyes: Malachite green, Crystal violet
- Anthraquinone dyes: Alizarin
- Indigoid dyes: Indigo
- Phthalocyanine dyes: Copper phthalocyanine (blue)
- Nitro/Nitroso dyes: Naphthol yellow

**(b) By method of application:**
- **Direct dyes** — applied directly from aqueous (often salt-containing) bath to cotton
- **Acid dyes** — applied from acidic bath, used for wool, silk, nylon (anionic dye–fibre ionic bond)
- **Basic dyes** — cationic dyes, used on acrylic fibres / with mordants on cotton
- **Mordant dyes** — need a metal-salt mordant (e.g., Al³⁺, Cr³⁺) to fix to the fibre
- **Vat dyes** — insoluble; reduced to soluble leuco form for application, re-oxidised on fibre (e.g., Indigo)
- **Reactive dyes** — form a covalent bond with fibre –OH/–NH₂ groups
- **Disperse dyes** — fine aqueous dispersion, for hydrophobic fibres (polyester)

### Q2(iv). Non-textile uses of dyes

- Biological staining (histology, microscopy) — e.g., methylene blue, haematoxylin
- pH indicators — methyl orange, phenolphthalein
- Food, drug and cosmetic colourants (certified/food-grade dyes)
- Paper, leather, ink and plastic colouring
- Photography and laser dyes; solar cell (dye-sensitized) applications
- Biomedical diagnostics (fluorescent dyes for DNA/protein detection)

## 3. Amines

### Q3. Classification of amines and separation of 1°, 2°, 3° amines

**Classification** (by number of H atoms of NH₃ replaced by alkyl/aryl groups):
- **1° (primary) amine**: $RNH_2$ — one substituent (e.g., $CH_3NH_2$)
- **2° (secondary) amine**: $R_2NH$ — two substituents (e.g., $(CH_3)_2NH$)
- **3° (tertiary) amine**: $R_3N$ — three substituents (e.g., $(CH_3)_3N$)

**Separation — Hinsberg method** (uses benzenesulfonyl chloride, $C_6H_5SO_2Cl$, and aqueous KOH):

img["../../assets/amine-separation-hinsberg.svg"]

- 1° amine → $RNHSO_2C_6H_5$: retains an acidic N–H, **dissolves in KOH** (as K⁺ salt); regenerated by acid hydrolysis.
- 2° amine → $R_2NSO_2C_6H_5$: no N–H, **insoluble in KOH**; filtered off, regenerated by hydrolysis with conc. HCl.
- 3° amine: does not react with Hinsberg reagent (no N–H to substitute); separated directly by extraction/distillation.

### Q3(i). Sandmeyer and Gattermann reactions

img["../../assets/sandmeyer-gattermann-reaction.svg"]

Both convert an **aromatic diazonium salt** into an aryl halide (or other group), replacing $-N_2^+$.

- **Sandmeyer reaction:** $ArN_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{CuCl/HCl} ArCl$ (or $CuBr/HBr \rightarrow ArBr$; $CuCN/KCN \rightarrow ArCN$). Requires cuprous halide catalyst; proceeds via a free-radical mechanism.
- **Gattermann reaction:** Same overall transformation but uses **freshly precipitated copper powder** with the corresponding halogen acid (Cu/HCl → ArCl; Cu/HBr → ArBr) instead of a cuprous salt — milder, avoids handling Cu(I) salts directly.

### Q3(ii). Coupling reaction and Sulfa drug

img["../../assets/coupling-sulfa-drug-reaction.svg"]

**Coupling reaction:** An aromatic diazonium salt reacts with an activated aromatic ring (phenol or aromatic amine, electrophilic aromatic substitution) at low temperature (0–5 °C) and controlled pH to give a brightly coloured **azo compound**:

$$ArN_2^+Cl^- + Ar'H \xrightarrow{pH\approx 9\text{–}10} Ar-N=N-Ar' + HCl$$

Coupling occurs *para* (or *ortho* if *para* blocked) to the activating group of $Ar'H$. This is the key step in manufacturing azo dyes (e.g., methyl orange).

**Sulfa drug (sulfanilamide) synthesis:**
1. Aniline + CH₃COCl → Acetanilide (protects –NH₂)
2. Acetanilide + ClSO₃H (chlorosulfonic acid) → *p*-acetamidobenzenesulfonyl chloride
3. + NH₃ → *p*-acetamidobenzenesulfonamide
4. Hydrolysis (dil. HCl) removes the acetyl group → **Sulfanilamide**, $p\text{-}H_2N\text{-}C_6H_4\text{-}SO_2NH_2$ (first synthetic antibacterial sulfa drug)

### Q3(iii). Hofmann and Curtius degradations

img["../../assets/hofmann-curtius-degradation.svg"]

Both convert a carboxamide/derivative into a **primary amine with one fewer carbon**, via an isocyanate intermediate and 1,2-migration of R from carbon to (electron-deficient) nitrogen.

**Hofmann bromamide degradation:**
$$RCONH_2 + Br_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow RNH_2 + Na_2CO_3 + 2NaBr + 2H_2O$$
Mechanism: amide → N-bromoamide → base removes N–H → nitrene-like anion loses Br⁻ with concerted R-migration → alkyl/aryl isocyanate ($R-N=C=O$) → hydrolysis → $RNH_2 + CO_2$.

**Curtius degradation:**
$$RCON_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta,\,-N_2} R-N=C=O \xrightarrow{H_2O,\,-CO_2} RNH_2$$
The acyl azide ($RCON_3$, from acid chloride + $NaN_3$, or acid hydrazide + $HNO_2$) is thermally decomposed, losing $N_2$ with R-migration to give the isocyanate, which hydrolyses to the amine.

## 4. Amino Acids, Proteins and Peptides

### Q4(i). Protein, Amino acid, Essential/Non-essential amino acid, Zwitterion, Isoelectric point, Peptide linkage

| Term | Definition/Example |
|---|---|
| **Protein** | A high-molecular-weight biopolymer of α-amino acids joined by peptide (amide) bonds, folded into a specific 3-D structure; performs structural, catalytic (enzymes), transport, and regulatory functions |
| **Amino acid** | An organic compound containing both an amino ($-NH_2$) and a carboxyl ($-COOH$) group, generally on the same (α) carbon; the building block of proteins. E.g., glycine $H_2N-CH_2-COOH$ |
| **Essential amino acid** | Cannot be synthesized by the human body; must be obtained from diet. E.g., leucine, lysine, valine, threonine |
| **Non-essential amino acid** | Can be synthesized by the body from other metabolites. E.g., glycine, alanine, glutamic acid |
| **Zwitterion** | The dipolar ionic form of an amino acid, $H_3N^+-CH(R)-COO^-$, existing due to internal proton transfer from –COOH to –NH₂; electrically neutral overall |
| **Isoelectric point (pI)** | The pH at which an amino acid exists predominantly as the zwitterion (net charge zero) and does not migrate in an electric field |
| **Peptide linkage** | The amide bond, $-CO-NH-$, formed between the –COOH of one amino acid and the –NH₂ of the next, with loss of H₂O |

### Q4(ii). Synthesis of α-amino acid (Strecker synthesis)

img["../../assets/strecker-synthesis.svg"]

$$RCHO \xrightarrow{NH_3/NH_4Cl} RCH=NH \xrightarrow{HCN} RCH(NH_2)CN \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} RCH(NH_2)COOH$$

An aldehyde condenses with ammonia to give an imine, which adds HCN to form an α-aminonitrile; acid hydrolysis of the nitrile gives the racemic α-amino acid.

### Q4(iii). End-group analysis of peptides

img["../../assets/end-group-analysis.svg"]

**Sanger's method:** Peptide + **FDNB** (1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene) reacts with the free N-terminal $-NH_2$ (nucleophilic aromatic substitution) → DNP-peptide → complete acid hydrolysis (6N HCl) releases a yellow **DNP-amino acid**, identified chromatographically → reveals the N-terminal residue.

**Edman degradation:** Peptide + **PITC** (phenylisothiocyanate) reacts with the N-terminal amine → mild acid selectively cleaves only the terminal residue as a **PTH-amino acid**, leaving the rest of the peptide intact — allows repeated cycles, giving the full sequence from the N-terminus.

(C-terminal analysis is typically done by carboxypeptidase enzymatic digestion or hydrazinolysis, releasing the C-terminal residue.)

### Q4(iv). Synthesis of Gly–Ala peptide

img["../../assets/peptide-linkage-and-synthesis.svg"]

1. **Protect** the amino group of glycine: $Gly-NH_2 + C_6H_5CH_2OCOCl \rightarrow Cbz-NH-CH_2-COOH$ (carbobenzoxy-glycine)
2. **Activate** the carboxyl: $Cbz-Gly-COOH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow Cbz-Gly-COCl$
3. **Couple** with alanine: $Cbz-Gly-COCl + H_2N-CH(CH_3)-COOH \rightarrow Cbz-Gly-CO-NH-CH(CH_3)-COOH$
4. **Deprotect** by catalytic hydrogenolysis ($H_2/Pd$), which removes the Cbz group without touching the new peptide bond:
$$H_2N-CH_2-CO-NH-CH(CH_3)-COOH \;=\; Gly\text{-}Ala$$

### Q4(v). Essential vs. Non-essential amino acids

| Essential | Non-essential |
|---|---|
| Cannot be synthesized by the body; must come from diet | Can be synthesized in the body from metabolic intermediates |
| Deficiency causes growth/health disorders (e.g., kwashiorkor) | Deficiency is rare under normal metabolism |
| Examples: Leucine, Isoleucine, Valine, Lysine, Threonine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan, Histidine (9 in humans) | Examples: Glycine, Alanine, Serine, Glutamic acid, Aspartic acid, Proline |
