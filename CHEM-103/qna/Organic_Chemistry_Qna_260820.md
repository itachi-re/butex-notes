## 1. Amines and Diazonium Chemistry

### 1(a) Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Amines

**Basis of classification:** Amines are classified according to the **number of hydrogen atoms of ammonia (NH₃) replaced by alkyl/aryl groups**.

| Class | General structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1° amine | R–NH₂ (one H replaced) | CH₃CH₂NH₂ (ethylamine) |
| 2° amine | R–NH–R′ (two H replaced) | (CH₃)₂NH (dimethylamine) |
| 3° amine | R–N(R′)(R″) (three H replaced) | (CH₃)₃N (trimethylamine) |

R, R′, R″ may be alkyl or aryl, same or different.

**Separation of a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines — Hinsberg's Method**

*Principle:* Benzenesulfonyl chloride (**Hinsberg's reagent**, C₆H₅SO₂Cl) reacts differently with each class of amine because of the differing number of N–H bonds available.

**Reactions:**

1. **1° amine** — forms an *N*-alkylbenzenesulfonamide with one replaceable H still on N (acidic due to –SO₂– withdrawing electron density):
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_2\text{Cl} + \text{RNH}_2 \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_2\text{NHR} + \text{HCl}$$
This sulfonamide has an acidic N–H (due to the strong –I/–M effect of the SO₂ group), so it **dissolves in aqueous KOH** forming a soluble salt:
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_2\text{NHR} + \text{KOH} \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_2\text{NR}^-\text{K}^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

2. **2° amine** — forms an *N,N*-dialkylbenzenesulfonamide with **no H left on N**:
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_2\text{Cl} + \text{R}_2\text{NH} \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_2\text{NR}_2 + \text{HCl}$$
No acidic N–H is left, so this product is **insoluble in KOH** and remains as a precipitate/oil.

3. **3° amine** — has no N–H to substitute; it does not react with benzenesulfonyl chloride under these conditions. It simply **remains unreacted** and, being basic, **dissolves in dilute HCl** (as R₃NH⁺Cl⁻) while the sulfonamides do not.

**Steps in separation:**
- Treat the amine mixture with C₆H₅SO₂Cl/KOH(aq).
- **Filter/extract with ether or shake with dil. HCl**: the unreacted 3° amine dissolves in the acid layer (as its hydrochloride) — separated by acidification/basification.
- **Treat the residue with excess KOH(aq):**
  - Soluble part = sulfonamide of 1° amine (salt). Acidify with HCl to regenerate C₆H₅SO₂NHR, then hydrolyse (boil with HCl) to get back the free 1° amine.
  - Insoluble part = sulfonamide of 2° amine. Hydrolysed (boiling with acid/alkali) to regenerate the free 2° amine.

**Key distinguishing reactions (quick reference):**

| Amine | With HNO₂ (cold) | With Hinsberg's reagent |
|---|---|---|
| 1° (aromatic) | Diazonium salt | Soluble sulfonamide (in KOH) |
| 2° | N-Nitrosamine (yellow oil) | Insoluble sulfonamide |
| 3° | Forms unstable salt (aromatic 3° gives C-nitroso product) | No reaction |

**Exam takeaway:**
- Classification = number of H of NH₃ replaced.
- Hinsberg's reagent = C₆H₅SO₂Cl.
- 1° → soluble sulfonamide (acidic N–H); 2° → insoluble sulfonamide (no N–H); 3° → no reaction, separated via HCl solubility.

### 1(b) Sandmeyer and Gattermann Reactions

**Diazotization (needed first):** Primary aromatic amines react with nitrous acid (NaNO₂ + dil. HCl/H₂SO₄) at **0–5 °C** to form aryl diazonium salts.

$$\text{ArNH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0-5^\circ C} \text{ArN}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Low temperature is essential because diazonium salts decompose above ~5–10 °C.

**Sandmeyer Reaction**

Aryl diazonium salts react with **cuprous salts (Cu₂Cl₂, Cu₂Br₂, CuCN)** in the presence of the corresponding hydrohalic/cyanide acid; the diazo group is replaced by –Cl, –Br, or –CN via a radical mechanism involving Cu(I)/Cu(II).

$$\text{ArN}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow[\text{HCl}]{\text{Cu}_2\text{Cl}_2} \text{ArCl} + \text{N}_2$$
$$\text{ArN}_2^+\text{Br}^- \xrightarrow[\text{HBr}]{\text{Cu}_2\text{Br}_2} \text{ArBr} + \text{N}_2$$
$$\text{ArN}_2^+ + \text{CuCN/KCN} \rightarrow \text{ArCN} + \text{N}_2$$

*Example:* Benzenediazonium chloride + Cu₂Cl₂/HCl → chlorobenzene + N₂.

**Gattermann Reaction**

A variant that uses **finely divided copper powder** (instead of cuprous salts) together with the corresponding halogen acid to replace –N₂⁺ by –Cl or –Br. Copper acts as a catalyst/reducing agent.

$$\text{ArN}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{Cu powder, HCl}} \text{ArCl} + \text{N}_2$$
$$\text{ArN}_2^+\text{Br}^- \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{Cu powder, HBr}} \text{ArBr} + \text{N}_2$$

**Distinction (do not confuse):**

| Feature | Sandmeyer | Gattermann |
|---|---|---|
| Reagent | Cuprous halide/cyanide (Cu₂Cl₂, Cu₂Br₂, CuCN) | Copper powder + HX |
| Yield | Generally higher | Somewhat lower |
| Scope | Cl, Br, CN | Chiefly Cl, Br |
| Note | Classical, more widely used industrially | Cheaper reagent (Cu metal), simpler prep |

*(Note: "Gattermann reaction" also refers separately to the Gattermann aldehyde synthesis (HCN/HCl/AlCl₃ on arenes) — a different reaction. In the context of diazonium chemistry, "Gattermann reaction" means the Cu-powder halogenation described above.)*

**Exam takeaway:**
- Diazotization: ArNH₂ + NaNO₂/HCl, 0–5 °C → ArN₂⁺Cl⁻.
- Sandmeyer: Cu₂Cl₂/Cu₂Br₂/CuCN + HX → ArCl/ArBr/ArCN.
- Gattermann: Cu powder + HX → ArCl/ArBr (no CN version in standard syllabus).

### 1(c) Coupling Reaction and Sulfa-Drug (Sulfanilamide) Reaction

**Azo Coupling Reaction**

Aryl diazonium salts act as weak electrophiles and react with activated aromatic rings (phenols, aromatic amines — the **coupling component**) via **electrophilic aromatic substitution** to form brightly coloured **azo compounds** (–N=N– linkage). This is the basis of azo dye chemistry.

- **Conditions:** with phenols — coupling carried out in **weakly alkaline** medium (pH 8–10); with amines — coupling carried out in **weakly acidic to neutral** medium.
- **Preferred position:** substitution occurs predominantly at the **para** position (relative to –OH or –NR₂) of the coupling component; if para is blocked, ortho coupling occurs.

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{NaOH (weakly alkaline)}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5-\text{N=N}-\text{C}_6\text{H}_4-\text{OH}(p) $$

Product: **p-hydroxyazobenzene** (an azo dye), orange-red in colour.

Another classic example: benzenediazonium chloride + N,N-dimethylaniline → **p-(dimethylamino)azobenzene** ("butter yellow").

**"Sulfa-Drug" Reaction (Preparation of Sulfanilamide)**

In the context of aromatic diazonium/amine chemistry, the standard undergraduate topic referred to as the "sulfa drug reaction" is the **synthesis of sulfanilamide**, the parent compound of the sulfa (sulphonamide) drugs, starting from aniline. It is **not** a reaction of the diazonium salt itself, but is grouped with amine chemistry because it starts from aniline and proceeds through protection of the –NH₂ group.

**Steps:**

1. **Acetylation of aniline** (protects –NH₂ from oxidation/over-sulfonation):
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + (\text{CH}_3\text{CO})_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NHCOCH}_3 + \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$$

2. **Chlorosulfonation** with excess chlorosulfonic acid (ClSO₃H) at the *para* position:
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NHCOCH}_3 + 2\,\text{ClSO}_3\text{H} \rightarrow p\text{-CH}_3\text{CONH-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{-SO}_2\text{Cl} + \text{HCl} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$$

3. **Ammonolysis** (treatment with excess NH₃) converts –SO₂Cl to –SO₂NH₂:
$$p\text{-CH}_3\text{CONH-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{-SO}_2\text{Cl} + 2\text{NH}_3 \rightarrow p\text{-CH}_3\text{CONH-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{-SO}_2\text{NH}_2 + \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$$

4. **Hydrolysis** (deacetylation, dil. HCl or NaOH) removes the acetyl protecting group to liberate the free amine:
$$p\text{-CH}_3\text{CONH-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{-SO}_2\text{NH}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+/\Delta} p\text{-H}_2\text{N-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{-SO}_2\text{NH}_2 \;(\textbf{Sulfanilamide})$$

**Exam takeaway:**
- Coupling: ArN₂⁺ + phenol/amine (activated ring) → azo compound (–N=N–), para-selective, mild alkaline (phenols) or neutral/acidic (amines).
- Sulfa-drug topic = synthesis of **sulfanilamide** from aniline via acetylation → chlorosulfonation → ammonolysis → hydrolysis. Acetylation is essential to protect –NH₂.

### 1(d) Hofmann and Curtius Degradation

**Hofmann Bromamide Degradation (Rearrangement)**

*Definition:* Conversion of a primary amide to a primary amine having **one carbon atom fewer** than the amide, by treatment with **bromine (or chlorine) and concentrated/aqueous alkali (KOH/NaOH)**.

$$\text{RCONH}_2 + \text{Br}_2 + 4\text{KOH} \rightarrow \text{RNH}_2 + \text{K}_2\text{CO}_3 + 2\text{KBr} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**Mechanism (key steps):**
1. Br₂/KOH converts the amide N–H to an *N*-bromoamide, RCONHBr.
2. Base removes the remaining N–H to give the anion RCONBr⁻.
3. **Key rearrangement step:** the R group migrates from carbon to nitrogen with loss of Br⁻, forming an **isocyanate**, R–N=C=O. This migration occurs with **retention of configuration** at R (if R is chiral) and is concerted with loss of the leaving group — no free carbocation/nitrene intermediate on R.
4. The isocyanate is rapidly hydrolysed by aqueous alkali to a carbamic acid, which spontaneously loses CO₂ to give the amine.
$$\text{R-N=C=O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{RNHCOOH} \rightarrow \text{RNH}_2 + \text{CO}_2$$

This loss of CO₂ (as carbonate, in the equation above) is why the **product amine has one carbon less than the starting amide**.

*Example:* CH₃CONH₂ (acetamide) + Br₂/KOH → CH₃NH₂ (methylamine) + K₂CO₃ + KBr + H₂O.

**Curtius Rearrangement (Degradation)**

*Definition:* Conversion of an **acyl azide** to a primary amine with loss of one carbon (as CO₂/N₂), by thermal decomposition followed by hydrolysis.

**Steps/reagents:**
1. Acid chloride + sodium azide (NaN₃) → acyl azide:
$$\text{RCOCl} + \text{NaN}_3 \rightarrow \text{RCON}_3 + \text{NaCl}$$
2. **Heat** the acyl azide (Δ): it loses N₂, and R migrates from C to N (analogous rearrangement to Hofmann) to give an **isocyanate** intermediate:
$$\text{RCON}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{R-N=C=O} + \text{N}_2$$
3. **Hydrolysis** of the isocyanate (with H₂O/H₃O⁺) gives the primary amine (via unstable carbamic acid, losing CO₂):
$$\text{R-N=C=O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{RNH}_2 + \text{CO}_2$$

*Example:* CH₃CH₂COCl → (NaN₃) → CH₃CH₂CON₃ → (Δ) → CH₃CH₂N=C=O → (H₂O) → CH₃CH₂NH₂ (ethylamine).

**Comparison Table**

| Feature | Hofmann Degradation | Curtius Degradation |
|---|---|---|
| Starting material | Primary amide, RCONH₂ | Acyl azide, RCON₃ (from acid chloride + NaN₃) |
| Reagent/condition | Br₂ (or Cl₂) + aq./conc. KOH | Heat (Δ), then H₂O for hydrolysis |
| Key intermediate | N-bromoamide → nitrene-like rearrangement → isocyanate | Acyl azide → loses N₂ → isocyanate |
| Common intermediate | Isocyanate (R–N=C=O) | Isocyanate (R–N=C=O) |
| Final product | 1° amine, RNH₂ (one C less than amide) | 1° amine, RNH₂ (one C less than acid) |
| Stereochemistry at R | Retention | Retention |

**Exam takeaway:**
- Both proceed through an **isocyanate intermediate** and both give **RNH₂ with one carbon fewer**, migration occurs **with retention of configuration**.
- Hofmann: amide + Br₂/KOH. Curtius: acid chloride → azide → heat → hydrolysis.

## 2. Amino Acids, Peptides and Proteins

### 2(a) Important Definitions

**1. Protein**
A high-molecular-weight biopolymer made of many **α-amino acid** units joined by **peptide (amide) bonds**, folded into specific three-dimensional structures that carry out structural, catalytic (enzymes), transport, and regulatory functions.

**2. Amino acid**
An organic compound containing both an **amino group (–NH₂)** and a **carboxylic acid group (–COOH)**. In α-amino acids (the biologically important class), both groups are attached to the same (α) carbon:
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–CHR–COOH}$$
*Example:* Glycine, H₂N–CH₂–COOH (R = H).

**3. Essential amino acid**
An amino acid that the human body **cannot synthesize** (or cannot synthesize in adequate amounts) and must be obtained from the diet.
*Examples:* Leucine, Lysine, Valine, Threonine.

**4. Non-essential amino acid**
An amino acid that the human body **can synthesize** in sufficient quantity from metabolic intermediates, so dietary intake is not essential.
*Examples:* Glycine, Alanine, Serine, Glutamic acid.

**5. Zwitterion**
The **dipolar/inner-salt form** of an amino acid, in which the –COOH group has donated a proton to the –NH₂ group, giving simultaneous positive and negative charges on the same molecule while the net charge is zero:
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–CHR–COOH} \; \rightleftharpoons \; \text{H}_3\text{N}^+\text{–CHR–COO}^-$$
This explains why amino acids are **amphoteric** (they can act as both acid and base):
- In strongly acidic solution (low pH): fully protonated **cation**, H₃N⁺–CHR–COOH.
- At intermediate pH: **zwitterion**, H₃N⁺–CHR–COO⁻ (predominant, net charge zero).
- In strongly basic solution (high pH): **anion**, H₂N–CHR–COO⁻.

**6. Isoelectric point (pI)**
The **pH at which an amino acid exists predominantly as the zwitterion**, i.e., the pH at which its **net charge is zero** and it does not migrate in an electric field (used in electrophoresis).

- For a simple **neutral** amino acid (one –NH₂, one –COOH, e.g. glycine, alanine), the pI is the average of the two relevant pKₐ values:
$$\text{pI} = \frac{pK_{a1} + pK_{a2}}{2}$$
where pKₐ₁ is for the –COOH group and pKₐ₂ is for the –NH₃⁺ group.
*Example (glycine):* pKₐ₁ ≈ 2.34, pKₐ₂ ≈ 9.60 → pI ≈ (2.34 + 9.60)/2 ≈ **5.97**.

- For **acidic amino acids** (e.g. aspartic acid, glutamic acid — extra –COOH side chain), pI is the average of the **two most acidic pKₐ values** (the two –COOH groups):
$$\text{pI} = \frac{pK_{a1} + pK_{a(R)}}{2}$$

- For **basic amino acids** (e.g. lysine, arginine — extra –NH₂/basic side chain), pI is the average of the **two most basic pKₐ values** (the two amino/basic groups):
$$\text{pI} = \frac{pK_{a2} + pK_{a(R)}}{2}$$

**7. Peptide linkage (peptide bond)**
The **amide bond (–CO–NH–)** formed between the α-**carboxyl group** of one amino acid and the α-**amino group** of another, with loss of a water molecule:
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–CHR}_1\text{–COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{N–CHR}_2\text{–COOH} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{N–CHR}_1\text{–CO–NH–CHR}_2\text{–COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
This is a planar, partially double-bond character linkage (due to resonance with the C=O), which restricts rotation and is important in protein secondary structure.

**Exam takeaway:**
- Zwitterion = internal salt (H₃N⁺–CHR–COO⁻); explains amphoteric behaviour.
- pI = pH of zero net charge = average of the two pKₐ values flanking the neutral (zwitterion) form.
- Peptide bond = –CO–NH– between –COOH of one amino acid and –NH₂ of the next, with loss of H₂O.

### 2(b) Synthesis of α-Amino Acids

**Most commonly expected method in undergraduate exams: the Strecker Synthesis** (Gabriel synthesis, discussed below, is primarily a method for **primary amines**, and its adaptation to amino acids—via the malonic ester-type route—is a secondary/supplementary method; Strecker's synthesis is the standard direct route to α-amino acids.)

**1. Strecker Synthesis**

*Starting materials:* An aldehyde (RCHO), ammonia (NH₃), and hydrogen cyanide (HCN) [often generated in situ from NH₄Cl + NaCN or KCN].

**Steps:**

1. The aldehyde reacts with NH₃ to form an **imine**, which adds HCN to give an **α-aminonitrile**:
$$\text{RCHO} + \text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{RCH=NH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
$$\text{RCH=NH} + \text{HCN} \rightarrow \text{RCH(NH}_2\text{)CN}$$
(Often written directly: RCHO + NH₃ + HCN → RCH(NH₂)CN, the α-aminonitrile)

2. **Hydrolysis** of the nitrile (boiling with aqueous acid, e.g. HCl, or alkali) converts –CN to –COOH:
$$\text{RCH(NH}_2\text{)CN} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+/\Delta} \text{RCH(NH}_2\text{)COOH} + \text{NH}_3 \;(\text{as its salt, then neutralized})$$

*General equation:*
$$\text{RCHO} \xrightarrow{\text{NH}_3} \text{RCH=NH} \xrightarrow{\text{HCN}} \text{RCH(NH}_2\text{)CN} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+, \Delta} \text{RCH(NH}_2\text{)COOH}$$

*Example:* Acetaldehyde (CH₃CHO) + NH₃ + HCN → CH₃CH(NH₂)CN → (hydrolysis) → **Alanine**, CH₃CH(NH₂)COOH.

*Limitation:* Produces the amino acid as a **racemic (DL) mixture** since the intermediate imine is attacked by CN⁻ from either face (no chiral induction) — the product is not optically pure and needs resolution if a single enantiomer is required.

**2. Gabriel Phthalimide Method (adapted for amino acid synthesis)**

*Note:* The classical Gabriel synthesis makes primary amines from phthalimide. Its adaptation to α-amino acids uses **phthalimidomalonic ester**.

**Steps:**

1. Potassium phthalimide is alkylated with **bromomalonic ester**, Br–CH(COOC₂H₅)₂:
$$\text{Phthalimide-K}^+ + \text{BrCH(COOC}_2\text{H}_5)_2 \rightarrow \text{Phthalimide–N–CH(COOC}_2\text{H}_5)_2 + \text{KBr}$$

2. The active methine hydrogen is alkylated with an alkyl halide (R–X) using a base (e.g., NaOEt):
$$\text{Phthalimide–N–CH(COOC}_2\text{H}_5)_2 \xrightarrow{\text{NaOEt, RX}} \text{Phthalimide–N–CR(COOC}_2\text{H}_5)_2$$

3. **Hydrolysis** (acidic or with hydrazine, Ing–Manske) removes the phthalimide group to liberate the amino group, and hydrolyses the esters to acids; the resulting substituted malonic acid derivative undergoes **decarboxylation** (loses one –COOH) on heating:
$$\text{Phthalimide–N–CR(COOC}_2\text{H}_5)_2 \xrightarrow[2)\,\Delta \,(-\text{CO}_2)]{1)\,\text{H}_3\text{O}^+/\Delta \text{ (or N}_2\text{H}_4)} \text{H}_2\text{N–CHR–COOH}$$

*Example:* Using R = CH₃ (from CH₃I) gives, after hydrolysis and decarboxylation, **alanine**, CH₃CH(NH₂)COOH.

*Limitation:* Multi-step, also gives the racemic (DL) amino acid; mainly of pedagogical/historical value for amino acid synthesis compared to Strecker's more direct route.

**Exam takeaway:**
- **Strecker synthesis is the standard expected answer**: RCHO + NH₃ + HCN → aminonitrile → hydrolysis → amino acid. Gives racemic product.
- Gabriel phthalimide route (via phthalimidomalonic ester) is a supplementary method — alkylation, hydrolysis, decarboxylation.

### 2(c) End-Group Analysis of Peptides

*Definition:* End-group analysis refers to chemical methods used to identify the **terminal amino acid residues** of a peptide/protein chain — the **N-terminal** (free α-NH₂ end) and the **C-terminal** (free α-COOH end) residues. This is distinct from total amino-acid composition analysis (acid hydrolysis + chromatography), which only tells *which* amino acids are present, not their sequence/position.

**A. N-Terminal Analysis**

**1. Sanger's Method (DNFB method)**

*Reagent:* 1-Fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (**DNFB**, Sanger's reagent).

*Principle:* DNFB reacts with the **free α-amino group** at the N-terminus (nucleophilic aromatic substitution, the amine displacing fluoride) to form a stable **yellow dinitrophenyl (DNP)-peptide**:
$$\text{Peptide–NH}_2 + \text{F–C}_6\text{H}_3(\text{NO}_2)_2 \rightarrow \text{Peptide–NH–C}_6\text{H}_3(\text{NO}_2)_2 + \text{HF}$$

The DNP-peptide is then **completely hydrolysed** (conc. HCl); all peptide bonds break, but the DNP group remains covalently attached only to the N-terminal amino acid, which is isolated as a yellow **DNP-amino acid** and identified (e.g., by chromatography), thereby identifying the N-terminal residue.

**2. Edman Degradation**

*Reagent:* **Phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC)**, C₆H₅–N=C=S.

*Principle:* PITC reacts with the free N-terminal α-amino group under mildly alkaline conditions to form a **phenylthiocarbamyl (PTC)-peptide**. On treatment with mild acid (e.g., dilute HCl/anhydrous acid), only the terminal residue is cleaved off (as a cyclic **phenylthiohydantoin, PTH-amino acid**) **without hydrolysing the rest of the peptide chain**:
$$\text{Peptide (n residues)} \xrightarrow{\text{PITC, mild base}} \text{PTC-peptide} \xrightarrow{\text{mild acid}} \text{PTH-amino acid} + \text{Peptide (n-1 residues)}$$

The shortened peptide (one residue less) is now available, and the cycle can be **repeated**, releasing one PTH-amino acid at a time from the N-terminus.

**Why Edman degradation is useful for sequential analysis:** Because cleavage occurs **selectively at the terminal residue under mild conditions**, leaving the rest of the chain intact, the procedure can be repeated cycle after cycle to read off the amino acid sequence **residue-by-residue from the N-terminus**, unlike Sanger's method which destroys the whole peptide in one hydrolysis step (giving information about only the first residue per peptide sample).

**B. C-Terminal Analysis**

*Enzymatic method — Carboxypeptidase:* **Carboxypeptidase** is an exopeptidase enzyme that specifically cleaves the peptide bond **nearest the free C-terminal –COOH group**, releasing the C-terminal amino acid. By following the reaction over time (which amino acid appears first, fastest), the C-terminal residue (and, with successive action, the order of the next few residues) can be identified.

*(Chemical alternative sometimes mentioned: hydrazinolysis, where the peptide is heated with anhydrous hydrazine, converting all residues except the C-terminal one into amino acid hydrazides, leaving the free C-terminal amino acid intact for identification — this is a supplementary chemical method.)*

**Distinction — Sanger's Method vs Edman Degradation**

| Feature | Sanger's Method | Edman Degradation |
|---|---|---|
| Reagent | 1-Fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (DNFB) | Phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) |
| Hydrolysis step | Complete (conc. HCl) — destroys whole peptide | Mild, selective — cleaves only terminal residue |
| Reusability of remaining peptide | Not possible (fully hydrolysed) | Possible — process repeated for sequencing |
| Use | Identifies only the N-terminal residue (single determination) | Sequential N-terminal sequencing (multiple cycles) |

**Exam takeaway:**
- N-terminal: Sanger's (DNFB, one-shot, full hydrolysis) vs Edman (PITC, mild, repeatable — used for sequencing).
- C-terminal: carboxypeptidase (enzymatic).
- End-group analysis ≠ amino acid composition analysis.

### 2(d) Synthesis of Gly-Ala Dipeptide

**Identifying terminal residues:** In "**Gly-Ala**" by convention, the **left-hand residue (Glycine) is the N-terminal** residue (its free α-NH₂ is retained) and the **right-hand residue (Alanine) is the C-terminal** residue (its free α-COOH is retained). The peptide bond forms between the **–COOH of glycine** and the **–NH₂ of alanine**.

**Structure of Gly-Ala** (peptide bond shown in bold):
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–CH}_2\text{–}\mathbf{CO–NH}\text{–CH(CH}_3\text{)–COOH}$$

**Why protecting groups are necessary:** If free glycine and free alanine are simply mixed, the –COOH of one could react with the –NH₂ of the *other* molecule of the *same* amino acid, or **Ala's –COOH could react with Gly's –NH₂** (giving the wrong dipeptide, Ala-Gly, or polymer mixtures), since all molecules have both reactive groups. Selective protection ensures that **only the desired –COOH (of Gly) reacts with only the desired –NH₂ (of Ala)**.

**Step-by-step synthesis:**

**Step 1 — Protect the amino group of glycine** (so that it cannot react with its own or another molecule's –COOH). A common protecting group is **carbobenzoxy (Cbz/benzyloxycarbonyl)**, introduced using carbobenzoxy chloride (C₆H₅CH₂OCOCl):
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–CH}_2\text{–COOH} + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{OCOCl} \xrightarrow{\text{base}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{OCO–NH–CH}_2\text{–COOH} + \text{HCl}$$
(This protected glycine, **Cbz-Gly-OH**, now has a free –COOH but a blocked –NH₂.)

**Step 2 — Protect the carboxyl group of alanine** (so that it cannot react with another alanine's –NH₂), e.g., as its methyl or ethyl ester:
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–CH(CH}_3\text{)–COOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{H}_2\text{N–CH(CH}_3\text{)–COOCH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
(This gives **H-Ala-OMe**, which has a free –NH₂ but a blocked –COOH.)

**Step 3 — Activate the free –COOH of protected glycine** (direct coupling is slow/low-yielding; the acid is converted to a more reactive acyl derivative, e.g., using DCC (dicyclohexylcarbodiimide) or by conversion to an acid chloride/mixed anhydride):
$$\text{Cbz–NH–CH}_2\text{–COOH} \xrightarrow{\text{DCC}} \text{Cbz–NH–CH}_2\text{–CO–(activated)}$$

**Step 4 — Coupling:** The activated Cbz-glycine reacts with the free –NH₂ of the alanine ester to form the protected dipeptide:
$$\text{Cbz–NH–CH}_2\text{–CO–(activated)} + \text{H}_2\text{N–CH(CH}_3\text{)–COOCH}_3 \rightarrow \text{Cbz–NH–CH}_2\text{–CO–NH–CH(CH}_3\text{)–COOCH}_3$$

**Step 5 — Deprotection:**
- Remove the Cbz group by **catalytic hydrogenolysis** (H₂/Pd) or HBr/AcOH, regenerating the free N-terminal –NH₂.
- Hydrolyse the methyl ester (mild aqueous NaOH, then acidify) to regenerate the free C-terminal –COOH.

$$\text{Cbz–NH–CH}_2\text{–CO–NH–CH(CH}_3\text{)–COOCH}_3 \xrightarrow[2)\,\text{OH}^-/\text{H}^+]{1)\,\text{H}_2/\text{Pd}} \text{H}_2\text{N–CH}_2\text{–CO–NH–CH(CH}_3\text{)–COOH} \;(\textbf{Gly-Ala})$$

**Why the final product is specifically Gly-Ala (not Ala-Gly):** Because the –NH₂ of glycine was blocked throughout (only its –COOH was left free and activated) and the –COOH of alanine was blocked throughout (only its –NH₂ was left free), the new peptide bond can only form between **Gly's carbonyl carbon and Ala's nitrogen** — giving Gly(N-terminal)–Ala(C-terminal), not the reverse.

**Racemization consideration:** During activation of the carboxyl group (Step 3), the α-hydrogen adjacent to the activated carbonyl can be epimerized under basic or prolonged reaction conditions, risking **racemization** at that stereocentre. Mild coupling reagents (e.g., DCC, often with additives like HOBt), low temperature, and short reaction times are used to **minimize racemization** and preserve the natural (L) configuration, particularly important for the residue whose carboxyl is being activated.

**Exam takeaway:**
- Gly = N-terminal (free NH₂ protected then freed); Ala = C-terminal (free COOH protected then freed).
- Sequence: protect Gly-NH₂ (Cbz) → protect Ala-COOH (ester) → activate Gly-COOH (DCC) → couple → deprotect both ends.
- Selective protection prevents self-condensation and ensures correct sequence; activation step carries racemization risk.

### 2(e) Essential vs Non-essential Amino Acids

| Criterion | Essential Amino Acids | Non-essential Amino Acids |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Amino acids the body **cannot synthesize** (or not in sufficient amounts) | Amino acids the body **can synthesize** in adequate amounts |
| Body synthesis | Absent / inadequate | Present, sufficient |
| Dietary requirement | **Must** be supplied through diet | Not obligatory through diet |
| Examples | Leucine, Isoleucine, Valine, Lysine, Threonine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan (commonly listed: 8–9 in adults) | Glycine, Alanine, Serine, Aspartic acid, Glutamic acid, Proline |
| Biological significance | Deficiency leads to impaired protein synthesis, growth retardation, health disorders | Body meets its own requirement via metabolic pathways (transamination etc.); dietary lack usually not critical |

**Note on "conditionally essential" amino acids:** Some amino acids (e.g., arginine, histidine, cysteine, tyrosine) are normally synthesized by the body but become **essential under certain physiological conditions** (infancy, illness, metabolic stress) — these are termed **conditionally essential**. This category is mentioned for completeness; the core exam distinction to memorize is essential vs non-essential as defined above.

**Exam takeaway:**
- Essential = not synthesized by body → must come from diet.
- Non-essential = synthesized by body → dietary intake not obligatory.
- Conditionally essential = normally non-essential but required in diet under special conditions.

## Must-Memorize Reactions

1. **Diazotization:** ArNH₂ + NaNO₂ + 2HCl (0–5 °C) → ArN₂⁺Cl⁻ + NaCl + 2H₂O
2. **Sandmeyer:** ArN₂⁺Cl⁻ + Cu₂Cl₂/HCl → ArCl + N₂ (similarly Cu₂Br₂/HBr → ArBr; CuCN/KCN → ArCN)
3. **Gattermann:** ArN₂⁺Cl⁻ + Cu powder/HCl → ArCl + N₂
4. **Azo coupling:** ArN₂⁺ + C₆H₅OH (alkaline) → p-HO-C₆H₄-N=N-C₆H₅ (azo dye)
5. **Sulfanilamide synthesis:** Aniline → (Ac₂O) → acetanilide → (ClSO₃H) → sulfonyl chloride → (NH₃) → sulfonamide → (hydrolysis) → sulfanilamide
6. **Hofmann degradation:** RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4KOH → RNH₂ + K₂CO₃ + 2KBr + 2H₂O (via isocyanate)
7. **Curtius degradation:** RCOCl → (NaN₃) → RCON₃ → (Δ, −N₂) → RNCO → (H₂O) → RNH₂
8. **Strecker synthesis:** RCHO + NH₃ + HCN → RCH(NH₂)CN → (H₃O⁺) → RCH(NH₂)COOH
9. **Sanger's reagent reaction:** Peptide-NH₂ + DNFB → DNP-peptide → (hydrolysis) → DNP-amino acid (N-terminal)
10. **Edman degradation:** Peptide + PITC → PTC-peptide → (mild acid) → PTH-amino acid + shortened peptide
11. **Peptide bond formation:** R₁CH(NH₂)COOH + R₂CH(NH₂)COOH → R₁CH(NH₂)CONH-CHR₂COOH + H₂O

## Common Mistakes Students Make

1. Confusing **Sandmeyer** (cuprous salts, Cu₂Cl₂/Cu₂Br₂/CuCN) with **Gattermann** (copper powder) — writing the wrong reagent for each.
2. Forgetting that diazotization **must be carried out at 0–5 °C**; writing it at room temperature loses marks.
3. Writing azo coupling in **strongly acidic** medium for phenols (it should be weakly alkaline) or forgetting the **para-directing** preference.
4. Confusing the "sulfa drug reaction" with a diazonium coupling reaction — it is actually the **aniline → sulfanilamide** synthetic sequence (acetylation is essential and often forgotten).
5. In Hofmann/Curtius degradation, forgetting that the product amine has **one carbon fewer** than the starting amide/acid, and omitting the **isocyanate intermediate**.
6. Writing Hofmann rearrangement as proceeding through a free carbocation/nitrene with **racemization** — it actually proceeds with **retention of configuration**.
7. Mixing up **Sanger's method** (complete hydrolysis, one-shot N-terminal ID) with **Edman degradation** (mild, repeatable, used for full sequencing).
8. Using **carboxypeptidase** and calling it an N-terminal method — it is specific for the **C-terminal**.
9. In pI calculation, using the wrong pair of pKₐ values for acidic/basic amino acids (must average the two pKₐs that flank the zwitterion, not simply the standard two for neutral amino acids).
10. In Gly-Ala synthesis, forgetting to protect **both** the amino group of the N-terminal residue and the carboxyl group of the C-terminal residue, which can lead to the wrong sequence (Ala-Gly) or polymerization.
11. Assuming the Gabriel phthalimide method is the "main" method for amino acid synthesis — the **Strecker synthesis** is the standard undergraduate expected route.
