## 1. What are alcohols and phenols?

### Alcohols

Alcohols are organic compounds in which one or more hydroxyl groups (–OH) are attached directly to a **saturated carbon atom**, usually an sp³-hybridized carbon atom.

**General formula:**

$$
\mathrm{R-OH}
$$

Here, `R` represents an alkyl group and `–OH` is the hydroxyl group.

Examples:

```text
Methanol:     CH3–OH
Ethanol:      CH3–CH2–OH
Propan-2-ol:  CH3–CH(OH)–CH3
```

Alcohols are classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary according to the number of carbon atoms attached to the carbon bearing the –OH group. They are also commonly used as solvents.

### Phenols

Phenols are aromatic compounds in which a hydroxyl group (–OH) is attached **directly to an aromatic ring**.

**General representation:** `Ar–OH`

**Phenol:** `C6H5OH`

```text
       OH
        |
     ⌬
  Benzene ring
```

The –OH group is attached directly to the benzene ring, not to a saturated side-chain carbon. Phenol shows characteristic reactions of aromatic compounds, including electrophilic aromatic substitution. It is used in the manufacture of chemicals and antiseptic products.

## 2. Distinguish 1°, 2°, and 3° alcohols by dehydrogenation

When alcohol vapour is passed over heated copper at about **300 °C**, primary and secondary alcohols undergo dehydrogenation.

### (i) Primary alcohol (1°)

A primary alcohol gives an **aldehyde** by loss of hydrogen.

```text
       Cu, 300 °C
R–CH2–OH  ───────────→  R–CHO + H2
```

Example:

```text
CH3–CH2–OH  ──Cu, 300 °C──→  CH3–CHO + H2
   Ethanol                         Ethanal
```

### (ii) Secondary alcohol (2°)

A secondary alcohol gives a **ketone** by loss of hydrogen.

```text
       Cu, 300 °C
R–CH(OH)–R′  ───────────→  R–CO–R′ + H2
```

Example:

```text
CH3–CH(OH)–CH3  ──Cu, 300 °C──→  CH3–CO–CH3 + H2
  Propan-2-ol                         Propanone
                                      (acetone)
```

### (iii) Tertiary alcohol (3°)

A tertiary alcohol does not undergo ordinary dehydrogenation to a carbonyl compound because the carbon bearing –OH has **no hydrogen atom** attached to it.

On suitable acidic heating conditions, tertiary alcohols can instead undergo **dehydration** to form an alkene.

Example:

```text
       CH3                                      CH3
        |                                        |
CH3 — C — OH   ──H2SO4, heat──→  CH2 = C — CH3 + H2O
        |
       CH3
  2-Methylpropan-2-ol              2-Methylpropene
```

**Key distinction:** dehydrogenation removes H₂; dehydration removes H₂O.

## 3. Distinguish 1°, 2°, and 3° alcohols by the Lucas test

### Lucas reagent

Lucas reagent is a mixture of **concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl)** and **anhydrous zinc chloride (ZnCl₂)**.

It converts an alcohol into an alkyl chloride. The alkyl chloride generally forms a cloudy or turbid mixture because it is poorly soluble in the aqueous medium.

**General reaction:**

$$
\mathrm{R-OH + HCl \xrightarrow[ ]{ZnCl_2} R-Cl + H_2O}
$$

### Observations

| Type of alcohol | Observation at room temperature | Relative reaction rate |
|---|---|---|
| Tertiary (3°) | Turbidity appears immediately or very rapidly | Fastest |
| Secondary (2°) | Turbidity appears in about 5 minutes | Intermediate |
| Primary (1°) | No turbidity initially; reacts very slowly, usually requiring heating | Slowest |

**Order of reaction with Lucas reagent:**

$$
\boxed{3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ}
$$

The faster reaction of tertiary alcohols is associated with the greater stability of the tertiary carbocation-like intermediate in the usual substitution pathway.

## 4. Comparison among primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohols

### Classification by structure

| Type | Structural requirement | General structure |
|---|---|---|
| Primary (1°) | The carbon bearing –OH is attached to one other carbon atom | `R–CH2–OH` |
| Secondary (2°) | The carbon bearing –OH is attached to two other carbon atoms | `R–CH(OH)–R′` |
| Tertiary (3°) | The carbon bearing –OH is attached to three other carbon atoms | `R–C(OH)(R′)–R″` |

### Structural diagrams

```text
Primary (1°)          Secondary (2°)          Tertiary (3°)

     H                      R                       R
     |                      |                       |
R — C — OH             R — C — OH             R — C — OH
     |                      |                       |
     H                      H                      R′
```

### Important comparative properties

| Property | Primary alcohol | Secondary alcohol | Tertiary alcohol |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation under ordinary conditions | Aldehyde, then carboxylic acid | Ketone | Resistant to mild oxidation; harsher conditions may break C–C bonds |
| Lucas-test rate | Slowest | Intermediate | Fastest |
| Reaction with HX by the usual substitution pathway | Slowest | Intermediate | Fastest |
| Steric hindrance around the carbon bearing –OH | Lowest | Intermediate | Highest |

**Note:** Solubility and boiling point should not be assigned a universal 1°/2°/3° order without specifying the compounds being compared. Molecular size, branching, and hydrogen bonding all affect these properties. For isomeric alcohols, increased branching generally lowers the boiling point.

## 5. Preparation of primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohols

### A. Primary alcohols (1°)

#### (a) Grignard reaction with formaldehyde

Formaldehyde reacts with a Grignard reagent to form a magnesium alkoxide. Acidic hydrolysis then gives a primary alcohol.

**Step 1 — Addition:**

$$
\mathrm{HCHO + RMgX \longrightarrow RCH_2OMgX}
$$

**Step 2 — Hydrolysis:**

$$
\mathrm{RCH_2OMgX \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} RCH_2OH}
$$

Overall:

```text
H–CHO + R–MgX  →  R–CH2–O–MgX  ──H3O+──→  R–CH2–OH
Formaldehyde       Magnesium alkoxide       Primary alcohol
```

#### (b) Hydration of an alkene

Ethene reacts with water in the presence of an acid catalyst to produce ethanol.

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} CH_3CH_2OH}
$$

```text
CH2=CH2 + H–OH  ──acid catalyst──→  CH3–CH2–OH
   Ethene                                  Ethanol
```

### B. Secondary alcohols (2°)

#### Grignard reaction with an aldehyde

An aldehyde other than formaldehyde reacts with a Grignard reagent. Hydrolysis of the magnesium alkoxide gives a secondary alcohol.

$$
\mathrm{RCHO + R'MgX \longrightarrow RCH(OMgX)R'}
$$

$$
\mathrm{RCH(OMgX)R' \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} RCH(OH)R'}
$$

```text
R–CHO + R′–MgX  →  R–CH(OMgX)–R′  ──H3O+──→  R–CH(OH)–R′
 Aldehyde            Magnesium alkoxide          Secondary alcohol
```

### C. Tertiary alcohols (3°)

#### Grignard reaction with a ketone

A ketone reacts with a Grignard reagent to form a magnesium alkoxide. Hydrolysis gives a tertiary alcohol.

$$
\mathrm{R_2C{=}O + R'MgX \longrightarrow R_2C(OMgX)R'}
$$

$$
\mathrm{R_2C(OMgX)R' \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} R_2C(OH)R'}
$$

```text
     O                                  OH
     ||                                 |
R — C — R′ + R″–MgX  →  R — C — R′  ──H3O+──→  R — C — R′
                              |                         |
                            OMgX                      R″
```

Example: acetone (propanone) reacts with a Grignard reagent, followed by hydrolysis, to give a tertiary alcohol.

### Additional reaction shown in the source: hydration of an alkyne

Hydration of propyne in the presence of mercuric sulfate and sulfuric acid gives propanone (acetone) after enol–keto tautomerism.

$$
\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CH + H_2O \xrightarrow{HgSO_4/H_2SO_4} CH_3COCH_3}
$$

This reaction produces a **ketone**, not a secondary alcohol.

## 6. Important reactions

### (i) Reimer–Tiemann reaction

When phenol is heated with chloroform (CHCl₃) and aqueous sodium hydroxide (NaOH), an aldehyde group (–CHO) is introduced mainly at the **ortho position** of the benzene ring. Acidification gives salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde) as the major product.

**Reaction scheme:**

```text
Phenol + CHCl3 + NaOH(aq)  ──heat──→  o-HO–C6H4–CHO
                                         Salicylaldehyde
                                  (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde)
```

A balanced overall representation is:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + CHCl_3 + 3NaOH}
\rightarrow
\mathrm{HOC_6H_4CHO + 3NaCl + 2H_2O}
$$

### (ii) Coupling reaction (azo coupling)

A coupling reaction joins two aromatic components. Phenol reacts with an aromatic diazonium salt in alkaline medium to form an azo compound.

**Example:** phenyl diazonium chloride reacts with phenol to give mainly *p*-hydroxyazobenzene.

```text
C6H5–N2+ Cl−  +  C6H5–OH
 Phenyl diazonium       Phenol
       |
       |  alkaline medium
       v
C6H5–N=N–C6H4–OH
          para

       p-Hydroxyazobenzene
```

The characteristic group in an azo compound is:

$$
\mathrm{-N=N-}
$$

### (iii) Kolbe–Schmitt reaction (Kolbe reaction)

Phenol reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium phenoxide. Sodium phenoxide reacts with carbon dioxide under pressure and heat to form sodium salicylate. Acidification gives salicylic acid (the ortho isomer).

**Step 1 — Formation of sodium phenoxide:**

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5ONa + H_2O}
$$

**Step 2 — Carboxylation:**

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5ONa + CO_2 \xrightarrow[pressure]{heat} HOC_6H_4COONa}
$$

**Step 3 — Acidification:**

$$
\mathrm{HOC_6H_4COONa + H^+ \rightarrow HOC_6H_4COOH}
$$

Product: **salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid; the ortho isomer)**.

### (iv) Rosenmund reduction

Rosenmund reduction converts an acid chloride into an aldehyde by catalytic hydrogenation using a poisoned palladium catalyst, commonly palladium on barium sulfate (Pd/BaSO₄).

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{RCOCl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/BaSO_4} RCHO + HCl}}
$$

The catalyst is poisoned to prevent further reduction of the aldehyde to an alcohol.

## 7. Preparation of salicylic acid and picric acid from phenol

### (a) Salicylic acid from phenol

Salicylic acid is prepared from phenol by the Kolbe–Schmitt reaction.

1. Phenol reacts with aqueous NaOH to form sodium phenoxide.
2. Sodium phenoxide reacts with CO₂ under heat and pressure to form sodium salicylate.
3. Acidification gives salicylic acid.

```text
       OH                         ONa
        |                           |
       ⌬   + NaOH  →               ⌬   + H2O
      Phenol                    Sodium phenoxide

Sodium phenoxide + CO2  ──heat, pressure──→  Sodium salicylate

Sodium salicylate  ──H+──→  Salicylic acid
```

### (b) Picric acid from phenol

Phenol reacts with concentrated nitric acid to form **2,4,6-trinitrophenol**, commonly called picric acid.

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + 3HNO_3 \rightarrow C_6H_2(NO_2)_3OH + 3H_2O}
$$

The three nitro groups are at positions 2, 4, and 6 relative to the –OH group.

```text
                 OH
                  |
                  ⌬
              NO2     NO2
                  NO2

       2,4,6-Trinitrophenol (picric acid)
```

## 8. Reactions of glycerol

Glycerol is propane-1,2,3-triol.

```text
CH2–OH
  |
CH –OH
  |
CH2–OH

Glycerol: HO–CH2–CH(OH)–CH2–OH
```

### (i) Reaction with potassium hydrogen sulfate (KHSO₄)

On heating with KHSO₄, glycerol undergoes dehydration to form acrolein (propenal).

$$
\mathrm{C_3H_8O_3 \xrightarrow[KHSO_4]{\Delta} CH_2{=}CHCHO + 2H_2O}
$$

```text
HO–CH2–CH(OH)–CH2–OH  ──KHSO4, Δ──→  CH2=CH–CHO + 2H2O
          Glycerol                            Acrolein
```

### (ii) Reaction with phosphorus pentoxide (P₂O₅)

P₂O₅ acts as a strong dehydrating agent. On heating, it removes water from glycerol and produces acrolein.

$$
\mathrm{C_3H_8O_3 \xrightarrow[P_2O_5]{\Delta} CH_2{=}CHCHO + 2H_2O}
$$

### (iii) Reaction with nitric acid

Glycerol undergoes nitration to form glyceryl trinitrate (commonly called nitroglycerin).

**Overall reaction (for textbook identification):**

$$
\mathrm{C_3H_5(OH)_3 + 3HNO_3 \rightarrow C_3H_5(ONO_2)_3 + 3H_2O}
$$

The product is **glyceryl trinitrate**. This is an energetic explosive material; the equation is included only as a classroom reaction, not as an experimental procedure.

## 9. Why is phenol acidic in nature?

Phenol is a weak acid. It can donate a proton (H⁺) to form a phenoxide ion.

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH \rightleftharpoons C_6H_5O^- + H^+}
$$

### Main reason: resonance stabilization of the phenoxide ion

After phenol loses H⁺, the negative charge on the phenoxide ion is delocalized by resonance over the oxygen atom and the ortho and para positions of the benzene ring.

A simplified representation is:

```text
C6H5–O−  ↔  resonance forms with negative charge
                 delocalized to the ortho and para positions
```

This delocalization stabilizes the phenoxide ion. The greater stability of the conjugate base makes loss of H⁺ more favourable.

### Reaction with sodium hydroxide

Phenol is acidic enough to react with aqueous NaOH and form sodium phenoxide.

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5ONa + H_2O}
$$

**Exam point:** Phenol is more acidic than ordinary alcohols because its conjugate base (phenoxide ion) is resonance-stabilized.

## 10. Distinguish between phenol and ethanol by chemical tests

### (i) Ferric chloride test

Add a few drops of neutral aqueous ferric chloride solution (FeCl₃).

| Compound | Observation |
|---|---|
| Phenol | Gives a violet, blue, or sometimes green colour due to formation of a coloured iron(III)–phenoxide complex |
| Ethanol | Does not give the characteristic colour |

### (ii) Bromine-water test

Add bromine water to the sample.

**Phenol:** Bromine water is decolourised and a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol is formed.

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + 3Br_2 \rightarrow C_6H_2Br_3OH + 3HBr}
$$

```text
Phenol + 3Br2(aq)  →  2,4,6-tribromophenol ↓ + 3HBr
                         white precipitate
```

**Ethanol:** No reaction occurs under this test condition; the bromine-water colour remains.

## 11. Are all hydroxy compounds alcohols?

**No.** All compounds containing an –OH group are not classified as alcohols.

An alcohol contains a hydroxyl group attached directly to a saturated carbon atom.

### Examples of hydroxy compounds that are not alcohols

**(i) Phenol**

In phenol, the –OH group is attached directly to an aromatic benzene ring.

```text
C6H5–OH
Phenol
```

It is therefore classified as a phenol, not an alcohol.

**(ii) Carboxylic acid**

In a carboxylic acid, the –OH group is part of the carboxyl group (–COOH). It is attached to a carbonyl carbon and is not an alcohol hydroxyl group.

```text
      O
      ||
R — C — OH
  Carboxyl group (–COOH)
```

Therefore, the presence of an –OH group alone does not make a compound an alcohol.

## 12. Preparation of phenol from coal tar

Coal tar contains phenol in one of its oil fractions. The phenol can be separated by extraction with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

### Step 1 — Formation of sodium phenoxide

Phenol reacts with aqueous NaOH to form water-soluble sodium phenoxide.

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5ONa + H_2O}
$$

### Step 2 — Acidification

The sodium phenoxide solution is treated with dilute sulfuric acid to regenerate phenol.

$$
\mathrm{2C_6H_5ONa + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow 2C_6H_5OH + Na_2SO_4}
$$

The phenol is then separated from the mixture.

## 13. What happens when ethanol reacts with dilute NaOH and 2,4-DNPH?

### (i) Ethanol with dilute sodium hydroxide

Ethanol does not react appreciably with dilute aqueous NaOH under ordinary conditions.

**Reason:** Ethanol is a weaker acid than water to a comparable extent and is not acidic enough for dilute NaOH to convert it appreciably into ethoxide under ordinary aqueous conditions.

```text
CH3–CH2–OH + dilute NaOH  →  No appreciable reaction
```

### (ii) Ethanol with 2,4-DNPH (Brady's reagent)

2,4-DNPH means **2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine**. It gives a yellow, orange, or red precipitate with aldehydes and ketones because those compounds contain a carbonyl group (>C=O).

Ethanol is an alcohol and does not contain a carbonyl group. Therefore, it gives **no reaction** with 2,4-DNPH.

```text
CH3–CH2–OH + 2,4-DNPH  →  No reaction
```

## 14. Preparation of ethanol from starch

Ethanol is produced from starch by enzymatic hydrolysis followed by fermentation.

### Step 1 — Conversion of starch into maltose

The enzyme **diastase** breaks down starch into maltose.

Schematic equation:

$$
\mathrm{2(C_6H_{10}O_5) + H_2O \xrightarrow{diastase} C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}}
$$

```text
Starch  ──diastase──→  Maltose
```

### Step 2 — Conversion of maltose into glucose

The enzyme **maltase** hydrolyses maltose into two molecules of glucose.

$$
\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O \xrightarrow{maltase} 2C_6H_{12}O_6}
$$

```text
Maltose + H2O  ──maltase──→  2 Glucose
```

### Step 3 — Fermentation of glucose into ethanol

The enzyme complex **zymase**, present in yeast, converts glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide.

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \xrightarrow{zymase} 2C_2H_5OH + 2CO_2}
$$

```text
Glucose  ──zymase (yeast)──→  Ethanol + Carbon dioxide
```

## 15. How is an alcohol identified?

### (i) Sodium metal test

When sodium metal is added to an alcohol, hydrogen gas is evolved. The gas appears as bubbles.

**General reaction:**

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{2R-OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2R-ONa + H_2\uparrow}}
$$

Example with ethanol:

$$
\mathrm{2CH_3CH_2OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2CH_3CH_2ONa + H_2\uparrow}
$$

### (ii) Lucas test

Add Lucas reagent (concentrated HCl and anhydrous ZnCl₂) to the alcohol. Formation of turbidity indicates the production of an alkyl chloride.

$$
\mathrm{R-OH + HCl \xrightarrow{ZnCl_2} R-Cl + H_2O}
$$

The time taken for turbidity helps distinguish primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohols (see Section 3).

### (iii) Iodoform test

Add iodine (I₂) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to the sample and warm gently. Ethanol gives a **yellow precipitate of iodoform (CHI₃)**.

Overall balanced reaction:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH + 4I_2 + 6NaOH}
\rightarrow
\mathrm{CHI_3\downarrow + HCOONa + 5NaI + 5H_2O}
$$

```text
Ethanol + I2 + NaOH  →  Yellow CHI3 precipitate
                              Iodoform
```

## 16. Important corrections and exam reminders

These points clarify statements or equations in the handwritten source that could otherwise lead to incorrect answers.

1. **Tertiary alcohols:** They do not undergo normal dehydrogenation to a carbonyl compound because the carbon bearing –OH has no hydrogen. Alkene formation is by dehydration, with loss of H₂O, not dehydrogenation with loss of H₂.
2. **Lucas test:** The correct reaction-rate order is `3° > 2° > 1°`. Tertiary alcohols react fastest; primary alcohols react very slowly at room temperature.
3. **Alkyne hydration:** Hydration of propyne gives propanone (acetone), a ketone. It does not give a secondary alcohol.
4. **Boiling point:** For comparable isomeric alcohols, increased branching generally lowers boiling point. Do not memorize a universal boiling-point order based only on 1°, 2°, and 3° classification.
5. **Solubility:** Water solubility depends on molecular size and branching as well as the –OH group. There is no universal solubility order for every primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohol.
6. **Phenol acidity:** The principal explanation is resonance stabilization of the phenoxide ion. The negative charge is delocalized over the oxygen and the ortho/para positions of the ring.
7. **Ethanol and 2,4-DNPH:** Ethanol gives no 2,4-DNPH precipitate because it has no carbonyl group.
8. **Glycerol nitration:** Glyceryl trinitrate is an energetic explosive material. The reaction is included only for academic identification; it is not an experimental instruction.
