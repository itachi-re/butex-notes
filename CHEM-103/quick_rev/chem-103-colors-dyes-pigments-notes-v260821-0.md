## 1. Color

### 1.1 Definition of Color

**Color** is the visual perception produced when light of a particular range of wavelengths in the visible spectrum (approximately 380–750 nm) is reflected, transmitted, or emitted by an object and detected by the human eye/brain.

> **Exam Point:** Color is *not* an inherent property of a substance alone — it results from the interaction between light, matter, and the observer's visual system.

### 1.2 Origin of Color in Substances

Color in a chemical substance arises when the molecule **selectively absorbs** certain wavelengths of visible light and transmits/reflects the remaining wavelengths. This selective absorption occurs due to:

- Electronic transitions (π→π*, n→π*, d–d transitions, charge transfer)
- Presence of **chromophores** (color-bearing groups) in the molecular structure
- Extended conjugation, which lowers the energy gap (HOMO–LUMO gap) so that absorption falls in the visible region (400–700 nm) rather than the UV region

### 1.3 Interaction of Light with Matter

When light strikes matter, one or more of the following occurs:

| Process | Description |
|---|---|
| **Absorption** | Light energy is taken up by the molecule, promoting electrons to higher energy states |
| **Transmission** | Light passes through the substance largely unabsorbed |
| **Reflection** | Light bounces off the surface without entering the material |
| **Scattering** | Light is redirected in multiple directions (important in pigments) |

The color observed corresponds to the **wavelengths that are *not* absorbed** — i.e., the transmitted/reflected light.

### 1.4 Visible Spectrum and Wavelength

The visible spectrum spans roughly **380 nm (violet) to 750 nm (red)**.

| Wavelength (nm) | Color Absorbed | Complementary Color Observed |
|---|---|---|
| 400–430 | Violet | Yellow-green |
| 430–490 | Blue | Orange |
| 490–510 | Blue-green | Red |
| 510–530 | Green | Purple/Magenta |
| 530–560 | Yellow-green | Violet |
| 560–590 | Yellow | Blue |
| 590–610 | Orange | Blue-green |
| 610–750 | Red | Green |

### 1.5 Wavelength, Frequency, and Energy Relationship

$$c = \nu \lambda \qquad E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

Where:
- $c$ = speed of light ($3 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$)
- $\nu$ = frequency
- $\lambda$ = wavelength
- $h$ = Planck's constant ($6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$)
- $E$ = energy of photon

> **Exam Point:** Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → higher energy. This inverse relation between $\lambda$ and $E$ explains why extended conjugation (which lowers transition energy) shifts absorption to *longer* wavelengths (**bathochromic/red shift**).

### 1.6 Why Substances Appear Colored

A substance appears colored when its **energy gap between HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) corresponds to a photon energy in the visible range**. If the gap is too large (as in simple saturated organic molecules), absorption occurs in the UV region and the compound appears colorless/white to the human eye.

### 1.7 Complementary Colors

**Complementary colors** are pairs of colors that, when combined, produce white light (in additive mixing) or cancel each other on the color wheel. The color we *see* is the complement of the color *absorbed*.

**Exam Point:** If a compound absorbs at ~600 nm (orange region), the compound will appear **blue** (its complementary color), not orange.

### 1.8 Chromophore and Auxochrome (Introductory Concept)

- **Chromophore**: an unsaturated functional group responsible for absorption of light and hence color (e.g., $-N=N-$, $-C=C-$, $-C=O$, $-NO_2$, quinonoid ring systems).
- **Auxochrome**: a saturated group with lone pair(s) of electrons that, when attached to a chromophore, **intensifies or shifts** the color by extending conjugation through resonance (e.g., $-NH_2$, $-OH$, $-OCH_3$, $-COOH$, $-SO_3H$).

*(Detailed treatment in Section 3.)*

## 2. Theories of Color

### 2.1 Electronic Theory of Color

**Principle:** Color originates from **electronic transitions** of loosely held (π or non-bonding) electrons within a molecule when they absorb visible light and jump from a lower energy molecular orbital to a higher one.

**Main assumptions:**
- Only molecules with **delocalized/mobile electrons** (conjugated π-systems, lone pairs conjugated with π-systems) can absorb visible light.
- Saturated compounds with only σ-electrons require very high energy (UV/far-UV) for excitation and hence are colorless.

**Chemical explanation:** The types of electronic transitions possible are:

| Transition | Approx. Energy | Typical Region |
|---|---|---|
| σ → σ* | Very high | Far UV (colorless) |
| n → σ* | High | UV |
| π → π* | Moderate | UV to visible (with conjugation) |
| n → π* | Lower | UV to visible |
| Charge transfer (CT) | Variable, often low | Visible (intense color) |
| d–d transitions (transition metals) | Low | Visible |

**Structural example:** In azo dyes, the $-N=N-$ group linked to aromatic rings creates an extended conjugated π-system; the π→π* transition energy falls within the visible range, producing color.

**Limitation:** The theory explains *why* color arises (electronic transition) but does not by itself predict *which* structural modifications will shift color — this is addressed by the chromophore–auxochrome and resonance theories.

---

### 2.2 Chromophore–Auxochrome Theory (Witt's Theory)

**Principle (Witt, 1876):** Color in organic compounds is due to the presence of specific unsaturated groups called **chromophores**. A compound containing a chromophore is called a **chromogen**, but a chromogen alone may only be faintly colored or colorless to the eye (though it absorbs UV/visible light). The color becomes deep and useful as a dye only when an **auxochrome** is also present.

**Main assumptions:**
1. Chromophores are responsible for light absorption (origin of color).
2. Auxochromes alone cannot produce color but **intensify and deepen** existing color when attached to a chromophore, and additionally impart **dye–fiber affinity** (solubilizing/salt-forming ability).

**Common chromophores:**

| Chromophore | Structure |
|---|---|
| Azo | $-N=N-$ |
| Nitro | $-NO_2$ |
| Nitroso | $-N=O$ |
| Carbonyl | $>C=O$ |
| Ethylenic | $>C=C<$ |
| Thiocarbonyl | $>C=S$ |
| Azomethine | $>C=N-$ |

**Common auxochromes:**

| Auxochrome | Nature |
|---|---|
| $-NH_2$ (amino) | Basic, electron-donating |
| $-NHR, -NR_2$ | Basic, electron-donating |
| $-OH$ (hydroxyl) | Acidic/electron-donating |
| $-OCH_3$ | Electron-donating |
| $-COOH$ | Acidic, solubilizing |
| $-SO_3H$ | Acidic, solubilizing |

**Chemical explanation:** Auxochromes possess lone pairs of electrons that conjugate (resonate) with the chromophore's π-system, extending delocalization and lowering the HOMO–LUMO gap — this **deepens** the color (bathochromic shift) and **intensifies** it (hyperchromic effect).

**Limitation:** This classical theory is largely descriptive/empirical. It does not quantitatively explain the *degree* of shift, and some auxochrome-free compounds (e.g., certain quinones, highly conjugated polyenes) are intensely colored, which the simple theory cannot fully account for. Modern **resonance and MO theories** provide the mechanistic basis.

---

### 2.3 Quinonoid Theory

**Principle:** Proposed to explain the color of certain dyes (especially triphenylmethane and azo dyes) — color is associated with the presence of a **quinonoid ring structure** (a six-membered ring with two exocyclic double bonds, as in *p*-quinone), as opposed to the colorless **benzenoid** (aromatic, Kekulé) structure.

**Main assumptions:**
- Benzenoid forms (fully aromatic, alternating ring double bonds) of many dye precursors are colorless or pale.
- Conversion to the **quinonoid form**, via tautomerism/ionization (e.g., under acidic or basic conditions), creates a cross-conjugated system that absorbs strongly in the visible region, producing intense color.

**Structural example:** In **phenolphthalein**, the colorless lactone (benzenoid) form converts to a colored **quinonoid, resonance-stabilized dianion** in basic medium — this is the classical example of quinonoid-based color change (acid–base indicator).

Similarly, many **triphenylmethane dyes** (e.g., malachite green, crystal violet) exist in a quinonoid resonance form that is responsible for their intense color.

**Limitation:** The quinonoid theory applies well to triphenylmethane- and indicator-type dyes but does **not universally explain** color in all dye classes (e.g., simple conjugated polyenes or metal-complex dyes do not require quinonoid structures). It should be regarded as a **special case explanation**, not a general theory of color.

---

### 2.4 Resonance Theory

**Principle:** Color intensity and position (wavelength of maximum absorption, $\lambda_{max}$) depend on the extent of **resonance delocalization** possible in the excited state relative to the ground state. Greater resonance stabilization of the excited state (relative to ground state) **lowers the energy gap**, producing a bathochromic (red) shift and often a hyperchromic (intensity-increasing) effect.

**Main assumptions:**
- A chromophore conjugated with electron-donating auxochromes (at appropriate positions, e.g., *para* or *ortho* to the chromophore) creates additional resonance structures involving charge separation (a "push–pull" system).
- The more contributing resonance structures (especially those placing positive/negative charge in a stabilized, delocalized manner), the greater the bathochromic shift.

**Chemical explanation (push–pull conjugation):**

For a donor–acceptor conjugated system:

$$\text{D}-\pi-\text{A} \longleftrightarrow \text{D}^{+}=\pi-\text{A}^{-}$$

where D = electron-donating auxochrome (e.g., $-NH_2$, $-OH$) and A = electron-withdrawing chromophore/acceptor (e.g., $-NO_2$, azo group). This intramolecular charge-transfer resonance is responsible for intensely colored azo and nitro dyes.

**Structural example:** *p*-Aminoazobenzene shows deeper color than azobenzene itself because the $-NH_2$ group (donor) conjugates through the azo chromophore (acceptor), enabling a charge-transfer resonance structure.

**Exam Point:** Resonance theory is considered the most complete **classical (pre-quantum-mechanical)** explanation, bridging the chromophore–auxochrome theory and quinonoid theory using the concept of contributing resonance structures.

---

### 2.5 Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory Explanation

**Principle:** Modern MO theory treats color as arising from the promotion of an electron from the **HOMO to the LUMO** (or higher unoccupied MOs) upon absorption of a photon whose energy matches $\Delta E = E_{LUMO} - E_{HOMO}$.

**Key points:**
- Extending conjugation (more alternating double bonds) increases the number of overlapping p-orbitals, **narrowing the HOMO–LUMO gap** (particle-in-a-box-like behavior), shifting $\lambda_{max}$ to longer wavelengths.
- Electron-donating auxochromes raise the HOMO energy; electron-withdrawing chromophores/groups lower the LUMO energy. Both effects **narrow the gap**, explaining bathochromic and hyperchromic effects on a quantum-mechanical footing.
- **Charge-transfer (CT) transitions** — common in azo and metal-complex dyes — involve significant electron density shift from donor to acceptor regions of the molecule upon excitation, and are typically associated with **high molar absorptivity** (intense color).

> **Exam Point:** MO theory unifies the classical chromophore–auxochrome, quinonoid, and resonance theories into a single quantum-mechanical framework: all structural factors that narrow the HOMO–LUMO gap deepen and intensify color.

## 3. Dyes

### 3.1 Definition of a Dye

A **dye** is a colored, chemically unsaturated organic compound that, when applied to a substrate (textile fibers, leather, paper, etc.), imparts color by **selective absorption** of light and possesses (or is chemically capable of forming) a reasonably firm attachment ("substantivity") to the substrate, generally with acceptable fastness properties.

### 3.2 Essential Characteristics of Dyes

A substance must satisfy the following to function as a good dye:

1. It must possess **color** (chromophore present).
2. It must have the ability to be **fixed onto the fiber** (solubility, affinity, or reactivity toward the substrate) — presence of auxochromes helps here.
3. It must show **fastness** — resistance to fading on exposure to light, washing, rubbing, and chemicals.
4. It should be **soluble** (or capable of being solubilized during application, as with vat dyes) in the dyeing medium.
5. It should have **uniform and level dyeing** properties (even color distribution).

### 3.3 Difference between Dyes and Colored Compounds

> **Exam Point (commonly confused):** *All dyes are colored compounds, but not all colored compounds are dyes.*

| Aspect | Dye | Merely Colored Compound |
|---|---|---|
| Color | Present | Present |
| Fiber affinity/fixation | Present (via auxochromes, ionic/covalent bonding, or physical entrapment) | Absent or negligible |
| Fastness on substrate | Adequate | Not applicable/poor |
| Example | Congo red (dyes cotton via direct affinity) | Azobenzene (colored but has no auxochrome/fiber affinity, used mainly as a chemical intermediate) |

### 3.4 Chromophores (in Dye Context)

See Section 2.2 for the general list. In the dye industry, the most industrially important chromophores are:

- **Azo group** ($-N=N-$) — by far the largest class of synthetic dyes (~60–70% of commercial dyes)
- **Anthraquinone/carbonyl system** — vat and disperse dyes
- **Indigoid system** — vat dyes (indigo)
- **Triphenylmethane system** — basic dyes
- **Phthalocyanine system** — pigments and some dyes

### 3.5 Auxochromes (in Dye Context)

Auxochromes serve a **dual role** in dyes:

1. **Color-modifying role**: deepen/intensify color via resonance (Section 2.4).
2. **Fiber-binding role**: provide the chemical handle for attachment to fiber.
   - **Acidic auxochromes** ($-SO_3H$, $-COOH$) → form salts with basic groups in protein fibers (wool, silk) → used in **acid dyes**.
   - **Basic auxochromes** ($-NH_2$, $-NR_2$) → form salts with acidic groups (e.g., in acrylic fibers) → used in **basic/cationic dyes**.

### 3.6 Bathochromic and Hypsochromic Effects

| Effect | Definition | Direction of Shift |
|---|---|---|
| **Bathochromic shift** ("red shift") | Shift of absorption $\lambda_{max}$ toward **longer** wavelength | Deepens color (e.g., yellow → red → blue) |
| **Hypsochromic shift** ("blue shift") | Shift of absorption $\lambda_{max}$ toward **shorter** wavelength | Lightens/shifts color toward blue/violet end |

**Causes of bathochromic shift:**
- Extending conjugation (more chromophoric units in series)
- Adding electron-donating auxochromes (e.g., $-NH_2$, $-OH$) at conjugating positions
- Planarization of the molecule (better orbital overlap)

**Causes of hypsochromic shift:**
- Removing conjugation
- Protonation of an auxochromic amino group (e.g., $-NH_2 \rightarrow -NH_3^+$), which removes the lone pair from conjugation
- Steric hindrance disrupting planarity/conjugation

### 3.7 Hyperchromic and Hypochromic Effects

| Effect | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Hyperchromic effect** | **Increase** in intensity (molar absorptivity, $\varepsilon$) of absorption — color becomes deeper/more intense |
| **Hypochromic effect** | **Decrease** in intensity of absorption — color becomes paler |

> **Exam Point (commonly confused):** Bathochromic/hypsochromic refer to **position (wavelength)** of $\lambda_{max}$; hyperchromic/hypochromic refer to **intensity (height, $\varepsilon_{max}$)** of the absorption band. A shift can be bathochromic *and* hyperchromic simultaneously (common with auxochrome addition), but they describe independent properties of the absorption spectrum.

### 3.8 Color and Constitution

The relationship between a dye's molecular structure ("constitution") and its observed color can be summarized:

- **More extended conjugation** → deeper color (bathochromic)
- **Stronger electron-donating auxochromes in conjugation with the chromophore** → deeper, more intense color
- **Planarity** of the molecule favors better conjugation and deeper color; non-planar/twisted structures show hypsochromic shifts
- **Position of substituents** (ortho/para vs meta to chromophore) matters — auxochromes must be positioned to allow resonance conjugation with the chromophore (para or ortho positions are effective; meta substitution largely breaks conjugation)

### 3.9 Dye–Fiber Interaction

Dyes attach to fibers through one or more of the following mechanisms:

| Mechanism | Bond type | Example dye class |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic (salt) linkage | Electrostatic | Acid dyes, basic dyes |
| Covalent bonding | Strong covalent (C–O or C–N linkage to fiber) | Reactive dyes |
| Hydrogen bonding + van der Waals (physical adsorption) | Weak, cumulative | Direct dyes |
| Mechanical entrapment (in situ formation, insoluble particle within fiber) | Physical | Vat dyes, sulfur dyes |
| Metal-complex (coordinate) bonding | Coordinate covalent, via mordant | Mordant dyes |
| Hydrophobic/physical dispersion within fiber matrix | Physical (solid solution) | Disperse dyes |

## 4. Classification of Dyes

> **Note:** Dyes can be classified by **chemical constitution** (chromophore-based, e.g., azo, anthraquinone) **or** by **method of application** (e.g., acid, direct, reactive, vat). These are two different, overlapping classification systems — a single chemical class (e.g., azo) may include dyes applied as acid, direct, disperse, or reactive dyes. Both systems are presented below as commonly required in Chemistry II syllabi.

### 4.1 Acid Dyes

- **Definition:** Anionic, water-soluble dyes containing acidic auxochromic groups (commonly $-SO_3Na$ or $-COONa$), applied from an acidic dye bath.
- **General structure:** Aromatic chromophore (often azo or anthraquinone) bearing one or more sulfonate groups.
- **Properties:** Water-soluble as sodium salts; bright shades; moderate-to-good light fastness depending on structure.
- **Method of application:** Applied to **protein fibers** (wool, silk) and **polyamide (nylon)** fibers from an acidic bath; the dye anion forms an **ionic bond** with protonated amino groups ($-NH_3^+$) of the fiber.
- **Example:** Orange II, Acid Orange 7, Methyl Orange (as a dye/indicator example).

### 4.2 Basic (Cationic) Dyes

- **Definition:** Dyes possessing a **cationic (positively charged) chromophore**, generally salts of colored organic bases.
- **General structure:** Triphenylmethane, azo, or xanthene chromophores bearing $-NR_3^+$ or protonated amino groups.
- **Properties:** Very bright, intense shades but generally **poor light fastness**; poor affinity for cellulose (cotton).
- **Method of application:** Applied to **acrylic fibers** (which contain anionic dye sites), silk, and tannin-mordanted cotton; the dye cation forms an **ionic bond** with anionic sites in the fiber.
- **Example:** Malachite green, Crystal violet, Methylene blue.

### 4.3 Direct Dyes

- **Definition:** Water-soluble anionic dyes (usually sulfonated azo compounds) that can be applied **directly to cellulose fibers** (cotton) without a mordant, from a neutral or slightly alkaline bath containing an electrolyte.
- **General structure:** Long, linear, highly conjugated **polyazo** structures (often bis- or tris-azo), which allows strong physical affinity for the linear cellulose chain.
- **Properties:** Good affinity for cotton via hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces along the polymer chain; generally **moderate wash fastness** (can be improved by after-treatment/diazotization).
- **Method of application:** Direct application ("substantive dyeing") — dye bath with common salt (NaCl) to aid exhaustion onto fiber.
- **Example:** Congo red, Direct Red 28.

### 4.4 Reactive Dyes

- **Definition:** Dyes that contain a **reactive functional group** capable of forming a **covalent bond** with hydroxyl groups of cellulose (or amino groups of protein fibers).
- **General structure:** Chromophore (often azo or anthraquinone) — bridge — reactive group (e.g., **triazine** or **vinyl sulfone** systems).
- **Properties:** Excellent wash fastness (due to covalent attachment); bright shades; wide color range.
- **Method of application:** Applied under alkaline conditions; the reactive group undergoes **nucleophilic substitution** (with fiber –OH) forming a **dye–fiber ether/covalent linkage**.

**Representative reaction (generalized, dichlorotriazine-type reactive dye with cellulose):**

$$\text{Dye–Triazine–Cl} + \text{HO–Cellulose} \xrightarrow{\text{alkali}} \text{Dye–Triazine–O–Cellulose} + \text{HCl}$$

- **Example:** Procion dyes (Procion Red MX), Remazol dyes.

### 4.5 Mordant Dyes

- **Definition:** Dyes that require a **mordant** (usually a metal salt, e.g., Al³⁺, Cr³⁺, Fe³⁺) to form an insoluble **metal-complex (chelate/lake)** with the fiber for fixation.
- **General structure:** Contains chelating groups (e.g., ortho-hydroxy azo or ortho-dihydroxy groups) capable of forming coordinate bonds with the metal ion.
- **Properties:** Excellent wash and light fastness due to strong coordinate covalent metal-complex formation; shade often depends on the metal used (same dye with different mordants gives different colors).
- **Method of application:** Fiber is pre-treated (or simultaneously treated) with the mordant; the dye then forms a **metal chelate ("lake")** bridging dye and fiber.
- **Example:** Alizarin (with Al³⁺ mordant → red "Turkey red" lake; with Fe³⁺ → violet/black shades).

### 4.6 Vat Dyes

- **Definition:** Water-insoluble dyes, typically containing a **keto (carbonyl) group**, that are converted to a water-soluble **leuco (reduced) form** using a reducing agent in alkaline medium ("vatting"), applied to the fiber, and then **re-oxidized** (usually by air) back to the insoluble colored pigment form trapped within the fiber.
- **General structure:** Anthraquinonoid or indigoid (containing $>C=O$ groups).
- **Chemistry of vatting:**

$$\text{Dye}(>C=O) \xrightarrow[\text{NaOH}]{\text{Na}_2S_2O_4 \text{ (sodium hydrosulfite)}} \text{Leuco-Dye}(>C\text{–ONa}) \ (\text{soluble}) \xrightarrow{\text{air/O}_2 \text{ oxidation}} \text{Dye}(>C=O) \ (\text{insoluble, fixed in fiber})$$

- **Properties:** Outstanding wash and light fastness (dye is mechanically trapped as insoluble particles within fiber pores after reoxidation); relatively expensive and complex application process.
- **Example:** **Indigo** (denim dyeing), Vat Blue 4 (Indanthrene Blue).

### 4.7 Disperse Dyes

- **Definition:** Very slightly water-soluble, non-ionic dyes applied as a **fine aqueous dispersion** (not true solution); designed for **hydrophobic synthetic fibers**.
- **General structure:** Small, simple azo or anthraquinone molecules with no ionic solubilizing groups.
- **Properties:** The dye dissolves into the hydrophobic fiber (acts like a "solid solution")); good fastness on synthetic fibers.
- **Method of application:** Applied to **polyester, acetate, and nylon** fibers, often at high temperature/pressure (or using a carrier) to allow the dye to diffuse into the hydrophobic fiber matrix.
- **Example:** Disperse Blue 3, Disperse Red 1.

### 4.8 Sulfur Dyes

- **Definition:** Complex, high-molecular-weight organic dyes containing **sulfur linkages** (–S–S–, thiazole rings), insoluble in water, applied similarly to vat dyes via reduction and reoxidation.
- **General structure:** Not precisely defined (complex polymeric structures formed by fusing amines/phenols with sulfur or sodium polysulfide).
- **Properties:** Cheap, give dull but very fast (especially wash-fast) shades, mainly dark colors (black, navy, brown, khaki); generally poor for bright shades.
- **Method of application:** Reduced with **sodium sulfide (Na₂S)** to a soluble leuco form, applied to cotton, then **reoxidized** (usually with dichromate, peroxide, or air) to the insoluble form within the fiber.
- **Example:** Sulfur Black 1 (widely used for black cotton dyeing).

### 4.9 Azo Dyes

- **Definition:** The largest class of synthetic dyes, characterized by one or more **azo groups ($-N=N-$)** linking aromatic ring systems.
- **Classification within azo dyes:** monoazo, disazo (bisazo), trisazo, polyazo — based on number of $-N=N-$ groups.
- **Properties:** Extremely versatile — can be formulated as acid, direct, disperse, mordant, or reactive dyes depending on auxochromes attached; wide color range (yellow through blue-black).
- **Method of application:** Depends on the specific auxochromes present (see Section 5 for synthesis).
- **Example:** Methyl orange, Congo red, Orange II.

> **Exam Point:** Azo dyes are classified **by chromophore (chemical constitution)**, but individual azo dyes are further sub-classified **by method of application** (e.g., Congo red = azo + direct dye; Orange II = azo + acid dye). This illustrates the overlap between the two classification systems.

### 4.10 Solvent Dyes

- **Definition:** Non-ionic dyes that are **soluble in organic solvents, oils, waxes, and plastics** rather than water (no solubilizing sulfonate/carboxylate groups).
- **Properties:** Used for coloring non-polar/hydrophobic media where water-solubility is undesirable.
- **Method of application:** Dissolved directly into the non-aqueous medium (e.g., petroleum products, plastics, waxes, solvent-based inks).
- **Example:** Solvent Yellow 124, Solvent Red 24 (used for fuel coloring/dyeing plastics).

### 4.11 Natural Dyes

- **Definition:** Dyes obtained from natural sources — plants, animals, or minerals.
- **Sources & examples:**
  - Plant-based: **Indigo** (from *Indigofera*), **Alizarin** (from madder root), Turmeric (curcumin), Henna
  - Animal-based: **Cochineal/Carminic acid** (from insects), Tyrian purple (from mollusks)
- **Properties:** Generally require mordanting (many are chemically mordant dyes); often less lightfast than synthetic counterparts; environmentally biodegradable.

### 4.12 Synthetic Dyes

- **Definition:** Dyes manufactured chemically, beginning historically with **William Perkin's discovery of Mauveine (1856)**, the first synthetic dye, derived from aniline.
- **Properties:** Wide color range, reproducible shades, generally more cost-effective and consistent than natural dyes; can be engineered for specific fastness and application properties.
- **Examples:** All azo, anthraquinone, triphenylmethane, phthalocyanine-based commercial dyes.

## 5. Important Dye Chemistry

### 5.1 Azo Dyes and the Azo Group

The **azo group**, $-N=N-$, is a chromophore linking two (usually aromatic) groups, most commonly formed by **diazotization followed by coupling**.

### 5.2 Diazotization

**Definition:** The reaction of a primary **aromatic amine** with **nitrous acid** (generated in situ from sodium nitrite + mineral acid) at **low temperature (0–5°C)** to form a **diazonium salt**.

$$\text{Ar–NH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0\text{–}5^\circ C} \text{Ar–N}_2^{+}\text{Cl}^{-} + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

> **Exam Point:** Diazotization must be carried out at **low temperature (0–5°C)** because diazonium salts are thermally unstable and decompose above this range (releasing N₂ and forming phenols or other side products).

### 5.3 Coupling Reaction

**Definition:** The diazonium salt (electrophile) reacts with an activated aromatic compound (**coupling component** — a phenol or aromatic amine, both strongly activated, electron-rich rings) via **electrophilic aromatic substitution**, typically at the position *para* to the activating group (or *ortho* if *para* is blocked), forming the azo dye.

$$\text{Ar–N}_2^{+}\text{Cl}^{-} + \text{Ar'–OH} \ (\text{or } \text{Ar'–NH}_2) \longrightarrow \text{Ar–N=N–Ar'} + \text{HCl}$$

This is called an **azo coupling reaction**, and the product is an **azo dye**.

### 5.4 Methyl Orange — Worked Example

**Synthesis:** Diazotization of **sulfanilic acid** followed by coupling with **N,N-dimethylaniline**.

**Step 1 — Diazotization of sulfanilic acid:**

$$p\text{-H}_2\text{N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–SO}_3\text{H} + \text{NaNO}_2 + \text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0\text{–}5^\circ C} p\text{-}(\text{Cl}^-\text{N}_2^+)\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–SO}_3\text{H}$$

**Step 2 — Coupling with N,N-dimethylaniline:**

$$p\text{-}(\text{N}_2^+)\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–SO}_3\text{H} + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}(\text{CH}_3)_2 \longrightarrow (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–N=N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–SO}_3\text{H}$$

**Structural features:** Chromophore = $-N=N-$; auxochromes = $-N(CH_3)_2$ (donor, basic) and $-SO_3H$ (acidic, solubilizing). This **push–pull** arrangement (donor and chromophore in conjugation) is responsible for the color, and methyl orange also functions as an **acid–base indicator** because protonation of the dimethylamino nitrogen under acidic conditions disrupts conjugation, causing a color change (yellow in base → red in acid), which is a classic example of the **hypsochromic effect due to loss of auxochrome conjugation upon protonation**.

### 5.5 Congo Red — Worked Example

**Type:** A **disazo (bis-azo) direct dye**, synthesized by **double diazotization–coupling** using **benzidine** as the central diamine, coupled with two equivalents of **naphthionic acid (1-amino-naphthalene-4-sulfonic acid)**.

**General scheme (simplified):**

$$\text{Benzidine} \xrightarrow{\text{double diazotization}} \text{Bis-diazonium salt} \xrightarrow{\text{coupling with naphthionic acid (2 eq)}} \text{Congo Red}$$

**Structural features:** Contains **two azo chromophores** in an extended, linear, conjugated system with terminal $-SO_3Na$ auxochromes — the long, planar, linear molecular shape gives strong affinity for the linear cellulose chains of cotton (hence classified as a **direct dye**). Congo red is also a classic **pH indicator** (blue in strong acid, red in neutral/base) due to protonation-induced conformational/electronic changes.

### 5.6 Indigo — Example of a Vat Dye

**Structure:** Indigo is a **bis-indole (indigoid)** compound with a central conjugated system containing two carbonyl groups and an intramolecular hydrogen-bonded, highly planar, conjugated chromophore — it is **not azo-based**.

**Vatting chemistry (reduction–oxidation cycle):**

$$\text{Indigo (insoluble, blue)} \xrightarrow[\text{NaOH}]{\text{Na}_2S_2O_4} \text{Leuco-indigo (soluble, yellow-white)} \xrightarrow{\text{air oxidation (on fiber)}} \text{Indigo (insoluble, blue, fixed in fiber)}$$

This reduction–application–reoxidation cycle is the defining chemistry of **all vat dyes** (Section 4.6) and is why vat dyes (like indigo used in denim) have excellent wash fastness despite being water-insoluble.

### 5.7 Reactive Dyes and Covalent Bonding with Fibers

(See Section 4.4.) The key chemistry is **nucleophilic substitution or addition** between a reactive group on the dye (commonly a **chlorotriazine** ring, which undergoes nucleophilic aromatic substitution, or a **vinyl sulfone** group, which undergoes Michael-type nucleophilic addition) and a nucleophilic **–OH group of cellulose**, under alkaline conditions, forming a permanent **covalent ether linkage** between dye and fiber.

### 5.8 Mordant Dyes and Metal-Complex Formation

(See Section 4.5.) Mordant dyes contain **ortho-hydroxy** or **ortho-dihydroxy** groups adjacent to the chromophore, which act as **bidentate chelating ligands** toward mordant metal ions (Cr³⁺, Al³⁺, Fe³⁺), forming a stable five- or six-membered **chelate ring (metal complex/"lake")**. This metal-dye complex is simultaneously bonded (coordinately) to functional groups on the fiber, anchoring the dye firmly and often shifting the color depending on which metal ion is used (**a form of bathochromic/hypsochromic shift induced by coordination**, related to ligand field/charge-transfer effects of the metal).

## 6. Pigments

### 6.1 Definition of Pigment

A **pigment** is a colored substance that is **insoluble** in the application medium (water, oil, binder, or polymer matrix) and imparts color by remaining as **finely divided, dispersed solid particles** on or within the substrate, rather than by dissolving and chemically bonding as dyes do.

### 6.2 Characteristics of Pigments

1. **Insolubility** in the medium of application (the defining property).
2. Color arises from **selective absorption and scattering** of light by dispersed particles.
3. Applied using a **binder/vehicle** (resin, oil, polymer) that physically holds pigment particles onto/within the substrate.
4. Generally excellent **lightfastness and chemical stability** (especially inorganic pigments).
5. **Particle size** critically affects color strength, opacity (hiding power), and gloss.

### 6.3 Organic Pigments

Colored organic compounds rendered insoluble (either inherently insoluble, or made insoluble by conversion, e.g., "lake" formation from a soluble dye precipitated with a metal salt onto an inert substrate).

- **Examples:** Phthalocyanine Blue/Green, Quinacridone Red, Azo pigment yellows/reds (structurally similar to azo dyes but formulated/processed to be insoluble).

### 6.4 Inorganic Pigments

Metal oxides, sulfides, and other inorganic compounds; generally more opaque, heat-stable, and lightfast than organic pigments, though often duller in shade.

- **Examples:** Titanium dioxide, iron oxides, ultramarine, chrome yellow, carbon black.

### 6.5 How Pigments Produce Color

Unlike dyes (molecular-level absorption in solution/fiber), pigment color depends on:

1. **Selective light absorption** by the pigment's electronic structure (same underlying physics as dyes — chromophoric groups or, for inorganic pigments, d–d transitions/charge-transfer transitions in the crystal lattice).
2. **Light scattering** at particle surfaces and internal crystal boundaries, which strongly depends on **particle size** relative to the wavelength of visible light.

### 6.6 Particle Size and Optical Properties

> **Exam Point:** Particle size is one of the most important variables controlling a pigment's visual performance.

- **Optimal scattering** occurs when particle size is comparable to (roughly half) the wavelength of visible light (~200–300 nm for maximum light-scattering efficiency, as in TiO₂ used for opacity/hiding power).
- **Smaller particles** → higher surface area → greater tinting strength but potentially lower opacity and different (often duller or more transparent) undertones.
- **Larger particles** → reduced color strength, gritty texture, poor gloss.

### 6.7 Solubility/Insolubility

This is the **fundamental chemical distinction** separating pigments from dyes (elaborated in Section 7).

### 6.8 Applications

Pigments are used in **paints, coatings, plastics, printing inks, cosmetics, ceramics, and pigment-based ("lake") textile printing** — wherever a colorant must remain as discrete particles bound by a separate vehicle/binder rather than dissolving into the substrate.

### 6.9 Important Pigment Examples

| Pigment | Chemical Nature | Color | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Titanium dioxide (TiO₂)** | Inorganic oxide | White | Highest refractive index among white pigments; excellent opacity/hiding power; most widely used white pigment |
| **Iron oxides** (Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄, FeO(OH)) | Inorganic oxide | Red, yellow, brown, black | Natural and synthetic; excellent lightfastness, low cost, non-toxic |
| **Ultramarine** | Complex sodium aluminosilicate with polysulfide chromophore | Blue | Historically from lapis lazuli; now made synthetically |
| **Chrome yellow** | Lead chromate (PbCrO₄) | Yellow | Bright yellow; toxicity concerns (lead and hexavalent chromium) restrict modern use |
| **Carbon black** | Elemental carbon (amorphous) | Black | Produced by incomplete combustion; extremely high tinting strength and UV stability |
| **Phthalocyanine pigments** | Organic — copper phthalocyanine macrocycle | Blue/Green | Exceptional brightness and lightfastness among organic pigments; intense color from extended macrocyclic conjugation and metal (Cu) coordination |

## 7. Dyes vs Pigments

> **Exam Point — this is one of the most frequently asked comparison questions.**

| Property | Dye | Pigment |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Colored compound that dissolves and chemically bonds/associates with the substrate | Colored compound that remains insoluble, dispersed as particles in a medium |
| **Solubility** | Soluble in the application medium (water or organic solvent) | Insoluble in the application medium |
| **Application method** | Applied from solution; dye penetrates and is fixed within/onto fiber structure | Applied as a dispersion in a binder/vehicle (paint, ink, resin) |
| **Affinity for fiber/substrate** | Chemical affinity via auxochromes (ionic, H-bonding, or covalent) | No inherent chemical affinity — held mechanically by the binder |
| **Chemical bonding** | Ionic, covalent, coordinate, or H-bonding/van der Waals with substrate | No direct bonding to substrate; bound by physical entrapment in binder film |
| **Particle size** | Molecular-level (true solution) | Micrometer-to-submicron discrete particles |
| **Lightfastness** | Variable, often lower (especially for basic/direct dyes); depends heavily on structure | Generally high, especially inorganic pigments |
| **Typical applications** | Textiles, leather, paper, biological staining | Paints, coatings, plastics, printing inks, cosmetics |
| **Examples** | Congo red, Methyl orange, Indigo (before vatting) | Titanium dioxide, Iron oxides, Phthalocyanine blue |

## 8. Important Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Chromophore** | An unsaturated functional group responsible for light absorption and hence the origin of color in a molecule (e.g., $-N=N-$, $>C=O$) |
| **Auxochrome** | A saturated, electron-donating (or acidic) group that, attached to a chromophore, intensifies/deepens color and confers fiber affinity (e.g., $-NH_2$, $-OH$, $-SO_3H$) |
| **Bathochromic shift** | Shift of $\lambda_{max}$ to a longer wavelength ("red shift"); deepens color |
| **Hypsochromic shift** | Shift of $\lambda_{max}$ to a shorter wavelength ("blue shift"); lightens/shifts color toward blue |
| **Hyperchromic effect** | Increase in the intensity (molar absorptivity) of light absorption |
| **Hypochromic effect** | Decrease in the intensity of light absorption |
| **Colorant** | A general/umbrella term encompassing both dyes and pigments — any substance used to impart color |
| **Dye** | A soluble colored compound with chemical affinity/fixation ability for a substrate |
| **Pigment** | An insoluble colored compound applied as dispersed particles in a binder |
| **Mordant** | A substance (typically a metal salt: Al³⁺, Cr³⁺, Fe³⁺) that forms an insoluble complex ("lake") with a dye, fixing it onto the fiber |
| **Lake pigment** | A pigment formed by precipitating a normally water-soluble dye (often as its metal salt/complex) onto an inert, insoluble substrate (e.g., alumina hydrate), converting a dye into pigment form |
| **Fastness** | The general resistance of a dye/pigment's color to fading or change under various conditions (light, washing, rubbing, chemicals) |
| **Lightfastness** | Specifically, resistance of color to fading upon prolonged exposure to light (UV/visible radiation) |
| **Wash fastness** | Resistance of a dyed material's color to fading or bleeding during washing/laundering |

## 9. Natural vs Synthetic Colorants

| Aspect | Natural Dyes | Synthetic Dyes | Natural Pigments | Synthetic Pigments |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Plants, animals, minerals | Petrochemical/coal-tar derived organic synthesis | Minerals, earths, plant/animal extracts | Manufactured inorganic/organic compounds |
| **Color range** | Limited, often muted | Very wide, precisely reproducible | Limited (earth tones common) | Very wide |
| **Stability/Fastness** | Generally lower (light/wash fastness); some exceptions (indigo, alizarin) | Generally higher, tunable by structure | Often high (mineral pigments) | Very high (engineered stability) |
| **Cost** | Higher (labor-intensive extraction, lower yield) | Lower (efficient industrial synthesis) | Variable | Generally lower, consistent |

| **Applications** | Traditional/artisanal textiles, cosmetics, food (limited) | Bulk of modern textile, paper, leather, ink dyeing | Traditional art, ceramics | Paints, plastics, coatings, inks (dominant modern use) |

## 10. Environmental and Industrial Aspects

### 10.1 Dye Wastewater

Textile dyeing generates large volumes of **colored effluent** containing unfixed dye, salts, and auxiliary chemicals. Colored wastewater reduces light penetration in receiving water bodies, harming aquatic photosynthetic organisms, and often contains high **chemical oxygen demand (COD)** and dissolved solids.

### 10.2 Toxicity of Certain Dyes

Some dye classes and their precursors/degradation products raise toxicity concerns:

- Certain **azo dyes**, upon **reductive cleavage** of the $-N=N-$ bond (e.g., under anaerobic conditions in wastewater treatment or in the gut), can release **aromatic amines**, some of which are classified as **carcinogenic** (e.g., benzidine-based dyes — benzidine itself is a known human carcinogen, which is why benzidine-based direct dyes like Congo red have been largely phased out or restricted in many countries).
- **Heavy-metal mordant dyes** (using Cr, in particular hexavalent chromium compounds) pose toxicity and environmental persistence concerns.

### 10.3 Azo Dye Degradation

$$\text{Ar–N=N–Ar'} \xrightarrow{\text{reductive cleavage (anaerobic bacteria, or reducing agents)}} \text{Ar–NH}_2 + \text{Ar'–NH}_2$$

This reductive cleavage of the azo bond is both a **degradation pathway** (relevant to wastewater treatment) and a **toxicological concern** (formation of aromatic amines).

### 10.4 Heavy-Metal-Containing Pigments

Traditional inorganic pigments such as **chrome yellow (PbCrO₄)**, **lead white**, and **cadmium-based pigments** contain toxic heavy metals (Pb, Cr(VI), Cd) that pose health and environmental hazards, leading to regulatory restrictions in many jurisdictions.

### 10.5 Environmental Concerns (Summary)

- Non-biodegradability of many synthetic azo dyes
- Bioaccumulation of heavy metals from mordants/pigments
- High water and salt consumption in dyeing processes
- Aquatic toxicity of dye-bath effluents

### 10.6 Modern Safer Alternatives

- **Reactive dyes** with high fixation efficiency (reduce unfixed dye discharge)
- **Metal-free** or reduced-heavy-metal pigment formulations (e.g., replacing chrome yellow with organic azo/bismuth vanadate yellows)
- **Enzymatic and advanced oxidation treatment** of dye wastewater
- Increased use of **natural dyes** and **low-impact dye** certifications in sustainable textile production

## Quick Revision Sheet

- **Color** arises from selective absorption of visible light (380–750 nm) due to electronic transitions; observed color = complement of absorbed color.
- $E = hc/\lambda$: shorter λ → higher energy.
- **Chromophore** = color-causing unsaturated group ($-N=N-$, $>C=O$, etc.); **Auxochrome** = color-intensifying, fiber-binding, electron-donating/acidic group ($-NH_2$, $-OH$, $-SO_3H$).
- **Theories of color:** Electronic theory (electronic transitions) → Chromophore–Auxochrome theory (Witt) → Quinonoid theory (benzenoid ↔ quinonoid tautomers, esp. triphenylmethane dyes/indicators) → Resonance theory (push–pull donor–acceptor resonance) → MO theory (HOMO–LUMO gap, unifying framework).
- **Bathochromic/hypsochromic** = shift in **position** (wavelength); **hyperchromic/hypochromic** = change in **intensity**.
- **Dye** = soluble, chemically bonds to substrate; **Pigment** = insoluble, held by a binder as dispersed particles.
- **Dye classes by application:** Acid, Basic, Direct, Reactive, Mordant, Vat, Disperse, Sulfur — each defined by a distinct fixation mechanism.
- **Azo dye synthesis:** Diazotization (ArNH₂ + NaNO₂/HCl, 0–5°C) → Coupling (with phenol/amine) → Azo dye.
- **Key examples:** Methyl orange (acid/indicator azo dye), Congo red (disazo direct dye), Indigo (indigoid vat dye), Alizarin (mordant dye).
- **Key pigments:** TiO₂ (white, high opacity), Iron oxides (red/yellow/brown), Ultramarine (blue), Chrome yellow (yellow, toxic), Carbon black (black), Phthalocyanine (blue/green, organic).
- **Environmental concerns:** azo dye reductive cleavage → carcinogenic amines (e.g., benzidine); heavy-metal pigments/mordants (Pb, Cr(VI), Cd) toxicity.

## Key Reactions (Collected)

**1. Diazotization:**
$$\text{Ar–NH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0\text{–}5^\circ C} \text{Ar–N}_2^{+}\text{Cl}^{-} + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**2. Azo Coupling:**
$$\text{Ar–N}_2^{+}\text{Cl}^{-} + \text{Ar'–OH/NH}_2 \longrightarrow \text{Ar–N=N–Ar'} + \text{HCl}$$

**3. Vat Dye Reduction–Oxidation Cycle:**
$$\text{Dye}(>C=O) \xrightarrow[\text{NaOH}]{\text{Na}_2S_2O_4} \text{Leuco-Dye (soluble)} \xrightarrow{\text{O}_2 \text{ (air)}} \text{Dye}(>C=O) \text{ (insoluble, fixed)}$$

**4. Reactive Dye–Fiber Covalent Bonding (generalized, triazine-type):**
$$\text{Dye–Triazine–Cl} + \text{HO–Cellulose} \xrightarrow{\text{alkali}} \text{Dye–Triazine–O–Cellulose} + \text{HCl}$$

**5. Azo Bond Reductive Cleavage (degradation/toxicity pathway):**
$$\text{Ar–N=N–Ar'} \xrightarrow{\text{reduction}} \text{Ar–NH}_2 + \text{Ar'–NH}_2$$

## Key Differences (Comparison Tables Summary)

### Dye vs Pigment
*(See Section 7 for full table.)* Core distinction: **dye = soluble + chemical bonding**; **pigment = insoluble + physical binder entrapment**.

### Chromophore vs Auxochrome

| Chromophore | Auxochrome |
|---|---|
| Unsaturated group | Saturated group |
| Directly responsible for absorption/color origin | Modifies (intensifies/deepens) existing color |
| Cannot itself confer fiber affinity | Confers fiber affinity (via ionic/salt-forming ability) |
| Example: $-N=N-$, $>C=O$ | Example: $-NH_2$, $-OH$, $-SO_3H$ |

### Natural vs Synthetic Dyes
*(See Section 9.)* Natural: muted shades, lower fastness generally, biodegradable, costlier. Synthetic: wide reproducible shades, tunable fastness, cheaper, potential environmental toxicity concerns.

### Acid vs Basic Dyes

| Acid Dyes | Basic (Cationic) Dyes |
|---|---|
| Anionic dye ion | Cationic dye ion |
| Auxochrome: $-SO_3H/-COOH$ | Auxochrome: $-NR_3^+$/protonated amine |
| Applied from acidic bath | Applied to acrylic fiber/mordanted cotton |
| Bonds to protonated $-NH_3^+$ in protein fibers | Bonds to anionic sites in fiber |
| Example: Orange II | Example: Malachite green |

### Direct vs Reactive Dyes

| Direct Dyes | Reactive Dyes |
|---|---|
| Physical affinity (H-bonding, van der Waals) | Covalent bond with fiber |
| Applied directly, no mordant, with electrolyte | Applied under alkaline conditions |
| Moderate wash fastness | Excellent wash fastness |
| Long, linear polyazo structure | Chromophore + reactive group (triazine/vinyl sulfone) |
| Example: Congo red | Example: Procion Red MX |

### Vat vs Sulfur Dyes

| Vat Dyes | Sulfur Dyes |
|---|---|
| Contain carbonyl ($>C=O$) chromophore | Contain sulfur linkages (–S–S–, thiazole) |
| Reduced with sodium hydrosulfite (Na₂S₂O₄) | Reduced with sodium sulfide (Na₂S) |
| Bright to moderate shade range | Mainly dull, dark shades (black, navy) |
| Excellent fastness, costlier | Good wash fastness, cheaper, dull shades |
| Example: Indigo | Example: Sulfur Black 1 |
